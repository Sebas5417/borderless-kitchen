Wasabi (*Wasabia japonica*) is a semiaquatic plant native to Japan, cultivated along cold mountain stream banks in very specific conditions of shade, temperature, and water flow. The rhizome — the knobby, green root — is grated fresh on a sharkskin grater (*oroshi*) immediately before use, producing a pale green paste with a fierce, nasal heat that dissipates within 30–60 seconds. It is among the most expensive food ingredients in the world: a single rhizome takes two to three years to reach harvest size, and the plant is notoriously difficult to cultivate outside its native conditions.

## Real wasabi vs. fake wasabi

This is the most important thing to know about wasabi as a pantry ingredient: almost all of what is served as wasabi — in restaurants outside Japan, and in much of Japan itself — is not wasabi.

The green paste served with sushi globally is almost always a mixture of horseradish, Chinese mustard, and green food dye. It is sharper, less complex, and more pungent than real wasabi. The pale green powder sold in tins is the same formula in dehydrated form. Products labeled "wasabi powder" or "wasabi paste" should be assumed to be horseradish-based unless they specifically state *Wasabia japonica* content — and even then, many products contain only a small percentage of real wasabi padded with horseradish.

Real wasabi has a more delicate, rounder heat with a subtle sweetness underneath. It is also more expensive by an order of magnitude. If you are paying restaurant prices in Japan or at high-end sushi counters elsewhere, there is a chance you are getting real wasabi. Everywhere else, assume you are getting the imitation.

Both are useful. The distinction matters for understanding what you are tasting.

## The chemistry of wasabi heat

Wasabi's heat comes from allyl isothiocyanate — the same volatile compound responsible for the heat in horseradish and mustard. This compound evaporates rapidly at air temperature, which is why:

1. Real wasabi must be grated immediately before use and loses most of its heat within 15 minutes of grating. This is why real wasabi is grated tableside or to order at serious sushi restaurants.

2. The heat is nasal, not mouth heat. It stimulates receptors in the nasal passages rather than the tongue, producing a sharp, sinus-clearing sensation rather than the slow burn of capsaicin. There is no lasting heat — it is gone in 30–60 seconds.

3. Covering wasabi slows the evaporation. Wasabi placed between the rice and fish in sushi is partly protected from air, which is part of why it is applied that way.

## How to use it

**Sushi:** A small amount applied between the rice and the fish. The wasabi should enhance the fish flavor, not cover it. Too much wasabi in sushi is a calibration error — the standard small amount applied by a sushi chef is the correct amount.

**Soba dipping sauce:** Dissolve a small amount of wasabi paste into the tsuyu (dipping broth) for cold soba. The wasabi floats and swirls through the broth as you eat.

**With grilled beef:** A pea-sized amount of wasabi alongside a slice of grilled steak or beef tataki — the nasal heat cuts through the fat in a way that neither black pepper nor chili can replicate. Common in Japanese izakaya cooking.

**Don't overuse it.** The purpose of wasabi in Japanese cooking is to enhance, not to compete. The famous sushi-bar challenge of loading your soy sauce with wasabi and dipping freely is not how wasabi is intended to be used — it numbs the palate and defeats the purpose of the fish.

## Storage

Powdered wasabi: store sealed, cool, dry. Mix with cold water to form paste; let sit 5 minutes before using to allow the heat compounds to develop. Refrigerate any mixed paste and use within a few hours.

Tube wasabi paste: refrigerate after opening; use within a few weeks for best flavor.

Fresh wasabi rhizome (if you find it): refrigerate wrapped in a damp cloth; use within 2 weeks. Grate only what you need immediately before serving.
