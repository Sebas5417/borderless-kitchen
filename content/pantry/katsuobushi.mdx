Katsuobushi is skipjack tuna — filleted, simmered, smoked repeatedly over oak or cherry wood, then inoculated with a specific mold (*Aspergillus glaucus*) and fermented for months until the blocks are, quite literally, the hardest food in the world. A finished katsuobushi block will ring like wood when you tap it. It is then shaved on a specialized planer (a *katsuobushi kezuriki*, which looks like a wooden box with a blade set into the lid) into the translucent pink-brown flakes familiar from Japanese cooking.

You will almost certainly buy it already shaved, in sealed bags.

## The two main types

**Hanakatsuo** (花かつお) — fine, soft flakes used primarily to make dashi. The surface area is large relative to the volume, which maximizes the extraction of inosinate (the umami compound) into the liquid.

**Kezuribushi** (削り節) — thicker shavings, used as a garnish or topping. This is what you see piled on okonomiyaki and takoyaki, moving in the steam like something alive. The movement is real: thin enough shavings are displaced by convection currents rising from the hot food beneath.

## Why it moves

The "dancing" of katsuobushi on hot food is not a party trick. The flakes are thin enough that the rising heat from the food beneath creates small air currents strong enough to move them. It stops when the food cools. It is one of the more beautiful things a garnish can do.

## Role in Japanese cooking

Katsuobushi provides inosinate (IMP), one of the three main dietary sources of glutamate-type umami (the others being glutamate from kombu and guanylate from dried mushrooms). In dashi — the fundamental Japanese stock — katsuobushi and kombu are combined specifically because inosinate and glutamate have a synergistic effect: together, they produce significantly more perceived umami than either alone. This principle (umami synergy) was documented by food scientists but was understood by Japanese cooks long before it was named.

## How to use it

**For dashi:** bring water and kombu to near-boil, remove the kombu, add hanakatsuo, steep for 2–3 minutes off the heat, strain. This is awase dashi — the foundation of miso soup, noodle broths, sauces, and braising liquids across Japanese cooking.

**As a garnish:** pile thick shavings onto hot dishes — okonomiyaki, fried rice, scrambled eggs, roasted vegetables, steamed tofu — immediately before serving. The movement is visual; the flavor is savory and smoky.

**As a seasoning:** crumble dried katsuobushi into rice seasoning (furikake), mix into mayonnaise, or use as a dry seasoning on grilled fish or vegetables.

## Where to buy and how to store

Available at Japanese grocery stores and many Asian supermarkets. Sealed bags are shelf-stable for months. Once opened, store in an airtight container and use within 2–3 weeks for peak flavor, though it doesn't go bad quickly. Exposure to moisture is the enemy — wet flakes clump and lose their lightness.

## The cross-cultural equivalent

The functional equivalent in Western cooking is anchovy. Both are small oily fish, dried or cured, used primarily as an umami base in broths or as a garnish/seasoning. The difference is delivery: anchovies melt into oil and dissolve into sauces; katsuobushi extracts into water and is then strained out. If you understand why a good Italian cook puts anchovies in their soffritto, you understand why a Japanese cook puts katsuobushi in their dashi. The mechanism differs; the purpose is the same.
