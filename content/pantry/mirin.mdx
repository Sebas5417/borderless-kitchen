Mirin is sweet rice wine, and it is one of the most important liquids in the Japanese pantry. At its core it is sake fermented with added rice and koji, producing a liquid that sits around 14% alcohol and 40–50% sugar. But calling it "sweet sake" undersells what makes it irreplaceable: the sugars in mirin are complex, the fermentation adds amino acids and depth, and when mirin hits a hot pan it caramelizes in a way that plain sugar never can. The sheen you see on teriyaki, the gentle gloss on simmered chicken — that comes from mirin.

## Flavor profile

True mirin tastes like something between a dessert wine and a syrup, with a mild sweetness that is never sharp and a slight savory note underneath. It has none of the bitterness of alcohol and none of the one-dimensional sweetness of sugar. The flavor is round, low, and background — you notice it most when it is missing.

## How to use it

Mirin appears in almost every cooked Japanese sauce. It is essential in teriyaki (combined with soy sauce and sake in roughly equal parts, reduced until glossy), in *nimono* (simmered dishes like *nikujaga* and *buri daikon*), and in glazes for grilled fish and chicken. A small amount added to dashi before serving rounds out the savory edge and gives miso soup a quieter sweetness. Unlike sake, mirin is almost always cooked rather than added raw — the heat drives off the alcohol and concentrates the sugars.

A reliable ratio for a basic teriyaki glaze: 2 tablespoons soy sauce, 2 tablespoons mirin, 1 tablespoon sake, 1 teaspoon sugar. Reduce over medium heat until slightly thickened.

## Buying guide

Three products on the market call themselves mirin, and they are not equal:

**Hon mirin** (true mirin) is the real product — fermented for 40–60 days, contains genuine alcohol, and tastes like it should. Look for bottles where the only ingredients are rice, rice koji, and shochu (distilled spirits). Kikkoman, Takara, and Morita all produce hon mirin available outside Japan. It costs more than the alternatives.

**Shio mirin** has salt added (around 1.5%) so it can be sold without alcohol licensing. It functions identically in cooking but tastes saltier raw. Fine as a substitute.

**Mirin-fu chomiryo** (mirin-style seasoning) is corn syrup with colorings and flavoring. It does not behave like real mirin in the pan, does not create the same glaze, and should be avoided if you can find the real product.

## Storage

Hon mirin stores at room temperature before opening; refrigerate after opening and use within six months. Shio mirin refrigerates from the start. If you see crystallization or off smells, discard. A good bottle lasts a year properly stored.

## The borderless angle

Mirin travels well. Korean cooks use it as a sweeter, milder substitute for *cheongju* (Korean rice wine) in marinades and glazes. Western chefs have picked it up as a finishing glaze for roasted vegetables and fish, where its caramelizing properties mirror those of a honey-and-butter pan sauce but with more subtlety and no dairy. It has also found a home in craft cocktails, where its combination of sweetness and mild umami adds a savory dimension that simple syrup cannot.
