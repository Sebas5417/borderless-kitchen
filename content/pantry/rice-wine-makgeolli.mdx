Makgeolli is the oldest documented alcoholic drink in Korea, with records stretching back over two thousand years. Unlike sake, which is filtered to translucency, or wine, which ferments from simple fruit sugars, makgeolli is an unfiltered rice wine — opaque, milky white, slightly fizzy, with a flavor that sits somewhere between a mild yogurt drink, a light beer, and fermented grain. It is unabashedly rustic, and it has recently been rediscovered by a generation of Korean drinkers who had set it aside for imported beer and soju.

The production is straightforward: glutinous rice (and sometimes wheat) is cooked, mixed with *nuruk* (a traditional Korean fermentation starter containing both wild yeast and saccharifying enzymes), and left to ferment for one to two weeks at room temperature. Unlike Japanese sake, which uses the cleaner, more isolated koji mold, nuruk is a blended culture — the resulting flavor is earthier, more complex, and slightly sour from lactic acid bacteria that also develop during fermentation. Alcohol content is modest: 6–8%, about the strength of a strong beer.

## Flavor profile

Makgeolli tastes lightly sweet, mildly sour, and faintly earthy, with a gentle carbonation that comes from active fermentation (in fresh, unpasteurized versions) or from added carbonation (in commercial shelf-stable versions). There is a grain character underneath — not hoppy or malty like beer, but clearly fermented cereal. Some versions have a distinct dairy-like tartness from the lactic bacteria. The sweetness is never cloying; the sourness is always mild.

Fresh makgeolli changes flavor over the course of a week in the refrigerator, becoming progressively less sweet and more sour as fermentation continues.

## How to use it

**In cooking:** Makgeolli's primary cooking application is in Korean pancake batter. *Buchimgae* (green onion pancake, or *pajeon*), *kimchi buchimgae* (kimchi pancake), and other savory pancakes traditionally use makgeolli in place of water or plain wheat flour batter. The carbonation from the rice wine — especially in unpasteurized bottles — creates a lighter, crispier result than still water does, for the same reason that beer batter crisps more effectively than plain flour. Use makgeolli wherever a Korean pancake recipe calls for the batter liquid. The flavor does not come through strongly in the finished pancake.

**As a drink:** Served cold, in a bowl or a wide ceramic cup, alongside pajeon and kimchi. The pairing of makgeolli and Korean savory pancakes is one of the most classic combinations in Korean food culture — the tartness and fizz of the drink cut through the richness of the fried batter. It also pairs well with spicy preparations where the mild sweetness provides contrast.

## Buying guide

Fresh, unpasteurized makgeolli is sold refrigerated — it must be kept cold and consumed within 5–10 days. Look for it at Korean grocery stores in the refrigerated drinks section, usually in 750ml plastic bottles. The brand *Jangsu* and *Woori Ssal* makgeolli are widely distributed.

Pasteurized makgeolli in shelf-stable cans and bottles is increasingly available at general Asian grocery stores and online. The carbonation is added rather than natural, and the flavor is slightly flatter, but it works fine for cooking and casual drinking.

## Storage

Fresh (unpasteurized) makgeolli: refrigerate and consume within 5–10 days. Always shake or stir before serving — the rice solids settle at the bottom. Once opened, consume within a day or two; the carbonation dissipates quickly. Pasteurized versions keep unopened until the printed date; refrigerate after opening and drink within a few days.

## The borderless angle

The makgeolli batter technique transfers directly to any culture's pancake or fritter tradition. Japanese *okonomiyaki* cooks have adopted it. Western chefs use it in place of beer batter for tempura and vegetable fritters, where the milder flavor interferes less than a hoppy beer would. Makgeolli has also entered the cocktail world: its mild alcohol, gentle fizz, and slightly sour sweetness make it an easy base for spritz-style drinks, usually combined with fresh citrus and a neutral spirit or none at all.
