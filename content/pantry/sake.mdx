Sake is fermented rice wine — rice plus water plus koji (*Aspergillus oryzae*, the same mold used in miso and soy sauce) plus yeast, fermented over weeks into a clear or slightly cloudy liquid at 14–16% alcohol. Unlike wine, which ferments simple grape sugars, sake requires a two-step process: koji converts the rice starches into sugars, which yeast then ferments into alcohol. This parallel fermentation is unique to sake and produces its characteristic clean, layered flavor.

## Drinking sake vs. cooking sake

These are two different products.

**Cooking sake** (*ryorishu*) has salt added — typically 2–3% — which makes it unpleasant to drink but completely fine for cooking. The salt prevents it from being taxed as an alcoholic beverage in Japan, which keeps the price low. This is the sake to buy for cooking. It comes in large, cheap bottles and lives on the pantry shelf indefinitely.

**Drinking sake** works in cooking and will produce slightly cleaner results, but it costs significantly more for no meaningful flavor difference once the alcohol has cooked off. Save the good sake for drinking.

## Why sake in cooking

Sake does three things in Japanese cooking:

**Deglazing and aroma.** Added to a hot pan after browning meat or vegetables, sake lifts the fond (the caramelized bits on the pan surface) and contributes a clean, slightly sweet aroma that rounds out the dish. The alcohol evaporates quickly at cooking temperatures.

**Marinades and tenderizing.** The alcohol in sake partially denatures surface proteins, which allows marinades to penetrate more effectively. Chicken, pork, and fish all benefit from a sake marinade — the flesh becomes more tender and the flavor penetrates deeper.

**Depth and body.** A splash of sake added to braises, stews, and sauces adds a subtle sweetness and complexity that is hard to pin down but noticeably absent when you skip it. It bridges other flavors without asserting itself.

## How to use it

Add sake to a hot pan and let it sizzle and reduce for 30–60 seconds before adding other liquids. This burns off the raw alcohol while preserving the flavor compounds. In cold applications (marinades, quick pickles), the alcohol doesn't need to cook off — it functions as a tenderizer and flavor carrier.

Typical amounts: 1–3 tablespoons for a stir-fry or pan sauce; 2–4 tablespoons for a braise or marinade.

## Where to buy and storage

Cooking sake is available at Asian grocery stores and increasingly at regular supermarkets in the Asian foods aisle. Look for bottles labeled *ryorishu* or simply "cooking sake." Kikkoman makes a widely available version. Store at room temperature; it keeps for months after opening due to the salt content.

## The cross-cultural equivalent

Dry sherry does approximately 80% of what sake does in cooking. Both are fermented, both add depth and sweetness, and both work as pan deglazing agents. The difference: sherry has a distinct nuttiness (from the flor yeast used in its production) that sake doesn't have. In dishes where the sake flavor is prominent — teriyaki glaze, Japanese-style braises — the sherry note will be detectable. In dishes where sake is one of many background elements, the substitution is seamless. Use about the same amount and add it the same way.
