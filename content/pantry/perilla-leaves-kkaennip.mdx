Perilla (*Perilla frutescens*) is a mint-family herb that occupies a position in Korean cooking unlike any other single ingredient. In Korean it is called *kkaennip* (깻잎), and it functions simultaneously as a vegetable, a wrapper, a pickling subject, a garnish, and a primary flavor. Korean cooks use it the way Italian cooks use basil — as an herb that is front and center, not a background note.

It is the same species as Japanese *shiso*, but this botanical fact obscures a meaningful difference. The Korean cultivar is larger (palm-sized or bigger), with a more assertive flavor: bold mint, anise, a faint pepperiness, and something distinctly green and green-smelling that shiso doesn't share. Japanese shiso is more delicate, more floral, and smaller. In practice they are not interchangeable if you want the dish to taste Korean or Japanese; they are substitutable if you simply need the herb family's aromatic character.

## Flavor profile

Kkaennip is intensely aromatic. The flavor is a compound of mint, basil, and anise with a rougher, more assertive edge than any of those individually. Fresh leaves have a slight bitterness that mellows when briefly pickled or blanched. The texture is soft but substantial — thicker than basil, thinner than a proper leafy green. At Korean barbecue, it functions as a palate reset between bites of rich grilled meat.

## How to use it

**As ssam (wrapping leaf):** At Korean barbecue, kkaennip replaces or supplements lettuce as a wrapper for grilled meat. A piece of *samgyeopsal* or *bulgogi*, a dab of ssamjang, a slice of garlic — wrapped in a perilla leaf and eaten in one bite. The herb's assertive flavor cuts through the fat.

**Kkaennip kimchi:** One of the quieter kimchi styles, less common than cabbage but intensely flavored. Layers of leaves packed with garlic, gochugaru, soy sauce, fish sauce, and sesame oil, fermented briefly or served fresh.

**Kkaennip jorim (braised in soy):** Leaves stacked and simmered briefly in soy sauce, sesame oil, garlic, gochugaru, and sugar, served as banchan. This is arguably perilla's best form — the braising softens the assertiveness and concentrates the flavor into something savory and herb-forward.

**Fresh garnish:** Sliced thinly into chiffonade over cold noodle dishes (*naengmyeon*, *bibim guksu*), rice bowls, and grilled fish.

## Buying guide

Kkaennip is sold fresh at Korean grocery stores, usually in small bunches. Look for leaves that are bright green with no yellowing or wilting at the edges. Avoid any bunch where the stems look dried out — the herb starts declining fast once cut. Some Asian grocery stores that are not specifically Korean carry it, but availability is inconsistent.

Frozen perilla exists (usually in Southeast Asian stores, used in Vietnamese and Thai cooking) but the texture becomes limp on thawing — fine for cooked preparations, not for wrapping.

## Storage

Fresh perilla is highly perishable: 2–3 days refrigerated, wrapped loosely in damp paper towel inside a bag. Do not wash until use. Blanching in salted boiling water for 30 seconds, shocking in ice water, and draining extends the useful life to about a week — blanched perilla can then be seasoned and served immediately or refrigerated for use in cooked dishes. The braised preparation (kkaennip jorim) keeps refrigerated for 5–7 days.

## The borderless angle

Perilla has followed Korean immigration and Korean food culture into kitchens that were never exposed to it through either Japanese or Korean traditions. It appears in Vietnamese *bun bo hue* and is used in some Thai preparations. In the Borderless Kitchen context, kkaennip is one of those herbs that makes obvious sense the moment you use it beyond its origins — try it in place of basil on a Neapolitan pizza, or as a wrapper for any grilled protein where basil or lettuce would normally go. The anise-mint compound works with grilled lamb in a way that surprises even dedicated Korean cooks.
