Rice vinegar is vinegar fermented from rice — rice wine is fermented further by acetobacter bacteria, which convert the alcohol into acetic acid. The result is a vinegar with 4–4.5% acidity, significantly lower than distilled white vinegar (5–8%) or malt vinegar (5–8%), and with a mild, slightly sweet flavor that doesn't overpower the other ingredients in a dish.

Both Japanese and Chinese cuisines use rice vinegar extensively, but the Japanese version (*komezu*) is generally lighter and more delicate, while Chinese rice vinegar tends toward a slightly fuller flavor. For most Japanese recipes, Japanese rice vinegar is specified and worth using.

## Plain vs. seasoned rice vinegar

This distinction matters, particularly for sushi rice.

**Plain rice vinegar** is exactly what it sounds like: vinegar, no additives. Use this when you want to control the seasoning yourself — for dressings, quick pickles, or adding to recipes alongside separate measures of sugar and salt.

**Seasoned rice vinegar** (*sushizu*) already has sugar and salt incorporated in the correct ratios for sushi rice preparation. You add it directly to the cooked rice without needing additional seasoning. If a sushi rice recipe tells you to combine plain rice vinegar with sugar and salt, you can substitute seasoned rice vinegar 1:1 and skip those added ingredients.

Do not confuse them in other applications. Adding seasoned rice vinegar to a salad dressing without accounting for its sugar and salt content will throw off your seasoning.

## Uses in cooking

**Sushi rice:** The defining application. Rice vinegar seasoned with sugar and salt is folded into hot steamed rice and fanned while it absorbs, creating the characteristic glossy, slightly tangy sushi rice.

**Quick pickles:** Rice vinegar is ideal for quick vegetable pickles — cucumbers, daikon, carrots, cabbage. Its mild acidity softens vegetables without overwhelming them. A basic ratio: 3 parts rice vinegar, 2 parts sugar, 1 part salt, dissolved over heat and poured over sliced vegetables. Ready in 20–30 minutes; better after a few hours.

**Dressings and sauces:** Wherever a recipe calls for a mild acid note — salad dressings, ponzu sauce, goma dare (sesame dressing) — rice vinegar provides brightness without sharpness.

**Brightening finished dishes:** A teaspoon of rice vinegar stirred into a finished soup, stew, or stir-fry at the end of cooking lifts the flavors without adding any detectable vinegar taste. Use it the way a cook uses lemon juice.

## Where to buy and storage

Mizkan is the standard brand and is widely available at Asian grocery stores and most large supermarkets. Store at room temperature, away from light. It keeps essentially indefinitely — acidity is a natural preservative.

## The cross-cultural equivalent

White wine vinegar is the closest Western equivalent, at approximately 5–6% acidity. To substitute: use slightly less than the recipe calls for (start with 75% of the stated amount and adjust). The flavor difference is small in most applications. Distilled white vinegar is sharper and less sweet — it works in a pinch but the edge is noticeable. Apple cider vinegar adds a fruity note that is distracting in Japanese applications.
