Char siu (叉燒) translates as "fork roasted" — *chā* (叉) means fork, *siu* (燒) means roasted or burned. The name describes the traditional preparation: pieces of marinated pork hung on long metal forks and lowered into wood-fired ovens in Cantonese roast meat restaurants (*siu mei*, 燒味).

The dish is one of the defining flavors of Cantonese cuisine — sweet, savory, slightly caramelized, with a specific red-mahogany color that comes from a combination of red fermented bean curd and the Maillard browning of the honey glaze. It is used as:

- A standalone dish with rice (*chāshāo fàn*)
- The filling for char siu bao (BBQ pork buns — steamed white or baked golden)
- A component of wonton filling
- A topping for noodle soups
- A filling for pineapple buns
- An element in fried rice and pad thai (in some Southeast Asian adaptations)

## The Cut

**Pork shoulder or neck (*bú huā ròu*, 豬頸肉 / *méibāo ròu*, 梅花肉):** This is the correct cut. Pork shoulder has an ideal fat distribution — enough intermuscular fat to stay moist through the high-heat roasting, but lean enough to form a proper glaze. Pork neck is even more marbled and is considered superior by many Cantonese chefs.

**What not to use:** Pork tenderloin (too lean — dries out completely), pork belly (too fatty for this specific preparation; that's used for different dishes).

**Preparation:** Cut the pork into strips approximately 3–4cm wide and 4–5cm thick, as long as the cut allows. The long strips maximize the surface area for the glaze to develop.

## The Marinade and Glaze

The characteristic flavor of char siu comes from a specific marinade with one key ingredient most home recipes omit:

### Core Marinade Ingredients

- 2 tablespoons dark soy sauce
- 2 tablespoons oyster sauce
- 2 tablespoons hoisin sauce
- 2 tablespoons honey (or maltose — traditional; deeper caramelization)
- 1 tablespoon Shaoxing rice wine
- 1 tablespoon sugar (white)
- 1 teaspoon sesame oil
- ¼ teaspoon five-spice powder

### The Critical Ingredient: Red Fermented Bean Curd (*nán rǔ*, 南乳)

Red fermented bean curd is tofu that has been fermented with red rice (*Monascus purpureus*), producing an intensely savory, funky paste with a deep reddish-purple color. One or two cubes mashed and added to the marinade:

1. Contributes the characteristic red-mahogany color that artificial red coloring approximates in cheap versions
2. Adds a deep, slightly fermented savory note that underlies the sweetness of the glaze
3. Provides enzymes that help tenderize the meat during marination

Red fermented bean curd (*nán rǔ* or red fermented tofu) is available at Chinese grocery stores. The jar looks like bean curd cubes in red liquid — use 1–2 cubes, mashed to a paste.

**Without red fermented bean curd:** The dish tastes good but is missing a layer of savory depth; the color will be brown from the soy and honey rather than the distinctive mahogany-red. Red food coloring (1/4 teaspoon) can approximate the color but not the flavor contribution.

## The Marination

Combine all marinade ingredients, including the mashed red fermented bean curd. Reserve approximately 3 tablespoons of the marinade as a glaze (set aside separately before adding meat).

Place pork strips in the remaining marinade. Ensure every surface is coated. Marinate:
- Minimum: 4 hours at room temperature or 8 hours refrigerated
- Ideal: overnight refrigeration (16–24 hours), turning once

---

## The Roasting Technique

### Oven Method (Home)

Preheat oven to 220°C (430°F). Set a wire rack over a foil-lined baking sheet (the drippings will burn and smoke).

Shake excess marinade from each piece; place on the rack. Roast 15 minutes.

**Turn and glaze:** Remove from oven. Brush the reserved glaze over each strip. Return and roast another 10 minutes.

**Final glaze and broil:** Remove; brush with glaze again. Switch oven to broil setting (high). Broil for 3–5 minutes until the glaze caramelizes and darkens — watch carefully; the sugar will go from caramelized to burned quickly. The surface should be dark red-mahogany and slightly sticky.

**Rest 5–10 minutes** before slicing. Slice across the grain into pieces 5–8mm thick.

### Gas Grill Method

Medium-high indirect heat. Follow the same turn-and-glaze sequence as above; finish over direct heat for the final caramelization.

---

## What "Done" Looks Like

The exterior should be:
- Deep mahogany to reddish-brown, darker in some spots (those are caramelized, not burned)
- Slightly sticky from the glaze
- Shrunken and slightly charred at the thinnest edge points

The interior, when sliced:
- A clear pinkish-beige cooked pork throughout (no raw pink)
- Slightly moist from the fat rendering — not dry and fibrous
- You should be able to pull the meat apart slightly with chopsticks

**Internal temperature:** 70–75°C (160–165°F) if using a thermometer.

## Char Siu Bao: The Two Versions

Char siu is used as the filling for char siu bao, one of the most iconic dim sum items:

**Steamed (*zhēng chāshāo bāo*, 蒸叉燒包):** White, pillowy steamed bun with a distinctive split at the top from steam pressure. The bun dough uses baking powder and a small amount of lard for the specific texture. Filling: diced char siu in a thick sweetened oyster sauce glaze.

**Baked (*jūshā bāo*, 焗叉燒包):** Golden-brown baked bun, often with a slightly shiny egg-wash surface. Denser than the steamed version; the filling is the same. Typically a bit sweeter.
