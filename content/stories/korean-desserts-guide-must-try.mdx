Korean desserts operate on different principles than Western sweets. They're rarely maximally sweet — the sugar is used to balance other flavors, particularly the earthiness of red beans, the starchiness of rice cake, the bitterness of sesame. Texture is often the primary pleasure: the chewiness of tteok, the crunch of yakgwa, the snow-like dissolution of bingsu. And many Korean desserts are tied to seasons or celebrations in a way that makes them meaningless outside context.

This is the guide to the major Korean dessert categories — what they are, what they taste like, and how to find them.

---

## Bingsu (빙수) — Shaved Ice

The summer dessert. Bingsu is finely shaved ice served in a large bowl with a combination of toppings: sweetened red bean paste (*팥*, pat), rice cakes (*tteok*), condensed milk, and fresh fruit. The shaving is so fine that the ice dissolves immediately on the tongue rather than crunching — it's closer in texture to snow than to Italian granita.

**Patbingsu** (팥빙수, red bean shaved ice) is the classic version — shaved ice with sweet red bean paste and tteok. It's been eaten in Korea since at least the Joseon Dynasty.

**Mango bingsu, strawberry bingsu, matcha bingsu:** The contemporary bingsu renaissance in Korean cafes uses premium ingredients. Bingsu cafes in Seoul serve elaborate versions that look almost architectural.

**How to eat:** Mix everything together. The condensed milk and sweet beans dissolve into the ice as you eat; the rice cake pieces are the chewy contrast. Eat quickly — bingsu melts.

## Tteok (떡) — Rice Cakes

Tteok is made from rice flour (glutinous or non-glutinous) pounded or pressed into shapes and prepared by steaming, pounding, or rolling. There are hundreds of varieties. It's the most significant Korean dessert category and appears at every important celebration.

**Key varieties:**
- **Songpyeon (송편):** Half-moon shaped rice cakes filled with sesame, chestnut, or red bean — made for Chuseok (Korean Thanksgiving). Pine-scented from steaming over pine needles
- **Injeolmi (인절미):** Glutinous rice pounded to a smooth, extremely chewy consistency, rolled in toasted soybean powder (*konggaru*). The texture is unusual — intensely chewy, almost sticky, with a roasted soy coating
- **Baekseolgi (백설기):** Plain white steamed rice cake — mild, slightly sweet, eaten at first birthday celebrations (*doljanchi*)
- **Chapssal tteok:** Glutinous rice cake, often colored and shaped; the category that includes Japanese-style mochi shapes
- **Jeolpyeon:** Patterned rice cake pressed in wooden molds with decorative designs. Traditional and highly aesthetic

**Tteok flavor:** Most tteok is mildly sweet with a prominent starchy, rice character. The flavors are subtle; the experience is primarily textural.

## Yakgwa (약과) — Honey Cookies

Yakgwa (meaning "medicinal confection") are deep-fried wheat cookies soaked in honey syrup and infused with ginger and cinnamon. They look like small brown cookies or hexagonal shapes; the texture is crispy outside, chewy inside from the honey absorption.

They were historically expensive — made with expensive ingredients (honey, sesame oil, wheat flour) and fried in sesame oil. A luxury food at Joseon court tables, now widely available at Korean grocery stores and online.

The flavor: intensely honey-sweet, slightly gingery, with a toasted sesame oil note underneath. Very rich for a small piece. A cup of tea alongside cuts the sweetness.

---

## Sikhye (식혜) — Sweet Rice Punch

Sikhye is a traditional fermented rice punch — a slightly sweet, amber-colored beverage made from malted barley and cooked rice, fermented at warm temperature until the enzyme activity converts starches to sugar. It's more of a drink than a dessert, but it's served as a concluding course.

The flavor: sweet and slightly fermented, with a gentle toasted grain character. Rice grains float in the liquid, providing a small textural element. Cold sikhye after a heavy meal has a distinctly refreshing, palate-clearing quality.

**Sujeonggwa** (수정과) is the other traditional sweet drink: a cinnamon-persimmon punch served cold, made by simmering cinnamon sticks and dried ginger, then steeping with dried persimmon. Much more intensely spiced than sikhye.

## Hodugwaja (호두과자) — Walnut Cookies

A famous street food from Cheonan city — small walnut-shaped pastries with a thin, slightly sweet batter exterior and a walnut + red bean paste filling. The walnut shell mold gives them their appearance. Very soft, mildly sweet, eaten hot from the mold at specialty stalls.

---

## Bungeoppang (붕어빵) — Fish-Shaped Waffles

Street food that appears from October to March — a batter cooked in a fish-shaped iron mold with a sweet red bean paste filling. The batter is similar to a waffle but lighter; the exterior is crispy and slightly sweet. The red bean center is warm and dense.

They're sold 3-5 for ₩1,000-2,000 (under $2) at street stalls and are one of Korea's most recognizable winter street foods. The Taiyaki equivalent in Japan came later and was influenced by Korean bungeoppang culture during the Japanese colonial period.

---

## Dasik (다식) — Traditional Tea Confections

Small, decorative pressed confections traditionally served with tea at formal occasions. Made from soybean powder, sesame, pollen, or grain powder pressed into flower molds. More impressive visually than strongly flavored — subtle, slightly sweet, meant as a companion to bitter tea.

---

## Korean Dessert Outside Korea

Korean bakery chains (Paris Baguette, Tous les Jours) are present in major cities globally and carry some traditional Korean desserts alongside Western-style pastry. Korean grocery stores (H Mart) carry packaged tteok and yakgwa year-round.

For seasonal items (bungeoppang, hotteok) outside Korea: Korean food festivals and Korean-neighborhood street markets are the most reliable sources. Some Korean restaurants serve bingsu in summer and bungeoppang in winter.
