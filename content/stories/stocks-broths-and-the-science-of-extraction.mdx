Stock is one of the oldest techniques in cooking and one of the least understood at home. It is treated as a background task — something that simmers unattended while other things happen — but the quality of every sauce, braise, risotto, and soup built on it depends entirely on decisions made during extraction: what goes in, at what temperature, for how long, and in what order.

The science is not complicated, but it is specific.

---

## What Stock Is

Stock is the water-soluble extraction of three distinct things: **gelatin** (from collagen in connective tissue and bones), **flavor compounds** (from the Maillard-browned surfaces, aromatics, and meat), and **minerals and nutrients** (from bone marrow and the bones themselves).

A stock that has all three in balance is glossy when hot, sets to a jelly when cold, and has depth that plain water cannot replicate. The gelatin is what gives professional sauces their body and what makes a reduced sauce coat a spoon. Without it, you have broth — flavorful, but thin.

**Stock vs. broth:** The distinction is not always maintained rigorously, but technically: stock is made primarily from bones (with connective tissue and some meat), is unseasoned, and is used as a building block. Broth is made from meat, is seasoned, and is drinkable on its own. In practice, most home stocks fall somewhere between the two. The key variable is collagen content — more bones and connective tissue, more gelatin, more body.

---

## Collagen and Gelatin: The Core Chemistry

Collagen is the most abundant structural protein in connective tissue — cartilage, tendons, the membranes around bones, the skin. When collagen is held in hot liquid (160°F–180°F / 71°C–82°C) for long enough, the triple-helix protein structure unwinds and hydrolyzes into gelatin.

This is why stock-making time is measured in hours, not minutes. Collagen conversion is not fast, and it is not complete at lower temperatures or shorter times. A chicken stock that simmers for 1 hour extracts flavor but relatively little gelatin. The same stock at 3–4 hours has meaningfully more body.

**High-collagen bones and cuts for maximum gelatin:**
- Chicken: backs, feet (extremely high collagen), necks, wings, carcasses
- Beef/veal: knuckles, oxtail, short ribs, marrow bones, calves' feet
- Pork: trotters, hocks
- Fish: heads and frames (not collagen-rich like other stocks, but they extract quickly)

A useful test: after a stock cools in the refrigerator, it should set to a loose jelly. If it stays liquid, it has insufficient gelatin. This is not ruined stock — it is just weak stock that will require more reduction before it has the body a finished sauce needs.

## Temperature: The Most Important Variable

The correct temperature for stock is a **barely-perceptible simmer** — bubbles rising occasionally, not boiling. This is the most common mistake in home stock-making, and it has significant consequences.

**What boiling does to stock:**
When stock boils aggressively, three things happen:
1. Fat and water emulsify under turbulence, creating a permanently cloudy, fatty liquid
2. Proteins that should coagulate and be skimmed off are instead beaten into tiny particles that stay suspended
3. The delicate, volatile aromatic compounds are driven off rather than remaining in solution

**What a gentle simmer produces:**
- Convection circulates the liquid slowly, extracting evenly without agitation
- Fat rises to the surface where it can be skimmed
- Proteins coagulate into a foam that floats and can be removed
- Flavor compounds remain in the liquid

The target temperature is 180°F–200°F (82°C–93°C) — hot enough for full extraction and collagen conversion, cool enough to avoid emulsification. A thermometer is more reliable than visual cues during the learning phase.

## Roasting: Flavor Before Extraction

For brown stocks (fond de veau, fond brun, dark chicken stock), bones and vegetables are roasted before going into the water. This is not optional — it is the source of the deep, complex flavor that distinguishes a brown stock from a white one.

**The Maillard reaction on bone surfaces** at oven temperatures (400°F–450°F / 200°C–230°C) creates hundreds of flavor compounds that would not form in simmering water. Roasting also renders some fat from the marrow and develops color that carries into the finished stock.

**Tomato paste** is often added to the roasting pan in the final minutes — caramelized in the oven with the vegetables, it adds acidity, color, and body. This is standard in classical veal stock and French mother sauces.

**White stocks** (fond blanc — white chicken stock, fish stock, veal blanc) skip the roasting step entirely. The bones go directly into cold water, and the stock is kept as pale and clean-flavored as possible. The goal is a neutral, light base that doesn't compete with the finished dish's flavor.

## The Process: Step by Step

**1. Start with cold water.** Bones go into cold water and are brought up slowly. This allows proteins to dissolve into the water and then coagulate and rise as foam, which you can skim. Starting with hot water seizes the proteins on the surface of the bone immediately, trapping impurities inside.

**2. Blanching for clarity (optional but recommended for beef/veal).** Bring bones to a boil, pour off the water (which will be grey and murky), rinse the bones under cold water, clean the pot, then start fresh with cold water. This produces a dramatically cleaner, more neutral stock. Not needed for chicken stocks made from roasted carcasses.

**3. Bring to a simmer, then skim.** As the stock heats, a grey/brown foam will rise. Skim it continuously for the first 20–30 minutes. This is coagulated protein and fat — removing it early prevents it from dissolving back into the liquid and clouding the stock.

**4. Add aromatics.** The classic French mirepoix ratio: **2 parts onion : 1 part carrot : 1 part celery** by weight. Leek can replace or supplement celery. A **bouquet garni** — bay leaf, thyme sprigs, parsley stems (not leaves), peppercorns — tied or bundled. Add these after skimming, not before: they would be harder to skim around and would be overcooked by the time the stock finishes.

**5. Simmer uncovered at the correct temperature.** Times by stock type:
- Fish stock: 20–30 minutes (fish frames turn bitter if overcooked)
- Chicken stock: 3–4 hours
- Veal/beef stock: 6–8 hours (some recipes go longer)
- Pork stock: 3–4 hours

**6. Do not stir.** Stirring agitates the stock and causes clouding. Leave it alone.

**7. Strain carefully.** Pour through a fine-mesh strainer or a colander lined with cheesecloth. Do not press the solids — pressing forces cloudy particles through. Let it drain by gravity.

**8. Cool quickly and degrease.** Spread the hot stock into wide, shallow containers for fast cooling (food safety), then refrigerate. The fat will solidify on top and can be lifted off in solid pieces. This is the easiest and most complete method of degreasing.

## Acid and Extraction

A small amount of acid — apple cider vinegar (1–2 tablespoons per gallon of water), wine, or the natural acidity of tomatoes — added at the start of extraction helps break down collagen into gelatin faster and more completely. The amount is small enough that it does not flavor the stock. This is standard in bone broth recipes and works in traditional stocks as well.

---

## Reduction and Glace

Stock can be reduced to concentrate it:
- **Demi-glace:** Stock reduced by half to 75%, thick and glossy, used as a sauce base or finishing element
- **Glace de viande (meat glaze):** Stock reduced to a syrup that coats the back of a spoon thickly and sets to a solid at room temperature. A tablespoon of glace dissolved in hot water reconstitutes to a cup of intensely flavored stock

These can be frozen in ice cube trays and used as instant flavor additions to any dish.

---

## What a Good Stock Enables

Stock is infrastructure. It is the reason a pan sauce made in 5 minutes with restaurant deglazing technique tastes like it simmered for hours — because it did, in a different form, before service. Every braise, risotto, and soup that uses stock instead of water is drawing on the time and technique invested in that stock.

The home cook who makes stock once every few weeks and freezes it in containers has a fundamentally different kitchen than the one who reaches for boxed broth every time. The flavor ceiling is simply higher.

Good stock is patient cooking stored in liquid form.
