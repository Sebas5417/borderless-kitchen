Sous vide — French for "under vacuum" — is a cooking technique where food is sealed in a bag and cooked in a temperature-controlled water bath. A precision circulator maintains the water at an exact temperature, typically for an extended period. The food reaches that temperature and holds there.

The reason anyone does this: it is impossible to overcook food when the water temperature equals your target doneness. A steak in water set to 130°F (54°C) can sit there for two hours or four hours — it will never exceed 130°F. This is not possible with any other cooking method.

---

## The Core Principle

Traditional cooking is imprecise by nature. A 400°F oven is 270° hotter than a perfectly cooked chicken breast (165°F / 74°C). You're racing against time — trying to pull the food before the outside dries out and the inside overcooks. The skill is timing; the margin is narrow.

Sous vide eliminates this race by making the cooking medium the same temperature as the desired result. There is no gradient to race against. The only question is whether the food has reached that temperature all the way through — which is a function of time and thickness, not skill.

This means:
- A 1-inch steak reaches its core temperature in about 1 hour
- A 2-inch steak in about 2.5 hours
- Chicken thighs at 165°F are done in 1–2 hours and stay perfectly moist because they never go above 165°F

## Equipment

**Immersion circulator:** A wand that clips to a container, heats the water, and circulates it to maintain even temperature. Anova and Joule (now ChefSteps) are the leading consumer brands. Price range: $80–$250.

**Container:** Any deep pot, cambro, or hotel pan. For longer cooks (4+ hours), a cover (lid, plastic wrap, or silicone cover) reduces evaporation.

**Bags:** Vacuum-sealed bags are ideal (vacuum sealer + bags), but zip-lock freezer bags work well for most applications. Use the water displacement method: seal most of the zip-lock, lower into water until the water pressure pushes air out, then seal completely.

## Temperatures and Times: The Key Reference Points

The temperature you set determines the final texture. The time determines how thoroughly it gets there and, for tougher cuts, how much connective tissue breaks down.

**Steak:**
| Doneness | Temperature |
|----------|-------------|
| Rare | 120–125°F (49–52°C) |
| Medium-rare | 129–134°F (54–57°C) |
| Medium | 135–144°F (57–62°C) |
| Medium-well | 145–155°F (63–68°C) |

Time: 1–4 hours depending on thickness. Longer doesn't harm a steak at these temperatures — it just doesn't improve it much beyond 2 hours for tender cuts.

**Chicken breast:** 140°F (60°C) for 1.5 hours produces extremely juicy, safe chicken. At this temperature, pasteurization is achieved through time rather than instantaneous kill — the USDA's safety guidelines assume instantaneous kill at 165°F, but the same safety level is achieved at lower temperatures held long enough. At 140°F / 60°C, chicken is pasteurized after 30 minutes.

**Chicken thighs:** 165°F (74°C) for 1–2 hours. Thighs have more collagen and benefit from slightly higher temperature and time for texture.

**Salmon:** 120–125°F (49–52°C) for 30–40 minutes. Silky, barely-set, translucent at the center. A completely different experience than conventionally cooked salmon.

**Pork tenderloin:** 140°F (60°C) for 1–2 hours. Slightly pink and juicy — safe (pork is safe at 145°F with a 3-minute rest; lower temperature for longer achieves the same pasteurization).

**Short ribs / tough braising cuts:** 155–175°F (68–79°C) for 24–72 hours. Long time + moderate temperature breaks down collagen without drying out the muscle fibers. The result is tender but with a distinct, intact texture — not the falling-apart of conventional braise, which some people prefer.

**Eggs:** 145°F (63°C) for 45–75 minutes. The white just sets (still slightly soft near the yolk), the yolk is completely fluid and glossy. The "onsen egg" — a Japanese preparation — uses this technique. At 167°F (75°C) for 13 minutes, you get a set white and jammy yolk similar to a soft-boiled egg.

## The Sear: Non-Negotiable for Meat

Sous vide cooks food to perfect internal temperature but produces no browning. The Maillard reaction — which creates crust, color, and the flavor compounds associated with "cooked meat" — requires temperatures above 280°F (138°C), which a sous vide bath never reaches.

The sear after sous vide is essential and should be aggressive:
1. Pat the meat completely dry after removing from the bag. Surface moisture is the enemy of a good sear.
2. Heat a cast iron or stainless pan until very hot — above smoking point.
3. Add a high-smoke-point oil.
4. Sear 45–90 seconds per side. The interior is already at temperature; you're only building the crust.
5. Baste with butter and herbs during the sear.

The advantage: because the interior is already perfectly cooked, you can sear aggressively without worrying about overcooking the center. This produces a thinner, crisper crust than the careful sear required on a raw steak.

## When Sous Vide Is Worth It

**Definitely use it for:**
- Cooking multiple steaks to identical doneness for a group
- Chicken breast (the single biggest win — sous vide chicken breast is dramatically better than conventionally cooked)
- Delicate fish like salmon that overcooks in seconds on a pan
- Pork tenderloin
- Eggs at unusual temperatures
- Tough cuts you want braised texture without braising liquid

**Probably not worth it for:**
- A single steak when you can develop the skill to sear precisely — the result is very similar
- Vegetables that can be roasted better with dry heat (Maillard reaction)
- Anything that's fast and forgiving conventionally
- When you need the food in under an hour and don't own the equipment

**The real killer app:** batch cooking. Sous vide five chicken breasts, refrigerate them in the bag, sear one each night as needed. The bags keep in the refrigerator for 3–5 days. This is where the technique justifies the equipment investment most clearly for home cooks.

## Food Safety

The main concern with sous vide is that extended low-temperature cooking can be in the "danger zone" (40–140°F / 4–60°C) where bacteria can grow. Two principles keep it safe:

1. **Time × temperature:** Pasteurization is achieved at lower temperatures given sufficient time. The FDA's food safety tables for time-temperature combinations are widely available and show that, for example, chicken at 140°F is safe after 30 minutes.

2. **Immediate sear or immediate refrigeration:** If not eating immediately, cool the bag rapidly in ice water and refrigerate. Reheat in the water bath before the final sear.

Never leave a sealed bag of protein sitting at room temperature. The sealed, low-oxygen environment is exactly what some anaerobic bacteria prefer.

---

Sous vide is not magic — it trades precise temperature control for the browning and rapid crust-building of conventional methods. For specific applications, particularly chicken breast and thick steaks for groups, the trade is strongly worth it. For most everyday cooking, the skill of reading heat by sight, sound, and feel achieves results that are equally good and considerably faster. Both have their place.
