The most frequent question in cooking advice columns is some version of: "I followed the recipe exactly, but it tastes flat. What did I do wrong?" Almost universally, the answer is the same: not enough salt, added too late, and not enough tasting along the way.

Seasoning is the most fundamental cooking skill and the least taught. It doesn't appear in measurements or timers. It requires judgment developed through repetition. But understanding what seasoning does and when to do it makes that judgment faster to build.

---

## What Salt Actually Does

Salt does not just make food taste salty. In the right quantity, it:

**Suppresses bitterness.** Salt inhibits the bitter taste receptors. This is why salted butter tastes sweeter than unsalted, why a pinch of salt in coffee reduces bitterness, and why undersalted food can taste harsh even when it's technically cooked correctly.

**Amplifies other flavors.** Salt increases the volatility of aromatic compounds — meaning more flavor molecules reach your nose. Food seasoned properly smells more interesting and tastes more complex.

**Draws out moisture.** Salt applied to vegetables or proteins draws water to the surface through osmosis. This can be useful (salting eggplant to reduce bitterness, dry-brining meat for better crust) or a problem if done at the wrong time (salting mushrooms before they brown causes them to steam rather than sear).

**Changes texture over time.** In proteins, salt denatures proteins and improves texture when given time to penetrate (brining, dry-brining). In vegetables, salt wilts and softens cell walls.

## Types of Salt and When They Matter

**Kosher salt:** The standard for cooking. Large, irregular crystals are easy to pinch and control. Two major brands — Diamond Crystal and Morton — are not interchangeable by volume. Morton is roughly twice as dense as Diamond Crystal. If a recipe specifies one, using the other without adjusting volume produces wildly different results. When in doubt, use Diamond Crystal, which is what most American recipe developers use.

**Fine sea salt / table salt:** Denser than kosher. Use about half the volume when substituting for kosher. Iodized table salt has a slight metallic flavor from the iodine additive — avoid for delicate dishes.

**Flaky sea salt (Maldon, fleur de sel):** Not for cooking — for finishing. The large, delicate flakes provide textural contrast and bursts of salinity. Add at the last second, after plating. Dissolves quickly and loses its point if added too early.

**When the type doesn't matter:** In soups, braises, and pasta water where the salt fully dissolves into liquid, the type is irrelevant. Use whatever you have.

## When to Salt: The Timing Rules

**Before cooking (proteins):** Salt penetrates meat when given time. Salt a steak at least 40 minutes before cooking, or up to 24–48 hours in the refrigerator (dry-brining). The surface draws out moisture, which then reabsorbs with the salt dissolved in it — better seasoning all the way through and better crust formation (less surface moisture to steam off).

Avoid: salting proteins right before cooking (10–30 minutes before). In this window, surface moisture is drawn out but hasn't had time to reabsorb — you cook with wet surface, which inhibits browning.

**Pasta water:** Salt the water heavily — it should taste like mild seawater. This is the only moment to season the pasta itself. A properly salted pasta water makes the difference between pasta that tastes of flour and pasta that has flavor at its core.

**Vegetables during sautéing:** Salt early to draw out moisture and concentrate flavor. For mushrooms or eggplant, wait until they've started to color before salting — salting too early causes them to steam in their own liquid.

**Soups and sauces:** Season in layers throughout cooking, not just at the end. Each addition changes as liquid reduces — taste frequently and adjust. A final adjustment of seasoning after reducing is always needed.

**Eggs and egg dishes:** Salt before cooking (in the beaten egg) for scrambles and omelets — this produces silkier texture. Salt after for fried and poached eggs.

**Baking:** Salt is added with the dry ingredients and distributed throughout. Don't reduce it — it's doing structural work (strengthening gluten, controlling yeast, amplifying sweetness) as well as seasoning.

## Pepper: Black, White, and When Not to Use It

Black pepper is sharp, aromatic, and spicy from piperine — a compound that also enhances the bioavailability of other spices. Fresh-ground black pepper has dramatically more flavor than pre-ground; the volatile oils dissipate within minutes of grinding.

**When to use black pepper:**
- On meat before searing (the heat toasts the peppercorns)
- In braised dishes and soups (long cooking mellows the sharpness)
- On eggs, pasta, cheese
- As a finishing element on salads and simple dishes

**When to avoid black pepper:**
- In delicate cream sauces where black specks are visually undesirable — use white pepper (the same berry, outer husk removed; sharper, less complex)
- When you want heat without pepper's specific flavor — use cayenne or chili
- In some Asian dishes where black pepper's flavor profile doesn't fit

## The Art of Tasting

The reason most home cooking is underseasoned is that cooks don't taste enough. Tasting once at the end — after everything is plated — is too late. You need to taste:

1. **At the beginning of cooking:** Taste your base (stock, sauce, brine) before adding ingredients.
2. **During cooking, multiple times:** Every 10–15 minutes for longer dishes. Every few minutes for quick sautés.
3. **After every major addition:** Each ingredient changes the balance.
4. **Before reducing:** Concentrated food tastes saltier — know where you started.
5. **Just before serving:** Final adjustment. This is the only moment you can rescue something.

**How to taste properly:**
- Let the food cool slightly — heat numbs taste buds
- Use a clean spoon each time
- Take a genuine mouthful, not a drop
- Wait a moment — some flavors develop slowly

**What to adjust when something tastes off:**

| Tastes | Fix |
|--------|-----|
| Flat/dull | More salt first, then acid |
| Too salty | Add acid (lemon), sweetness, or more liquid/bulk |
| One-dimensional | Add acid (brightens) or fat (enriches) |
| Too sharp/bright | Add fat, sweetness, or cook longer |
| Too rich/heavy | Add acid — almost always the answer |
| Bitter | Add salt, sweetness, or fat |

## Acid as a Seasoning Tool

Acid — lemon juice, vinegar, wine, citrus zest — is the most underused flavor tool in home cooking. Its role is to brighten and balance, not to make food taste sour.

When food tastes almost right but something is missing: add acid before adding more salt. A squeeze of lemon juice over a finished pasta, roasted vegetables, or a braise lifts the flavor in the same way that turning up the contrast on a photo reveals detail that was always there. The salt is the volume; the acid is the clarity.

**Timing:** Add acid at the very end. High heat destroys the volatile compounds that give citrus juice its brightness. Squeeze lemon juice over the finished dish, not into the pan thirty minutes before serving.

---

Seasoning is the skill that can't be fully taught from a recipe. The measurement "season to taste" is honest — it means your taste, informed by enough repetition to know what properly seasoned food feels like. The only way to build that is to taste constantly, adjust intentionally, and pay attention to the result.
