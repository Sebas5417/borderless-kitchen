Sri Lanka sits at a cultural and culinary crossroads — influenced by the trading history of the Indian Ocean, with South Indian Tamil cooking in the north and east, Sinhalese Buddhist cooking in the south and center, Malay and Dutch and British colonial layers, and an indigenous cooking tradition built around rice, coconut, and spice that predates all external influences.

Hoppers belong to the indigenous Sinhalese tradition. They are cooked in a small rounded pan (the hopper pan, or appachchi) — a miniature wok shape — that gives them their characteristic bowl form: the batter pools in the center for the soft, thick base while spreading up the sides in a thin, lacy rim that crisps as it sets.

The fermentation is the flavor. Hoppers made with fresh batter taste flat. The overnight fermentation — traditionally with toddy (palm wine) as the leavening agent, modernly with instant yeast — develops a subtle sourness that is as important to the finished hopper as the coconut milk richness. Together, they produce a flavor that nothing else tastes like.

## The Batter

**Rice flour:** Raw rice flour (not pre-cooked) is traditional. Some recipes call for soaking and grinding raw rice; rice flour is a workable substitute.

**Coconut milk:** Full-fat, freshly pressed in Sri Lanka; canned full-fat coconut milk is the home-cook substitute.

**Leavening:** Traditionally palm toddy (acts as a wild yeast). Modern standard: instant yeast or a combination of yeast and baking soda.

**Fermentation:** 8–12 hours at room temperature. The batter should slightly puff and develop bubbles on the surface.

## The Pan

A hopper pan (available at South Asian grocery stores or online) is strongly recommended. It produces the characteristic bowl shape. A very small non-stick wok with rounded sides approximates it. A flat pan produces a flat crêpe — technically possible but not a hopper.

## Pol Sambol (Coconut Sambol) — Essential Accompaniment

Pol sambol is what hoppers are eaten with:
- 1 cup freshly grated coconut (or shredded unsweetened dried coconut, slightly moistened)
- 2–3 red chilies or 1–2 tablespoons chili flakes (adjust heat)
- 1 shallot or small onion, very finely sliced
- Juice of 1 lime
- Salt

Combine; mix well. The coconut should be flecked red with chili, slightly moist, sour from the lime, fiery. This is a raw preparation — everything stays raw and fresh.

**Recipe: Hoppers (Makes 15–20)**

**Batter (make the night before):**
- 250g raw rice flour
- 1 can (400ml) full-fat coconut milk
- 100ml water
- 1 teaspoon instant yeast
- 1/2 teaspoon sugar
- 1/4 teaspoon salt

**Egg hoppers:** 1 egg per hopper

**Method — batter:**

1. Combine rice flour, coconut milk, water, yeast, sugar, and salt. Whisk until smooth.

2. Cover with a clean cloth; leave at room temperature 8–12 hours. The batter will puff slightly and develop bubbles.

3. In the morning, stir the batter. It should be thin enough to swirl in the pan; add a little water if needed.

**Method — cooking:**

1. Heat a hopper pan or small rounded wok over medium-high heat. Oil lightly.

2. Pour a ladleful of batter into the pan. Immediately swirl the pan so batter coats the sides in a thin layer while thickening in the center.

3. Cover; cook 2–3 minutes until edges are set and golden and center is cooked through.

*Egg hopper:* After swirling the batter, crack an egg into the center before covering. Cook until the white is set but yolk is still slightly soft.

4. Remove with a spatula; serve immediately with pol sambol and curry if desired.

*The hopper should have a white, soft, slightly spongy center and golden, crispy, lacy edges. If the edges aren't crisping, increase heat slightly.*
