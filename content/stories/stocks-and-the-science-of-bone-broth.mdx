Bones contain collagen — a structural protein organized into long, triple-helix strands that form the connective tissue, cartilage, tendons, and the matrix within the bone itself. When bones are simmered in water for an extended period, the heat breaks the hydrogen bonds holding the collagen triple-helix together. The strands unwind and dissolve into the water as individual gelatin molecules. When the stock cools, these gelatin molecules form a loose three-dimensional network that traps water — the stock gels.

The gel strength of a finished stock is the most reliable indicator of its quality. A stock that sets firmly in the refrigerator has high gelatin content; one that stays completely liquid has very little. Gel strength is determined by the collagen content of the bones used, the liquid-to-bone ratio, and the cooking time.

## Types of Bones and Their Collagen Content

Not all bones contribute equally to gelatin. The amount of collagen varies by bone type:

**High collagen (ideal for stock):**
- Chicken feet — the highest collagen-per-weight of any chicken part; produce extremely firm gel
- Chicken wings and necks — high cartilage content
- Beef knuckles and oxtail — large amounts of cartilage and connective tissue
- Pork trotters — extremely high collagen; often added to other stocks for gel boost
- Veal bones — very high collagen relative to weight; classic French stock base

**Moderate collagen:**
- Chicken carcasses (backs, frames)
- Beef short ribs (some cartilage, more meat flavor)
- Ham hocks

**Low collagen (add flavor, not gel):**
- Beef marrow bones — contribute rich fat and flavor; little collagen
- Flat bones (ribs, spine)
- Large beef shank bones — some collagen, primarily flavor

A practical approach: combine high-collagen bones (for gel) with meaty bones or scraps (for flavor). A chicken stock made only from chicken backs will be flavorful but weakly gelled; adding a pound of chicken feet will firm it significantly.

## Roasting: The Maillard Layer

Brown stock (fond brun) and white stock (fond blanc) differ primarily in one step: whether the bones are roasted before simmering.

**White stock:** bones added raw (sometimes blanched to remove impurities). Result: pale, clean, delicate flavor. Used in velouté, cream sauces, and poaching liquids where color matters.

**Brown stock:** bones roasted at 400–450°F (204–232°C) until deeply browned, then simmered. Result: rich amber color, complex roasted flavor, deeper umami. Used in demi-glace, braising liquids, and dark sauces.

The roasting step triggers Maillard reactions on the bone surfaces and any attached meat — amino acids and sugars react at high dry heat to produce hundreds of flavor compounds that would never form in a water environment. Tomato paste is often added during the roasting step (smeared on bones or caramelized in the roasting pan) to add acid, color, and additional Maillard products.

**The fond:** the browned bits that stick to the roasting pan are concentrated Maillard products. They should be added to the stock — deglaze the pan with water and scrape up every bit.

## The Simmer, Not the Boil

The most critical technique in stock-making: keep it at a gentle simmer, never a boil.

A rolling boil causes turbulence that beats fat droplets into the stock, creating a permanent emulsion — the stock turns opaque and murky and cannot be clarified. Fat and water don't fully emulsify permanently unless agitated into very small droplets under heat, which is exactly what boiling does.

A gentle simmer (190–200°F / 88–93°C, small bubbles rising slowly at one side of the pot) allows:
- Fat to rise to the surface where it can be skimmed
- The stock to remain clear
- Proteins and impurities to rise as scum (gray foam) that can be skimmed off

**The scum:** in the first 20–30 minutes of simmering, gray-brown foam rises to the surface. This is denatured protein and blood residue. Skim it off with a spoon. After the initial scum clears, the stock should be skimmed only for fat throughout cooking.

**Blanching (optional for white stock):** cover bones with cold water, bring to a boil, drain and rinse. This removes excess blood and protein before the main simmer, producing a cleaner, clearer white stock.

## Acids and Their Role

Adding a small amount of acid — typically 1–2 tablespoons of apple cider vinegar or wine per gallon of stock — is a common technique, particularly in bone broth production.

The rationale: acid helps break down collagen by disrupting the protein structure, potentially increasing gelatin extraction. The research support for meaningful additional gelatin extraction is modest, but the effect exists. The amount of acid used is small enough that it should not be detectable in the finished stock — if the stock tastes sour, too much acid was used.

Wine added to stock (white wine for white stocks, red wine for brown stocks) contributes acid plus flavor compounds from the wine itself, and any alcohol evaporates during the long simmer.

## Aromatics: The Holy Trinity and Beyond

The classic aromatic base for Western stocks:

**Mirepoix (2:1:1 ratio):** 2 parts onion, 1 part carrot, 1 part celery by weight. Aromatics are added roughly chopped — they simmer for hours and the size doesn't matter. Cutting them smaller only speeds extraction; for long-simmered stocks it makes no difference.

**Bouquet garni:** a bundle of herbs — typically thyme, bay leaf, and parsley stems — tied together or placed in cheesecloth. Added in the last hour or two of cooking; prolonged simmering can make herb flavors bitter.

**What not to add:**
- Cruciferous vegetables (broccoli, cabbage, cauliflower) — produce sulfurous compounds
- Starchy vegetables — cloud the stock
- Bell peppers — overpowering flavor

**Asian stock variations** add aromatics like ginger, scallions, and star anise (for Chinese-style stocks), or galangal and lemongrass (for Southeast Asian styles) — the principle is the same: aromatics are added to flavor the water, not to be eaten.

## Cooking Times by Stock Type

Extracting gelatin and flavor takes time. Different bones require different durations:

| Stock Type | Simmer Time | Notes |
|-----------|-------------|-------|
| Chicken stock | 3–4 hours | Longer than this, bones begin to taste hollow |
| Beef/veal stock | 6–12 hours | Large bones require longer for collagen extraction |
| Pork stock | 4–6 hours | |
| Fish stock | 20–30 minutes | Over-extraction makes fish stock bitter and glue-like |
| Vegetable stock | 45 minutes–1 hour | No collagen; longer cooking extracts bitter compounds |
| Bone broth | 12–24 hours | Extended simmer; maximum gelatin extraction |

**Fish stock is the exception:** fish bones contain a different form of collagen that extracts very quickly. Simmering fish stock beyond 30–40 minutes draws out bitter, glue-like compounds from the bones.

## Reduction and Demi-Glace

A finished stock can be reduced — simmered uncovered to evaporate water and concentrate the gelatin and flavor. Different concentrations have different names:

- **Stock (fond):** full dilution, standard
- **Jus lié:** reduced stock, lightly thickened, used as-is or with a small amount of starch
- **Demi-glace:** reduced by 75% to a syrupy, intensely flavored sauce base
- **Glace de viande (meat glaze):** reduced to a thick, sticky paste that sets to a firm solid; can be reconstituted with water

Demi-glace is the foundation of classical French cuisine — one quart of demi-glace represents 4–5 quarts of brown stock, reduced to capture maximum flavor and gelatin in minimum volume.

## Straining and Storage

Strain through a fine-mesh strainer (or cheesecloth for clarity) without pressing on the solids — pressing forces fat and fine particles through the strainer, clouding the stock. Let the stock drain by gravity.

Cool rapidly before refrigerating: set the stockpot in an ice bath and stir until cool. This prevents the temperature danger zone (40–140°F / 4–60°C) from allowing bacterial growth.

Refrigerated stock keeps 5–7 days. The fat cap that forms on the surface of chilled stock should not be removed until use — it seals the stock and extends its shelf life. Frozen stock keeps 3–6 months.

---

Stock is not a recipe — it is a process of extraction. Every decision (which bones, whether to roast, how long to simmer, at what temperature) directly affects what ends up in the water. The logic is simple: the more collagen in the bones, the lower the simmer, and the longer the time, the better the gel. Everything else is preference.
