*Kimchi jjigae* (김치찌개) is not a way to use up old kimchi. It is a dish that specifically requires old kimchi — sour, fully fermented, sometimes months old. Fresh kimchi (the kind you'd eat with a meal) produces a thin, bright stew with none of the depth that makes kimchi jjigae what it is. Over-fermented kimchi — funky, acidic, slightly fizzy when pressed — produces the real thing: a stew so complex and warming that most Koreans name it in their top three home comfort foods.

This is why Koreans often have two kimchi going simultaneously: fresh kimchi for eating raw, older kimchi earmarked for jjigae.

---

## The Kimchi

**How old is old enough:** Kimchi is ready for jjigae when it is sour enough that eating it straight would make you pucker. This typically means kimchi that has been fermenting in the refrigerator for at least 3-4 weeks. Six weeks to three months produces an even better stew.

**Signs your kimchi is ready for jjigae:**
- Sour smell (lactic acid) when you open the container
- Bright orange juice in the container — this is kimchi liquid, which goes into the stew
- Slightly translucent cabbage (the cells have broken down)

If you don't have well-fermented kimchi: buy the most aged kimchi you can find (some Korean grocery stores sell "ripe" kimchi specifically). Adding 1-2 tablespoons of rice vinegar to younger kimchi approximates some of the sourness.

## Ingredients (2-3 servings)

- 250-300g well-fermented kimchi, cut into 3-4cm pieces
- 100-150ml kimchi liquid (the juice in the kimchi container — don't discard)
- 200g pork belly, cut into bite-sized pieces (or pork shoulder)
- 200g firm or medium-firm tofu, cut into 2cm cubes
- 1 tablespoon gochugaru (Korean red pepper flakes) — adjust to taste
- 1 tablespoon gochujang
- 1 tablespoon soy sauce
- 1 teaspoon sesame oil
- 1 teaspoon sugar (balances the acidity)
- 200ml water or pork broth
- 3 cloves garlic, minced
- 1 teaspoon sesame seeds
- 2-3 green onions, sliced

---

## Method

### 1. Render the Pork

In a heavy pot or stone bowl (*dolsot*), heat sesame oil over medium heat. Add the pork belly pieces. Cook until the fat begins to render and the edges turn golden — 4-5 minutes. Do not fully cook through at this stage.

The pork fat will flavor the entire stew base. This step is why kimchi jjigae made with pork belly is better than versions made with canned tuna (which is lighter and faster but lacks the fat depth).

### 2. Build the Base

Add garlic to the rendered pork fat. Cook 1 minute until fragrant. Add gochugaru and gochujang. Stir and cook 1-2 minutes — you're blooming the chili in the fat, which deepens and intensifies the flavor and color. The oil should turn deep red-orange.

### 3. Add Kimchi

Add the kimchi pieces and stir to coat in the spiced pork fat. Cook 3-4 minutes, stirring occasionally. The kimchi should soften slightly and absorb the chili-fat flavor.

### 4. Add Liquid

Add the kimchi liquid, water (or broth), and soy sauce. Bring to a boil. Add sugar.

Taste the broth — it should be sour, spicy, and savory. Adjust: more kimchi liquid for acidity, more gochugaru for heat, more soy for saltiness.

### 5. Simmer

Reduce to a steady simmer. Cook 15-20 minutes uncovered. The broth will reduce slightly and intensify; the pork will become tender. The longer you simmer, the more mellow and integrated the flavor becomes.

### 6. Add Tofu

Add the tofu cubes gently (they will break if stirred aggressively). Simmer 5 more minutes until the tofu is heated through and has absorbed some of the broth color.

### 7. Finish

Add green onion and sesame seeds. Taste one final time. Serve immediately in the cooking pot (the dolsot stays hot at the table) or transfer to bowls.

**Serve with:** A bowl of rice alongside each serving. The rice and stew are eaten together — a spoonful of stew over rice, alternating bites. Not mixed.

## The Tuna Version (Chamchi Kimchi Jjigae)

When no pork is available: canned tuna (packed in water or oil) is the standard substitute. Drain the liquid, add the tuna at step 3 instead of pork. Skip the rendering step — start with sesame oil + garlic + gochugaru directly.

The result is lighter, faster (15 minutes total), and considerably less rich. This is the version for a quick weeknight meal when the pork jjigae version requires planning. Korean college students and single-person households often keep canned tuna specifically for this version.

---

## Storage and Reheating

Kimchi jjigae is one of the few dishes that is significantly better the next day. Overnight in the refrigerator, the flavors integrate further and the kimchi's sourness becomes rounder and more complex. Reheat gently on the stovetop, adding a splash of water if the stew has thickened.

It keeps refrigerated for 4-5 days. The pork remains tender; the kimchi continues to soften. Many Korean cooks consider day 2-3 kimchi jjigae the peak version.

## The Cultural Weight

Kimchi jjigae holds a specific place in Korean food culture: it is the dish made in every home, from the poorest to the wealthiest, and the experience of eating it is tied to family memory in a way that more elaborate Korean dishes are not. It is the meal a parent makes when you come home sick, the stew that fills the apartment with smell before a family gathering, the food associated with comfort specifically because it requires nothing except old kimchi and basic pantry items.

This is a quality shared by a handful of national dishes across cultures — the French have pot-au-feu, Italians have ribollita, Americans have chicken noodle soup. Kimchi jjigae is Korea's version: cheap, deeply flavored, inseparable from memory.
