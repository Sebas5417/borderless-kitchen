Rice is the center of Korean food culture. The Korean word for a cooked meal — *siksa* (식사) — implies rice. Asking "have you eaten?" (*bap meogeosseoyo?*, 밥 먹었어요?) literally translates as "have you eaten rice?" The word *bap* (밥) means both "cooked rice" and "meal" — the two are synonymous.

Getting Korean rice right matters not because it's difficult but because it's the foundation everything else is built around. Properly cooked Korean rice has a specific texture: slightly sticky (so it holds together when picked up with chopsticks), glistening with steam, soft throughout with no dry or hard centers.

This is how it's done.

---

## The Right Rice

Korean rice is short-grain japonica rice. This is not the same as:
- **Long-grain rice (jasmine, basmati):** Wrong starch structure; won't achieve the right texture
- **Chinese short-grain or medium-grain:** Close, but different variety
- **Japanese short-grain sushi rice:** Functionally identical — the same variety; Japanese and Korean short-grain are interchangeable

**What to buy:** Look for rice labeled "Korean rice," "japonica rice," or "sushi rice." Common Korean brands available internationally: Nishiki, Koshihikari, CJ Hetbahn (the vacuum-packed cooked rice brand for convenience), or any Korean supermarket house brand.

**Specific Korean varieties:**
- *Chuchong*: A high-quality Korean cultivar; slightly more expensive
- *Ilpum*: The standard premium Korean rice variety; excellent texture and aroma
- *Chucheong*: Another premium cultivar from Chungcheong province

For everyday cooking, any good-quality short-grain japonica rice produces excellent results.

---

## Washing: The Most Important Step

Korean rice must be washed before cooking. This is not optional and makes a measurable difference in the result.

**Why washing matters:**
- Removes surface starch that creates gummy, clumped, overly sticky results
- Removes dust, any residual milling debris
- The starch that remains after washing is the right amount for the desired texture — slightly sticky but not gluey

**How to wash:**

1. Place rice in a bowl or in the rice cooker pot
2. Add cold water, stir rice briskly (4-5 stirs), drain immediately — this first wash is the "rinse and release" pass
3. Repeat: add water, stir, drain — 3 to 5 times total, until the water runs mostly clear (not perfectly clear; some residual cloudiness is fine)
4. Final drain: drain as thoroughly as possible

The complete washing process takes 2-3 minutes and is one of the most reliable quality improvements in rice cooking.

---

## Soaking (Optional but Recommended)

After washing, soaking the drained rice in fresh cold water for 20-30 minutes allows the grains to hydrate evenly before cooking. This produces:
- More even cooking (no dry centers)
- Slightly more tender texture
- Better starch gelatinization

For quick everyday cooking, soaking can be skipped. For a special meal or when using an older rice that has dried out during storage, soaking noticeably improves the result.

---

## Water Ratio

The standard Korean rice water ratio: **1:1.1 to 1:1.2** (rice to water by volume).

This is slightly less water than Japanese sushi rice preparations (which typically use 1:1.1) and less than Western rice cooking conventions. The result is steamed rice, not boiled — the water is absorbed, not drained.

**Adjustments:**
- For older rice (stored over a year): add slightly more water (1:1.3)
- For very fresh rice (new harvest, *shinmai*): reduce slightly (1:1.05)
- Rice cooker vs. stovetop: rice cookers typically need slightly less water than stovetop (the sealed environment traps steam better)

**Measuring:** Most rice cooker pots have measurement lines on the inside. Use the rice cooker's cup (which is typically 180ml, not the standard 240ml Western cup) and the corresponding water line.

---

## Rice Cooker Method

The rice cooker (*bapsot*, 밥솥, or *jeongi bapsot*, 전기밥솥) is how almost all Korean households cook rice. A quality rice cooker is one of the most used appliances in Korean kitchens.

**How to use:**

1. Wash rice as described (in the rice cooker pot or a separate bowl)
2. Add washed rice to the rice cooker pot
3. Add water to the correct measurement line (or use the 1:1.1 ratio)
4. Let soak 20-30 minutes (optional)
5. Start the cooker
6. When done, do not open immediately — let the rice steam internally for 10 minutes with the lid closed
7. Open, and use the rice paddle (*jusgeok*, 주걱) to fold the rice gently — do not stir. Folding releases steam without breaking the grains. This is called *dum* (뜸) — the steam rest.

**The steam rest is not optional.** Opening the rice cooker immediately produces rice that is wet on top and dry below. The 10-minute rest redistributes moisture evenly throughout the pot.

---

## Stovetop Method (Pot Method)

For those without a rice cooker or cooking small quantities:

**Equipment needed:** A heavy-bottomed pot with a tight-fitting lid. A heavy pot distributes heat evenly; a loose lid loses steam.

**Method:**

1. Combine washed rice and water (1:1.1 ratio) in a heavy pot
2. Let soak 20-30 minutes if time allows
3. Bring to a boil over medium-high heat — watch carefully; do not cover until it begins to boil
4. When it reaches a full boil (you'll see steam puffing from the edge), reduce heat immediately to the lowest possible setting and cover tightly
5. Cook for 12-15 minutes — do not lift the lid during this time
6. Remove from heat completely; let rest with lid on for 10 minutes
7. Open and fold gently with a rice paddle

**Avoiding burnt rice:** The transition from boil to lowest heat must be immediate. Any delay after the water starts boiling creates burnt rice on the bottom. The first few times using the stovetop method, watch carefully at the boiling transition.

**The scorched bottom (*nurungji*):** A slight golden-brown crust on the bottom of the pot is not a failure — it's *nurungji* (누룽지), the beloved Korean scorched rice. Scrape it up, add water, and simmer briefly to make *sungnyung* (숭늉), the traditional post-meal rice tea. Or eat the crispy pieces directly as a snack.

## Dolsot (Stone Pot) Rice

For a special preparation, Korean rice cooked in a *dolsot* (돌솥, stone pot) is distinctive:

- Use the same 1:1.1 water ratio
- Cook on stovetop over medium heat until steaming
- The stone pot retains heat and continues cooking after removal from heat
- Creates an even more developed *nurungji* (scorched bottom crust)
- The pot goes directly to the table while still crackling

Dolsot rice requires care — the pot is extremely hot and retains heat for 20+ minutes. Handle with thick mitts.

## Common Problems and Solutions

**Mushy or gluey rice:** Too much water, or too much starch left from insufficient washing. Reduce water ratio by 1-2 tbsp; wash more thoroughly.

**Dry or hard centers:** Too little water, or not enough resting time. Add 1-2 tbsp more water; ensure full 10-minute steam rest.

**Top layer dry, bottom layer wet:** Insufficient resting time. Always rest 10 minutes before opening and folding.

**Stuck to the pot (not *nurungji*, but damaged grains):** Heat too high during the low simmer phase. Use the absolute lowest heat setting.

**Clumped, gummy rice:** Washed too little, or water ratio too high. The standard 1:1.1 with thorough washing (3-5 rinses) should prevent this.

---

## Storing and Reheating

**Same day storage:** Cover loosely with a clean cloth or leave in the rice cooker on "warm" setting (most rice cookers have a keep-warm mode that maintains safe temperature)

**Leftover rice:** Store in an airtight container in the refrigerator up to 3 days. Cold refrigerated rice is ideal for kimchi bokkeumbap (fried rice) — it stir-fries better than fresh rice.

**Reheating:** Add a splash of water (1-2 tbsp per cup of rice), cover tightly, and microwave 2-3 minutes. The added water restores steam and prevents dry, hard texture. Alternatively, reheat in a covered pan on very low heat.

**Freezing:** Cooked Korean rice freezes well. Portion into single-serving amounts while still warm, wrap in plastic, freeze. Reheat from frozen in microwave 2-3 minutes with a splash of water.

Korean rice is a daily practice, not a special occasion technique. The method described here is what Korean home cooks do every day — wash thoroughly, use the correct water ratio, rest properly before serving. Each step matters, and combined they produce the specific texture that makes Korean rice the correct foundation for bansang culture.
