Stock is the backbone of Western cooking and the most neglected item in most home kitchens. The difference between a dish made with good stock and one made without it is not subtle — it's the difference between something that tastes complete and something that tastes like it's missing a layer.

Making stock is not difficult. It's slow, and it requires you to have saved bones and vegetable scraps (or bought them), but the active work is minimal. The result is a liquid that costs almost nothing and improves almost everything.

---

## Stock vs. Broth

These terms are used interchangeably in everyday cooking, but there is a technical distinction:

**Stock** is made primarily from bones, with or without some meat attached. Bones release collagen during long cooking, which converts to gelatin and gives stock its body and mouthfeel. A properly made stock will set to a jelly when refrigerated.

**Broth** is made primarily from meat, which contributes more flavor but less gelatin. Broth is lighter-bodied and typically more savory than stock.

In practice: use stock for sauces and braises where body matters. Use broth when you want clean, direct flavor. Most "stock" recipes in home cooking produce something between the two.

## The Mirepoix Foundation

Nearly every Western stock begins with mirepoix: onion, carrot, and celery in a 2:1:1 ratio by weight. These three aromatics contribute sweetness, earthiness, and a background savory note that forms the flavor base.

**Standard mirepoix:** 2 parts onion : 1 part carrot : 1 part celery

For a 4-liter batch of stock: roughly 200g onion, 100g carrot, 100g celery. Halve them roughly — you don't need to peel carrots or celery, just wash them. The goal is surface area, not presentation.

**For darker stocks (brown stock, demi-glace):** roast the mirepoix alongside the bones before adding to the pot. The caramelization adds color and depth.

## The Four Core Stocks

### Chicken Stock

The most versatile. Used in soups, risottos, pan sauces, braises, and grains.

**Bones:** 2–3 lbs chicken carcasses, backs, necks, or feet (feet are high in collagen — excellent for gelatinous stock). A cooked carcass from a roast chicken works well.

**Method:**
1. Optional but recommended: rinse the bones and blanch them — cover with cold water, bring to a boil, drain, and rinse. This removes blood and impurities that would cloud the stock and create foam.
2. Combine bones, mirepoix, a bouquet garni (parsley stems, thyme, bay leaf, peppercorns tied in cheesecloth or bundled), and enough cold water to cover by 2 inches.
3. Bring to a bare simmer over medium heat — never a boil. Skim the foam and fat that rises in the first 20–30 minutes.
4. Maintain a very gentle simmer (occasional bubble, not rolling) for 3–4 hours.
5. Strain through a fine-mesh strainer. Do not press the solids — this clouds the stock.
6. Cool rapidly (ice bath), refrigerate, skim the solidified fat from the top.

Yield: approximately 2–3 liters from a 4-liter pot of bones and water.

### Beef Stock / Brown Stock

Deeper, more complex, and better for braises, red wine sauces, and soups.

**Key difference:** roast everything first.

1. Roast bones at 425°F (220°C) for 45–60 minutes until deep mahogany brown, turning once.
2. Roast mirepoix in the same pan during the last 20 minutes.
3. Transfer to stockpot. Deglaze the roasting pan with water or wine and add to pot.
4. Add tomato paste (2 tbsp) — it adds color and rounds the flavor. Cook the tomato paste briefly in the roasting pan before deglazing.
5. Cover with cold water, bring to bare simmer, skim.
6. Cook 6–8 hours (or overnight in a low oven at 200°F / 93°C).

The result will be deeply colored and, when reduced further, becomes the basis for demi-glace.

### Vegetable Stock

Faster (30–45 minutes), and better when the liquid itself should taste of vegetables rather than meat.

Mirepoix plus: mushrooms or mushroom stems, leek greens, parsley stems, tomato, fennel (optional), garlic. Avoid: brassicas (cabbage, broccoli, Brussels sprouts) — they make stock bitter and sulfurous. Beets turn it purple.

Bring to a simmer and cook 30–45 minutes — longer doesn't improve vegetable stock the way it does meat stocks. Strain.

### Fish Stock (Fumet)

The fastest stock — overcooked fish bones become bitter.

Use fish frames, heads (gills removed), or shells from shrimp/lobster. Sweat mirepoix briefly in butter, add fish bones and cold white wine, cover with cold water. Simmer 20–25 minutes only. Strain. Fish stock is the base for classic fish sauces and seafood risotto.

## The Gelatin Test

A properly made meat stock, when refrigerated, should set to a semi-firm gel. This gelatin — converted from collagen in the bones — is what gives stock its body and what allows pan sauces made with it to reduce to a glossy, clinging consistency.

To test: refrigerate a small container after cooling. If it gels, the stock is well-made. If it remains completely liquid, either:
- Not enough collagen-rich bones (add feet, knuckles, or more carcass)
- Cooked at too high a temperature (boiling emulsifies fat and clouds the liquid but doesn't extract more gelatin)
- Not cooked long enough

## Storage and Reduction

**Refrigerator:** 3–5 days. Fat solidifies on top and acts as a seal; don't remove until ready to use.

**Freezer:** 3–6 months. Freeze in 1-cup and 2-cup portions (ice cube trays work for smaller amounts). Stock freezes solid and keeps well.

**Glace de viande:** Reduce beef stock by 75–80% until syrupy. This concentrate has an intense flavor and keeps in the refrigerator for months. A spoonful stirred into any sauce or braise is a significant flavor upgrade.

---

## Using Stock as an Ingredient

The upgrade stock provides is most dramatic in:

**Pan sauces:** Deglaze a pan after searing meat with wine, add stock, reduce until slightly syrupy. This is the core of French cooking. Without stock, the sauce is thin and one-dimensional; with it, the sauce is glossy and complex.

**Risotto:** Each addition of stock builds layers of flavor as the rice absorbs it. Stock quality is the primary variable in risotto quality.

**Braises:** The braising liquid becomes the sauce; stock is its backbone.

**Grain cooking:** Cook rice, farro, or barley in stock instead of water — the grain absorbs the flavor as it cooks.

**Soups:** The difference between soup made with homemade stock and soup made with water or carton stock is immediately apparent. Homemade stock has depth that commercial products approximate but don't replicate.

---

The most important thing about making stock is simply making it. Once you have stock in the freezer — even in small quantities — you'll find yourself using it constantly. The habit of saving carcasses, vegetable scraps, and bones turns something that would have been waste into the most useful thing in your kitchen.
