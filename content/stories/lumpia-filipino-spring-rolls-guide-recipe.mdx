Lumpia arrived in the Philippines with Chinese immigrants centuries ago — the name comes from the Fujian Chinese "lun pia" (soft spring roll). Filipino cooks transformed the Chinese spring roll by using local ingredients (ground pork, singkamas/jicama, garlic), developing thinner, crispier wrappers, and creating the distinctly Filipino sweet-tangy dipping sauce combination of vinegar and banana ketchup.

The Chinese origin is also why the most iconic variety is called "lumpia Shanghai" — named to reflect its Chinese-immigrant identity — despite having no particular connection to the city of Shanghai. The labeling reflects Filipino experience with Chinese immigration, not culinary geography.

## The Main Varieties

**Lumpia Shanghai:** The thin, crispy fried variety. Ground pork filling with carrots, onion, and garlic, rolled in thin lumpia wrappers and deep-fried until very crispy. These are typically 8–10cm long, thin (about 1.5cm diameter) — bite-sized. This is the party staple.

**Lumpiang gulay (vegetable lumpia):** Same technique, meatless filling — tofu, carrots, bean sprouts, cabbage.

**Lumpiang sariwa (fresh lumpia):** Completely different dish, not fried. Soft, crepe-like wrapper filled with heart of palm, shrimp, tofu, carrots, and bean sprouts. Topped with sweet-savory peanut sauce. Closer to Vietnamese fresh spring roll in concept.

**Lumpiang ubod:** Fresh lumpia with heart of palm as the main filling. A Batangas specialty.

## The Wrappers

Filipino lumpia wrappers are thinner and more delicate than Chinese egg roll wrappers:

**Lumpia Shanghai wrappers:** Very thin, almost translucent — available frozen at Asian grocery stores. Look for "spring roll wrappers" (Vietnamese or Filipino style) rather than the thicker "egg roll wrappers" sold at mainstream supermarkets. Thicker wrappers produce a doughier, heavier lumpia.

**If unavailable:** Thin rice paper spring roll wrappers (rehydrated briefly) can substitute for fresh sariwa. For fried lumpia, Chinese spring roll wrappers are the best substitute.

## The Dipping Sauces

**Spiced vinegar:** White cane vinegar with garlic and sliced bird's eye chili. The standard sauce for fried lumpia — the acid cuts the oil.

**Banana ketchup:** Filipino banana ketchup (not tomato ketchup) — sweet, slightly spiced, bright orange-red. Common alongside or in place of vinegar.

**Peanut sauce (for sariwa):** Crushed peanuts, brown sugar, garlic, soy sauce cooked to a thick, pourable sauce — poured directly over the fresh lumpia.

**Recipe: Lumpia Shanghai (Makes approximately 40 small rolls)**

**Filling:**
- 500g ground pork
- 1 medium carrot, peeled and finely grated
- 1 medium onion, finely minced
- 4 cloves garlic, minced
- 2 tablespoons soy sauce
- 1/2 teaspoon black pepper
- 1 egg (for binding)
- 1 teaspoon sesame oil

**Assembly:**
- 1 package thin lumpia wrappers (Filipino spring roll wrappers), thawed if frozen
- 1 egg, beaten (for sealing)
- Vegetable oil for deep frying

**Dipping sauce:**
- 120ml white cane vinegar or white rice vinegar
- 3 cloves garlic, crushed
- 2 bird's eye chilies, sliced (optional)
- Salt to taste

**Method:**

1. **Filling:** Mix all filling ingredients together until well combined. (Don't overmix or the filling becomes dense.) Refrigerate 30 minutes — cold filling is easier to roll.

2. **Roll:** Separate lumpia wrappers carefully (they tear easily when dry — work quickly). Place wrapper on a clean surface. Add 1 tablespoon of filling in a line near the bottom of the wrapper. Roll tightly, like a small cigar, folding in the sides after the first roll. Seal the edge with beaten egg. The rolls should be tight and thin — about 1.5cm diameter.

3. **Fry:** Heat oil in a deep pot to 175°C. Fry rolls in batches (don't crowd — they will stick), turning occasionally, until deep golden brown and crispy, about 5–6 minutes. Drain on paper towels.

4. **Dipping sauce:** Mix vinegar, garlic, and chili. Season with salt.

5. Serve immediately — lumpia loses its crispiness within 15–20 minutes of frying.

*Lumpia can be rolled in advance and frozen raw (unfried) for up to 2 months. Fry directly from frozen — add 2 minutes to frying time.*
