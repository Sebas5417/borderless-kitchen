A knife blade separates food along a path determined by the blade's geometry and sharpness. When a blade is sharp, it displaces and severs cells cleanly. When it is dull, it tears and crushes cells — the mechanical damage ruptures more cell walls than a sharp blade would, releasing more liquid, causing faster oxidation, and producing a bruised texture at the cut surface.

This distinction is most visible with delicate foods. A sharp knife through fresh herbs produces vibrant, intact ribbons; a dull knife bruises the same herbs, releasing the pigments and enzymes that cause immediate browning. The same applies to fish, soft vegetables, and bread. Sharpness is not a preference — it is the mechanism by which cutting works correctly.

## Blade Geometry and Knife Selection

A chef's knife (French knife or Gyuto) is the single most important kitchen tool. The blade is typically 8–10 inches long, curved from heel to tip, with a bolster (the thick junction between blade and handle) that provides balance. The curve is the design: it allows a rocking motion where the tip stays in contact with the board while the heel lifts and falls — efficient for mincing and chopping.

**German vs. Japanese geometry:**
- **German knives** (Wüsthof, Henckels): thicker spine, more curved blade, 15–20° bevel angle per side. More robust — tolerates harder use and lateral forces. Better for tasks involving bone, squash, or applications where the blade might flex or hit hard surfaces.
- **Japanese knives** (Shun, Global, MAC, hand-forged): thinner spine, flatter edge with less curve, 10–15° bevel angle (sometimes single bevel). Sharper edge, more brittle — better for delicate precision work on vegetables and boneless proteins. Chips more easily if used inappropriately.

**Other knives and their purposes:**
- **Paring knife (3–4 inch):** small control tasks — peeling, trimming, turning vegetables, tasks where the food is held in the hand
- **Bread knife (serrated, 8–10 inch):** the serrations tear through crust without compressing the soft interior; do not use a bread knife on soft proteins or vegetables
- **Boning knife (6 inch, flexible):** follows the contours of bones and joints; required for fabricating whole animals
- **Slicing knife (12 inch, narrow):** long single-stroke cuts through roasts, whole fish, smoked meats; the narrow blade minimizes resistance

## The Physics of Sharpening

A knife edge is a wedge. The acuity (sharpness) of that wedge is determined by the angle at which the two beveled sides meet. A narrower angle is sharper but more fragile; a wider angle is more durable but less acute.

**Honing vs. sharpening — two different operations:**
- **Honing** (steel or ceramic rod): realigns the edge. With use, the fine edge of a blade folds or rolls microscopically — the steel is still there but bent out of alignment. Honing pushes it back. Takes 10–15 seconds. Should be done before every heavy use session.
- **Sharpening** (whetstone, electric sharpener): removes metal to create a new edge. Necessary when honing no longer restores cutting performance. Frequency depends on use — a professional might sharpen weekly; a home cook might sharpen 2–4 times per year.

**Whetstone sharpening fundamentals:**
- Grit progression: start with a coarse grit (120–400) if the edge is damaged; move to medium (800–1200) to refine; finish with fine (2000–6000+) to polish
- Angle: maintain a consistent angle (15–20° for most knives) through the entire stroke
- The burr: as metal is removed from one side, a thin wire of metal (the burr) forms on the opposite side. When you can feel the burr along the entire edge, flip and repeat on the other side. Remove the final burr with alternating light strokes.
- The paper test: a properly sharpened knife slices through a sheet of paper cleanly without tearing

## Grip and Body Mechanics

**The pinch grip:** the most important technique correction for most home cooks. Instead of wrapping all fingers around the handle, pinch the blade itself between thumb and the side of the index finger, just ahead of the bolster. The remaining fingers wrap the handle. This provides:
- Direct control over the blade angle
- Better feedback from the cut
- Keeps fingers naturally away from the edge

**The claw grip:** the guiding hand (the one holding the food) curls fingers under, knuckles forward, fingertips tucked back. The flat of the blade rests against the knuckles, using them as a guide. The knife cannot hit fingertips because they are behind the knuckles.

**Body mechanics:** stand square to the cutting board, not at an angle. The dominant shoulder should be directly over the knife. Weight transferred forward into each cut, not just arm motion.

## Fundamental Cuts and Their Dimensions

**Brunoise:** 1/8 inch (3mm) cube. The finest standard dice. Used as a garnish, in delicate sauces, or when very small pieces are needed for even cooking.

**Small dice:** 1/4 inch (6mm) cube. Standard for aromatics in quick-cooked dishes.

**Medium dice:** 1/2 inch (12mm) cube. Most common dice size for sautéed vegetables, soups, stews.

**Large dice:** 3/4 inch (20mm) cube. For roasted vegetables, stews, and preparations where the piece needs to hold up through long cooking.

**Julienne:** 1/8 inch × 1/8 inch × 2 inch matchsticks. Used for stir-fries, garnishes, raw salads. The precision is necessary for even cooking — irregular matchsticks cook unevenly.

**Chiffonade:** fine ribbons of leafy herbs or greens. Stack leaves, roll tightly, slice across the roll into thin ribbons. The tighter the roll, the thinner and more even the ribbons. Used for herb garnishes, basil, mint, larger leafy greens.

**Mince:** very fine, irregular pieces — smaller than brunoise but without the requirement of uniformity. Used for garlic, ginger, shallots, and herbs where they will dissolve into a dish. The knife rocks rapidly with the tip anchored.

**How to produce a uniform dice:**
1. Square the vegetable: cut one flat side to stabilize it
2. Slice into planks of your target thickness
3. Stack planks and cut into sticks (batons) of the same width
4. Cut across the sticks into cubes

## Onion Anatomy and the Radial Technique

An onion is organized in concentric layers around a root end. The radial cut technique uses this structure to produce a uniform dice without the onion falling apart during cutting:

1. Halve the onion through the root end (not across the equator) — the root holds the layers together
2. Peel
3. Make horizontal cuts parallel to the board, stopping just before the root (don't cut through it)
4. Make vertical cuts toward the root, fanning out radially (not parallel to each other) — again, stop before the root
5. Slice across the onion: each slice produces cubes as the grid you've created falls apart

The root end is discarded. The radial cuts account for the onion's natural curve; straight parallel cuts would produce irregular pieces.

## Garlic: Two Techniques and Their Results

**Sliced garlic:** thin slices, most cell integrity preserved. Produces a mild, more textured garlic flavor in the finished dish. For dishes where you want to see and taste garlic pieces.

**Minced garlic:** more cell damage, more allicin released. Stronger, more pungent flavor. For dishes where garlic should dissolve and flavor the oil.

**Crushed garlic:** pressing with the flat of the blade ruptures the cells completely. Releases the most allicin. Skin slips off easily. For dishes where maximum garlic intensity is needed, or where you want to infuse oil with garlic before removing it.

**Grated garlic (microplane):** essentially liquefied garlic with maximum cell destruction. Even more intense than minced. Used in marinades, dressings, and any application where no texture is desired.

## Cutting Board Material and Stability

The cutting board is as important as the knife. The board should be:
- **Large enough:** at least 12×18 inches for home use. Cramped boards produce sloppy, unsafe cutting.
- **Stable:** a damp kitchen towel under the board prevents sliding — critical for safety.
- **The right material:** wood and plastic are both appropriate. Wood is gentler on edges (the wood fibers yield slightly; hard surfaces like glass or ceramic damage edges). Plastic is non-porous and dishwasher safe for raw meat. Glass, ceramic, and marble boards damage edges and should not be used for cutting.

Sharp knife, stable board, correct grip, consistent technique: these four variables determine the quality of everything that happens at the cutting board, which is the foundation of everything that happens at the stove.
