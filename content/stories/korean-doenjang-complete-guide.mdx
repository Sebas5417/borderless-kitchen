Doenjang (된장) and Japanese miso are both fermented soybean pastes. They look similar. They serve similar culinary functions. But they taste meaningfully different — and understanding why reveals something important about the different fermentation philosophies of Japanese and Korean cooking.

## What Doenjang Is

Doenjang is made from *meju* — a specific Korean fermentation starter that is not found in Japanese miso production.

**Meju production:**
1. Soybeans are boiled until soft and mashed into blocks
2. The blocks are left to dry in autumn (traditionally hanging in the rafters)
3. Wild mold, bacteria, and yeast from the environment colonize the blocks over 1-3 months
4. The colonized blocks are washed and packed in salt brine in earthenware *onggi* jars
5. Fermentation continues for months to years

During fermentation in the brine, the liquid that rises to the top becomes *hansik ganjang* (traditional soy sauce). The solid paste remaining at the bottom becomes *doenjang*.

## Why It Tastes Different From Miso

**Miso:** Uses controlled pure-culture *Aspergillus oryzae* (koji mold) to initiate fermentation. Predictable, consistent, cleaner.

**Doenjang:** Uses the wild microbiome of meju — dozens of mold species, bacteria, and yeasts that colonized the blocks from the environment. The specific organisms include *Bacillus subtilis* (also responsible for natto), various *Aspergillus* species, and lactic acid bacteria. This wild diversity produces more variable, more complex, and more intensely pungent fermentation products.

The result: doenjang is funkier, earthier, slightly bitter, more assertive. Some describe it as having a barnyard quality that miso lacks. This is not a defect — it's the character of wild fermentation.

## Commercial vs Traditional Doenjang

**Traditional doenjang** (*jaerae doenjang*): Made from meju, brined, aged 2-3 years minimum. Chunky texture with visible soybean pieces. Intensely pungent. Less common commercially — found at specialty Korean food purveyors.

**Commercial doenjang** (*gaeryang doenjang*): Made using koji inoculation (similar to Japanese miso production) rather than traditional meju, for faster and more consistent results. The most common type in Korean supermarkets. Less funky than traditional, more consistent, milder.

**Chungjang/Samjang:** Doenjang blended with gochujang and sesame oil — a pre-made version of ssamjang paste. Not plain doenjang.

## Applications in Korean Cooking

**Doenjang jjigae (된장찌개 — Soybean Paste Stew):**
Korea's equivalent of Japanese miso soup but much more substantial. Anchovy-kelp broth, doenjang dissolved in separately (not added to the pot directly), tofu, zucchini, mushrooms, potatoes. The doenjang is the main flavoring, not a background note — typically 2-3 tablespoons per small pot.

**Doenjang gui (된장구이 — Doenjang-Grilled Proteins):**
Doenjang used as a marinade/glaze for grilled meats, especially at Korean BBQ. Doenjang + sesame oil + garlic applied to pork belly before grilling. Caramelizes and darkens on the grill.

**Ssamjang (쌈장):**
The standard Korean BBQ wrap paste — doenjang + gochujang + sesame oil + garlic + scallion + sugar. The doenjang provides the fermented base; the gochujang adds heat and sweetness.

**Raw as condiment:**
Small amounts eaten directly with rice, or as a dipping paste for fresh vegetables (cucumbers, raw daikon). This directness is unusual among fermented soybean products — Japanese miso is rarely eaten raw without dilution.

## Substitution

When doenjang is unavailable: red miso (hatcho or sendai) is the closest Japanese substitute. The character will be less funky but the fermented depth is comparable. Use slightly less miso than the recipe calls for doenjang — miso is less intense.

---

Doenjang is the foundational fermented paste of Korean cooking in the way that miso is foundational to Japanese cooking — but the specific microbial history of its production gives it a character that miso cannot replicate, and vice versa. Using one for the other is possible but always produces a recognizably different result.
