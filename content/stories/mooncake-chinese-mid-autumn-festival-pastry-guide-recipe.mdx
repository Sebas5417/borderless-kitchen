The salted egg yolk in the center of a mooncake is the most elegant symbol in Chinese pastry: a circular egg yolk, cured in salt until bright orange and firm, nestled in the lotus paste filling, surrounded by pastry — so that when the mooncake is cut in half, the yolk appears centered, glowing orange, perfectly round, exactly as the full moon appears in the night sky on the 15th day of the eighth lunar month. The symbolism is complete and visual and unavoidable.

The Mid-Autumn Festival has been celebrated for over a thousand years; the mooncake as the specific festival food dates to at least the Song dynasty, with the gift-exchange tradition becoming particularly elaborate during the Tang dynasty. The story most often told is of the moon goddess Chang'e (嫦娥), who drank an immortality elixir and ascended to the moon; the mooncake celebration includes looking at the moon together, eating mooncake, and thinking of absent family members.

The social dimension of mooncakes is now as important as the culinary one. Premium mooncakes from established brands — Maxim's in Hong Kong, Peninsula Hotel, luxury brands from Shanghai and Beijing — are sold in elaborate metal gift tins that are kept as storage boxes for years afterward. The price of premium mooncakes has become a mild cultural anxiety (they have become extremely expensive) and a point of affectionate irony among Chinese communities.

## The Traditional Baked Mooncake: Components

**The pastry shell (golden syrup pastry):**
Unlike standard shortcrust or puff pastry, the traditional mooncake shell is made from:
- All-purpose flour
- Golden syrup (or treacle) — provides the amber color, keeps the pastry soft and pliable
- Alkaline water (*kansui* — lye water, a solution of potassium carbonate) — gives the pastry its golden color when baked, similar to the alkaline in ramen noodles or pretzels
- Neutral oil

The dough is mixed and rested for at least 2 hours (some cooks rest overnight) before rolling.

**The filling:**
- **Lotus seed paste (*lián róng*):** The prestige filling — made from dried lotus seeds cooked until soft, then ground and cooked with sugar and oil into a smooth, dense paste. Expensive and labor-intensive. Available pre-made at Asian grocery stores.
- **Red bean paste (dòushā):** More common and less expensive than lotus paste; slightly grainier texture.
- **Five kernel filling:** Mixed nuts and seeds (pine nuts, melon seeds, sesame, pumpkin seeds) bound with syrup — the oldest traditional version, increasingly old-fashioned.

**The salted egg yolks:**
- Raw duck eggs salted in brine for 3–4 weeks or coated in a salt-clay paste; the yolks separate and cook when baked inside the mooncake, becoming bright orange and slightly firm with a characteristic savory-rich flavor that contrasts with the sweet filling.

## The Stamp Mold

Traditional mooncakes are formed in a wooden stamp mold — a carved wooden block with an intricate design (flowers, Chinese characters meaning 'longevity' or 'double happiness', the bakery name). The filled dough ball is pressed into the mold; the mold is then inverted and the molded mooncake dropped out. Modern plastic plunger-style molds make the process accessible for home cooks.

---

## The Snowskin Mooncake (*Bīng Pí Yuèbǐng*)

A modern (1980s Hong Kong origin) version that requires no baking:
- **Skin:** Cooked glutinous rice flour (*gāo fěn*, baked flour) mixed with icing sugar, shortening, and sometimes food coloring or pandan juice; forms a soft, mochi-like shell
- **Filling:** Same lotus or red bean paste, often with more delicate flavors (green tea, taro, champagne truffle)
- **Served chilled** — must be refrigerated; does not keep at room temperature

## The Complete Recipe (Baked Mooncakes)

**Makes:** 8 mooncakes (70–80g each) | **Time:** 2–3 days (including rest and filling prep if homemade)

### Pastry
- 200g all-purpose flour
- 130g golden syrup (or light corn syrup + a small amount of molasses)
- 50g neutral oil
- ½ teaspoon alkaline water (*kansui*) or ½ teaspoon baking soda dissolved in 1 tablespoon water (imperfect substitute)

### Filling (use pre-made, or see below)
- 500g lotus seed paste or red bean paste (store-bought)
- 4–8 salted duck egg yolks (1 per mooncake, or 1 per two mooncakes)

### Egg Wash
- 1 egg yolk + 1 tablespoon water, mixed

### Method

**1. Mix pastry dough:** Combine golden syrup, oil, and alkaline water; stir. Add flour; stir until a smooth dough forms. Do not overwork. Wrap; rest at room temperature 2 hours (or overnight).

**2. Prepare filling:** Divide lotus paste into 8 portions (approximately 50g each). Wrap one salted egg yolk in each portion, encasing completely.

**3. Divide dough:** Divide rested dough into 8 portions (approximately 30g each).

**4. Assemble:** Flatten one portion of dough into a thin disc; place a filling ball in the center. Wrap dough around filling, pinching and sealing to completely enclose. Roll into a smooth ball.

**5. Mold:** Dust mold lightly with flour; place the dough ball in the mold; press firmly. Invert the mold onto a parchment-lined baking sheet; press the plunger to release the molded mooncake.

**6. Bake:** Preheat oven to 180°C. Bake mooncakes 10 minutes. Remove; cool 5 minutes. Brush lightly with egg wash (very thin layer — too much will hide the mold pattern). Return to oven; bake 10–12 minutes more until golden.

**7. Rest:** Allow to cool completely. The pastry needs 1–2 days to 'return oil' (*huí yóu*) — the oil from the filling migrates into the pastry, softening and shining it. Fresh-baked mooncakes are drier; day-two mooncakes are better.
