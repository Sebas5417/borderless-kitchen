Wales is a country of hill farming and wet weather, and cawl is a dish built for both. The hills provide Welsh lamb — bred on the steep, rocky hillsides, the grass salt-influenced by Atlantic winds in coastal regions — and the weather provides both the reason to want a hot bowl of broth and the leek and swede that grow reliably in the Welsh climate.

The dish has been recorded in Welsh literature since at least the 13th century (the Mabinogion mentions a cawl broth). It predates systematic recipe writing; it was a living tradition passed by demonstration and family memory. Every region of Wales makes a slightly different cawl: coastal regions add more leek; some families use all lamb, others mix lamb and beef; swede (called turnip in parts of Wales) might be replaced with parsnip; bay leaf and thyme versus dried mixed herbs.

The two-day method is the most distinctive aspect: cooking the day before, cooling overnight, removing the solidified fat layer from the surface, and reheating to serve. This produces a cleaner, less fatty broth than a same-day preparation. It is also practical — a cold Welsh kitchen overnight achieves what a refrigerator does today.

## The Lamb

Welsh salt marsh lamb (particularly from Gower and the Llyn Peninsula) is the ideal. The lamb from those areas is distinctive in flavor from the mineral-rich saltmarsh pasture.

**The cut:** Bone-in neck, shoulder, or leg pieces, or a combination. The bones are the soul of the broth; boneless lamb produces a thin, less satisfying cawl. Welsh lamb neck fillet is ideal: fatty, flavorful, and cheap.

## The Vegetables

In cawl, vegetables are added in stages by cooking time required:
- Long-cooking: swede (rutabaga), carrot, parsnip — added early
- Medium: potato — added midway
- Short: leek — added in the last 30 minutes to retain its freshness

The leek is the national emblem of Wales; in cawl it is not an optional ingredient.

**Recipe: Cawl (Serves 6–8)**

- 1.5 kg bone-in Welsh lamb (neck or shoulder pieces)
- 1 large swede (rutabaga), peeled and cut in large chunks
- 3 carrots, cut in rounds
- 2 parsnips, cut in chunks
- 4 medium potatoes, peeled and quartered
- 4 large leeks, cleaned, sliced into 3cm rounds (white and light green parts)
- 2 onions, quartered
- 3 bay leaves
- 3 sprigs fresh thyme
- Salt and black pepper
- 2.5–3 liters water

**To serve:**
- Fresh crusty bread
- Grated Welsh Caerphilly cheese (optional — some serve it alongside)

**Day 1 — cooking:**

1. Place lamb pieces in a large pot. Cover with cold water. Bring to a boil; skim foam vigorously until clear (5–10 minutes). This is critical for a clear broth.

2. Add onions, bay leaves, thyme, salt, and pepper. Reduce to a gentle simmer. Cook partially covered 1.5 hours.

3. Add swede, carrot, and parsnip. Continue simmering 30 minutes.

4. Add potato. Simmer 20 more minutes until potatoes are tender.

5. Cool; refrigerate overnight.

**Day 2 — finishing:**

1. Remove the solidified fat layer from the surface.

2. Gently reheat the cawl over medium heat.

3. Add leeks in the final 20–25 minutes of reheating; they should be tender but retain some texture.

4. Taste and adjust salt.

5. Serve as two courses traditionally: first the broth with a little bread; then the vegetables and meat removed from the bone. Or serve combined in deep bowls with bread alongside.

*Traditional Welsh service: the broth is drunk from a wooden bowl (cawl bowl); the meat and vegetables are eaten with a wooden spoon. The proper wooden cawl spoon is a Welsh artisan tradition still active today.*
