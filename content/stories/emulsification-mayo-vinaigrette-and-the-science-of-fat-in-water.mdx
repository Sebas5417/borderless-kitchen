Oil and water are immiscible — their molecular structures repel each other. Shake them together in a jar, and they form temporary droplets that immediately separate back into distinct layers. Add an emulsifier, and the droplets stay suspended indefinitely.

An emulsifier is a molecule with one end that is attracted to water (hydrophilic) and one end attracted to fat (hydrophobic). It positions itself at the interface between oil droplets and the surrounding water, stabilizing the droplets in suspension. The result is an emulsion — a homogeneous mixture of two liquids that would not normally combine.

Cooking produces emulsions constantly: mayonnaise, hollandaise, vinaigrette, pan sauces finished with butter, cream soups, chocolate ganache, beurre blanc. Each uses a different emulsifier or a different proportion of oil to water.

---

## The Two Types of Emulsion

**Oil-in-water emulsion:** Oil droplets dispersed in a water-based liquid. Mayonnaise is oil dispersed in the water phase of egg yolk. Hollandaise is clarified butter dispersed in a water/egg yolk base. Most cream sauces are oil-in-water.

**Water-in-oil emulsion:** Water droplets dispersed in oil or fat. Butter is a water-in-oil emulsion — tiny water droplets suspended in butterfat. Vinaigrette can exist in this form if made with a high ratio of oil to vinegar.

The distinction matters because oil-in-water emulsions behave and look different from water-in-oil: oil-in-water is usually lighter in color and pourable (mayonnaise, sauce); water-in-oil tends to be richer and more cohesive (butter).

## Emulsifiers in Cooking

**Lecithin (from egg yolks):** The most important emulsifier in cooking. Egg yolks contain lecithin in abundance — it is responsible for the stability of mayonnaise, hollandaise, and crème anglaise. One egg yolk can emulsify up to a cup of oil in mayonnaise.

**Mustard:** Contains mucilage — complex polysaccharides that act as emulsifiers. A small amount of Dijon mustard added to a vinaigrette significantly extends how long it stays emulsified. It also adds flavor.

**Proteins:** Milk and cream contain proteins (casein, whey) that act as emulsifiers, stabilizing the fat droplets in dairy-based sauces. This is why cream-based sauces are more stable than butter-based ones — the dairy proteins stabilize the emulsion.

**Starch:** Cooked starch (from a roux or added potato) thickens a sauce and stabilizes fat droplets mechanically — the thick liquid makes it harder for droplets to coalesce.

## Mayonnaise: The Stable Emulsion

Mayonnaise is an oil-in-water emulsion stabilized by egg yolk lecithin. The ratio of oil to water-based ingredients (egg yolk + lemon juice) is extremely high — roughly 75–80% oil by volume — making it one of the most concentrated food emulsions.

**Method:**
1. Whisk together egg yolk, a small amount of lemon juice or vinegar, salt, and mustard
2. Add oil drop by drop at first, whisking constantly. Each tiny drop is surrounded by lecithin molecules and incorporated into the water phase
3. As the emulsion becomes established (after the first few tablespoons of oil), the remaining oil can be added in a thin, steady stream
4. Season and adjust acidity

**Why drop by drop at the start:** Adding too much oil too quickly overwhelms the available lecithin before it can surround the droplets. The oil droplets coalesce into large pools instead of staying dispersed — the emulsion breaks.

**Why it breaks:** Over-addition of oil, too little emulsifier, or the ingredients being too cold (fat becomes too viscous to disperse into fine droplets). Fix a broken mayo by starting with a fresh yolk in a clean bowl and whisking the broken mayo into it drop by drop — the new yolk has enough fresh lecithin to re-emulsify.

**Room temperature matters:** Cold eggs and cold oil both impede emulsification. The egg yolk lecithin is less mobile when cold, and the oil is more viscous. Use ingredients at room temperature for a more stable, faster emulsion.

## Vinaigrette: The Temporary Emulsion

A vinaigrette is a temporary emulsion — it will separate if left to stand, but can be re-emulsified by shaking. The standard ratio is 3 parts oil to 1 part acid (vinegar or citrus juice), though this is adjustable based on the sharpness of the acid and personal preference.

Without an emulsifier (pure oil + vinegar), the emulsion lasts only as long as the physical energy of shaking keeps the droplets dispersed — usually seconds to minutes.

**With mustard:** Add ½ teaspoon of Dijon to the vinegar before whisking in the oil. The mustard's mucilage stabilizes the droplets for significantly longer — a well-made mustard vinaigrette can hold for 30–60 minutes before separating.

**With garlic:** Minced or pasted garlic contains saponins that also contribute emulsifying properties. A vinaigrette made by whisking oil into crushed garlic and vinegar is more stable than one without.

**Emulsified vinaigrette (Caesar-style):** Adding egg yolk to a vinaigrette base (as in a classic Caesar dressing) produces a much more stable emulsion that approaches the consistency of a thin mayonnaise.

## Pan Sauces: The Butter Emulsion

Finishing a pan sauce with cold butter (monter au beurre) creates a temporary fat-in-water emulsion. The butter melts gradually as it is swirled into the hot liquid, releasing fat droplets and milk proteins that disperse into the sauce. The result is a glossy, slightly thickened sauce with a silky mouthfeel.

**Why it breaks:** If the sauce gets too hot after butter is added (above about 160°F / 71°C), the emulsion breaks and the fat separates into greasy pools. Serve immediately after mounting with butter, and keep the sauce warm rather than hot.

**Beurre blanc:** A classic French butter sauce — shallots and wine reduced to almost nothing, then cold butter whisked in piece by piece off the heat. The sauce holds because the butter is added into a small amount of hot liquid (just enough to keep it above the melting point of butter) while being whisked constantly. It is fragile — it will break if overheated or left to stand too long.

---

## The Rule Across All Emulsions

Every stable emulsion requires the same three elements:
1. A sufficient emulsifier (enough lecithin, mustard, or protein)
2. Mechanical energy (whisking, blending) to break the oil into fine droplets
3. The right ratio of oil to water phase — too much oil overwhelms the emulsifier

When any of these is insufficient, the emulsion breaks. Understanding which element failed tells you how to fix it.
