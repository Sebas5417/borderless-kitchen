Nochebuena in Puerto Rico means pernil. The preparation begins the night before, sometimes two nights before — the pork shoulder scored, massaged with garlic and spices, and left to absorb flavor in the refrigerator. On Christmas Eve, it goes into the oven in the morning and cooks all day, filling the house with the smell of roasting pork and garlic. The family gathers around it.

Pernil is technically simple but emotionally complex — it is the dish that every Puerto Rican family believes they make best, and every family's version has minor differences that are fiercely defended. The constant is the overnight marinade, the pork shoulder (not loin, not tenderloin — the shoulder has enough fat to survive hours in the oven), and the cuero.

## The Cuero

The cuero is the skin — and getting it right is the point of difficulty in making pernil. Properly done cuero is shatteringly crispy, almost like a pork rind, crackling audibly when you cut through it. Improperly done cuero is soft, rubbery, and disappointing.

Two things create great cuero:

**Dry skin:** The skin must be completely dry before the high-heat finish. Pat with paper towels. Some cooks leave the scored pernil uncovered in the refrigerator overnight — the refrigerator air dries the surface. Wet skin steams instead of crisping.

**High heat finish:** After the long, low roast (which cooks the meat), the temperature is raised to 230°C+ for the final 30–45 minutes. This crisps the fat under the skin, which puffs and cracks the skin into crackling.

The low-to-high heat transition is the technique. Don't skip either phase.

## The Marinade: Adobo and Sofrito

**Adobo** in Puerto Rican cooking is the spice rub — garlic, dried oregano, black pepper, salt, and olive oil or vinegar. This is applied liberally and pressed into the scored skin and into pockets cut in the meat.

**Sofrito** is the aromatic base of Puerto Rican cooking: blended ají dulce peppers (sweet peppers), culantro (not cilantro — culantro is broader-leafed and more intense), garlic, onion, and recao. It can be made fresh or purchased jarred (Goya makes a commercial version). Sofrito is rubbed under the skin and into the meat.

**Recipe: Puerto Rican Pernil (Serves 8–10)**

- 1 whole bone-in pork shoulder (picnic or Boston butt), 3–4 kg, skin on
- 8 cloves garlic, minced
- 2 tablespoons dried oregano
- 2 teaspoons black pepper
- 1.5 tablespoons salt
- 2 tablespoons olive oil
- 2 tablespoons white vinegar
- 3 tablespoons sofrito (homemade or Goya)
- 1 packet sazón con achiote (Goya) — optional but adds color and flavor

**Method:**

1. **Score the skin:** With a very sharp knife, score the skin in a diamond pattern — cuts should go through the skin and into the fat layer, but not into the meat. This allows the marinade to penetrate and the skin to crisp evenly.

2. **Make the adobo paste:** Combine garlic, oregano, black pepper, salt, olive oil, vinegar, sofrito, and sazón. Mix into a paste.

3. **Marinate:** Rub the paste all over the pork — under the skin (lift it at the edges and push paste underneath), into the scores, all over the meat sides, and into any pocket cuts you make in the meat. Refrigerate uncovered (or covered) overnight, minimum 8 hours. Longer is better — 24–48 hours is ideal.

4. **Day of — bring to room temperature:** Remove pork from refrigerator 1–2 hours before roasting.

5. **Low roast:** Preheat oven to 150°C (300°F). Place pork skin-side up in a roasting pan. Roast for 3.5–5 hours depending on size (about 1 hour per 750g). The meat is done when it is fall-apart tender and an instant-read thermometer reads 75–80°C (165–175°F). Baste with pan juices every hour.

6. **Crisp the cuero:** Pat the skin very dry with paper towels. Raise oven temperature to 230°C (450°F). Roast 30–45 minutes until the skin is completely crispy, bubbled, and dark golden. Watch carefully — it can go from perfect to burnt quickly.

7. **Rest:** Allow to rest 15–20 minutes before carving. The meat will pull apart easily from the bone.

8. **Serve with:** Arroz con gandules (rice with pigeon peas), tostones, and pernil pan juices poured over the rice.
