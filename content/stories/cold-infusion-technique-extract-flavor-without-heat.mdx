Every standard cooking technique involves heat as the extraction mechanism. Boiling, steaming, sautéing, roasting — all of them use thermal energy to break down cell walls, volatilize aromatics, and drive compounds from their source into whatever medium you're cooking in. Cold infusion does something different: it extracts at ambient or refrigerator temperatures, over longer time, and pulls a fundamentally different set of compounds.

This isn't just a slower version of the same result. Cold and heat are selective filters. Understanding what each one passes — and what each one blocks — gives you a technique that creates flavors heat cannot.

---

## Why cold extracts different compounds than heat

At the molecular level, extraction is about three things: solubility, diffusion rate, and stability.

**Solubility** — some compounds dissolve readily in cold water or cold oil; others require heat to loosen from their cellular structure. Delicate volatile aromatics (the compounds that give fresh herbs, yuzu peel, and raw kombu their characteristic lightness) are often already soluble at room temperature. They don't need heat.

**Diffusion rate** — heat speeds diffusion dramatically. Cold infusion is slow precisely because the temperature differential driving diffusion is low. This is a feature, not a bug: slow diffusion means you can time extraction to get the compounds you want without overshooting into compounds you don't.

**Stability** — many aromatic compounds are thermally unstable. The volatile compounds that make fresh basil smell like basil, that give yuzu its clean citrus note, or that give a good kombu its oceanic depth without bitterness — these break down, transform, or evaporate quickly at cooking temperatures. Cold preserves them intact.

The result: a cold-infused liquid tends to be cleaner, brighter, and more representative of the ingredient's raw character. A heat-extracted liquid is deeper, more complex, and contains compounds created by the extraction process itself (Maillard products, thermally transformed compounds, caramelization byproducts). Neither is better. They're different tools.

## Kombu cold dashi: the Japanese baseline

Kombu cold dashi (mizudashi kombu) is the clearest example of intentional cold extraction in Japanese cooking. Where hot dashi produces a full, deep stock-like base, cold kombu dashi produces something cleaner — more distinctly oceanic, with less bitterness and a finer clarity.

The reason: kombu contains glutamate (the umami compound) and mannitol (a natural sweetener), both of which are water-soluble at cold temperatures. But kombu also contains compounds that produce bitterness and sliminess when extracted by heat — specifically, when the kombu reaches above 60°C and begins releasing alginates. Cold extraction gets the glutamate and mannitol without the alginates.

**Kombu cold dashi:**
- 10g kombu (one 4-inch piece) per 1 liter cold water
- Submerge in a container, refrigerate 8-12 hours (overnight is fine, 24 hours is the maximum before bitterness increases)
- Remove kombu and use immediately, or refrigerate up to 3 days

**Uses:** As the base for hot pot broth (heat after infusing), as the liquid for steaming delicate fish or vegetables, as the base for any soup where you want a clean, distinct umami note rather than a rounder stock character. Can be used anywhere water is called for in a recipe where you want additional depth — rice cooking, braising, vinaigrette.

## The temperature window for volatile aromatics

The compounds most worth cold-extracting are volatile aromatics: the terpenes and terpenoids in citrus peel, herbs, spices, and flowers. "Volatile" means they evaporate readily — which is the property that makes them fragrant, and the property that makes them vulnerable to heat.

Citrus peel is the most practical example. Yuzu peel cold-infused into sake or neutral oil produces a yuzu note that is unmistakably fresh — the aromatic profile is close to the raw peel. Hot-infused yuzu (yuzu zest heated in oil or liquid) produces a cooked citrus note: deeper, more jammy, less bright. Neither is wrong, but they're suited to different applications. A cold-infused yuzu oil works as a finishing oil on a finished dish; a hot-infused yuzu sauce belongs in a braise or reduction.

**Yuzu cold infusion in sake:**
- Zest of 1 yuzu (or substitute Meyer lemon + a few drops of yuzu juice)
- 200ml sake
- Refrigerate 4-6 hours, strain
- Use as a component in dipping sauces, dressings, or light pan sauces — add off heat

**Sansho peel cold infusion:**
Sansho (Japanese pepper) cold-infused into rice vinegar for 24-48 hours produces a numbing, citrusy vinegar that has no equivalent in hot extraction. The numbing compound (sanshool) is alcohol-soluble and moderately water-soluble at cold temperatures. Use this infused vinegar to finish soups, dress salads, or as a component in dipping sauces.

## Italian cold infusion applications

Italian cooking has its own tradition of cold extraction, though it's rarely named as a technique. The most common form is herb-infused olive oil — which is almost always made cold (or at most, at very low temperatures).

**Why olive oil is the right cold infusion medium for Italian herbs:**
Most aromatic compounds in Mediterranean herbs (basil, rosemary, thyme, marjoram) are fat-soluble — they dissolve in oil but barely in water. This is why water-based herb infusions are pale compared to oil-based ones, and why finishing oil carries the full aromatic load.

**Basil oil (cold method):**
- Blanch basil leaves 15 seconds in boiling water, immediately ice bath, squeeze dry (this destroys enzymes that cause browning without volatilizing aromatics)
- Blend with good olive oil at ratio 1:4 by weight (25g basil: 100g oil)
- Let stand at room temperature 30 minutes for initial infusion
- Refrigerate and use within 48 hours

This is cleaner and more vibrantly green than any heated version.

**Lemon-infused white wine vinegar:**
- Lemon zest (from 2 lemons) + 250ml white wine vinegar
- Room temperature, covered, 24-48 hours
- Strain and store
- Produces a citrus-forward vinegar with none of the cooked quality of heated citrus vinegar — ideal for finishing fish dishes, dressing vegetables, building light pan sauces

## Cold brew coffee and the extraction parallel

Cold brew coffee is now widespread enough that its mechanics have been absorbed into general food knowledge — this is useful because it's the most studied and clearly articulated example of differential cold extraction. Cold brew extracts fewer acidic and bitter compounds than hot brew (specifically, low-solubility chlorogenic acid lactones and phenylindanes that require heat to release), producing a different flavor profile that isn't just cold coffee — it's a genuinely different extraction.

The principle transfers directly to any water-based cold infusion: you're selecting against the high-solubility-at-heat, often-bitter compounds while selecting for the compounds readily soluble at cold temperatures. Applied to cooking: if a hot-extracted stock or tea has bitterness you're working to neutralize, consider whether cold extraction would provide what you need without the bitterness.

## Timing and ratio guide

The two variables in cold infusion are ratio (how much ingredient per unit of solvent) and time (how long). More of either increases concentration, but time has a ceiling: over-infusion eventually pulls the compounds you're trying to avoid — the bitter, astringent, or slimy ones that are slow-extracting even in cold.

| Ingredient | Solvent | Ratio | Time | Max time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kombu | Cold water | 10g per liter | 8-12 hrs | 24 hrs |
| Yuzu/lemon zest | Sake or white wine | 1 medium fruit per 200ml | 4-6 hrs | 12 hrs |
| Sansho peel | Rice wine vinegar | 5g per 200ml | 24 hrs | 48 hrs |
| Fresh herbs (basil, tarragon) | Olive oil | 1:4 by weight | 2-4 hrs | 24 hrs |
| Lemon zest | White wine vinegar | 2 lemons per 250ml | 24 hrs | 48 hrs |
| Dried mushroom | Cold water | 10g per 200ml | 6-8 hrs | 24 hrs |
| Coffee (coarse ground) | Cold water | 1:8 by weight | 12-16 hrs | 24 hrs |

## Cold vs. hot: a decision framework

Use cold infusion when:
- The ingredient is delicate (fresh herbs, citrus peel, fresh yuzu)
- You want the raw aromatic character preserved — clean, bright, volatile
- The compound you're extracting is already soluble at cold temperatures (glutamate in kombu, fat-soluble aromatics in herbs)
- Bitterness is a concern in the hot-extracted version

Use heat when:
- You want depth, complexity, and the transformation that thermal extraction creates
- The compound requires heat to release (starch, collagen, tightly bound aromatic compounds in woody spices)
- Time is a constraint — heat is dramatically faster
- You're building a base (stock, braise liquid) where complexity is the goal

In practice, the most sophisticated technique combines both: cold-infuse the delicate aromatics first, then add heat for the structural elements. The kombu cold dashi technique does exactly this — cold-infuse for clean glutamate, then optionally heat to 80°C for a slightly deeper version without bitterness. Two extractions, two compound sets, one more complete result.
