Pelmeni are one of the great dumpling traditions of the world and one of the least understood outside Russia. They are often confused with pierogi (Polish, larger, with vegetable or potato fillings) or with Georgian khinkali (larger, with soup broth inside). Pelmeni are smaller, simpler, and more austere than either — the elegance is in the thinness of the dough and the seasoning of the filling.

The Siberian origin story explains the design: pelmeni were made in vast quantities in autumn before the freeze, stored frozen outdoors through the Siberian winter (temperatures reliably cold enough to keep them frozen for months), then boiled directly from frozen as needed. The small size was functional — they freeze quickly and cook quickly from frozen. The plain meat filling was what was available. The simple flour-water dough required no leavening and kept well.

## The Dough

Pelmeni dough is flour, egg, water, and salt — no fat, no leavening, no yeast. It is very similar to pasta dough but typically firmer and more elastic. The goal is a dough thin enough to be delicate but strong enough to hold the filling during boiling without tearing.

Traditional pelmeni dough is rested after kneading (15–30 minutes wrapped), which relaxes the gluten and makes it easier to roll thin. It should be rolled to 1–2mm — translucent thin, not opaque.

## The Filling

Traditional pelmeni filling is a mixture of:
- Ground pork (for fat and richness)
- Ground beef (for flavor and texture)
- Raw onion (very finely grated or minced — it should be almost a paste to avoid texture lumps)
- Salt and black pepper

The filling should be well-seasoned and slightly loose — adding 2–3 tablespoons of ice water or cold water to the meat mixture helps keep the filling tender after boiling. A raw filling that goes directly from the bowl into the dumpling, then is boiled — the filling cooks inside the dough.

Some family recipes add a small amount of veal or lamb. The classic ratio is roughly 50/50 pork and beef.

## The Folding Technique

Pelmeni are formed in a specific way. The dough is cut into circles (a glass rim is traditional, about 7–8cm diameter). A small amount of filling (about 1 teaspoon) goes in the center. The circle is folded in half over the filling, the edges pressed firmly together to seal, and then the two ends of the semicircle are bent toward each other and pinched together — creating a shape like a tortellino or an ear.

This shape is functional: the folded edge creates more structural integrity against boiling, and the curved shape helps them cook more evenly.

Large Russian families often make pelmeni in assembly lines — one person rolling and cutting dough, others filling and folding — producing hundreds at a time.

## Cooking and Serving

Pelmeni are boiled in salted water (or light meat stock) until they float and the filling is cooked through — about 5–8 minutes from fresh, 10–12 from frozen.

Traditional serving in Russia is simple:
- **Smetana:** Russian sour cream — thick, slightly tangy — the essential accompaniment
- **Butter:** Just melted butter over freshly boiled pelmeni
- **Vinegar:** A splash of vinegar — sounds unlikely but is traditional in Siberia

In broth (kak borshch): pelmeni served in a clear meat broth with dill and sour cream.

**Recipe: Pelmeni (Makes 60–70 small dumplings)**

**Dough:**
- 300g plain flour (plus extra for dusting)
- 1 egg
- 100ml cold water (approximately)
- 1/2 teaspoon salt

**Filling:**
- 250g ground pork
- 250g ground beef
- 1 medium onion, very finely grated
- 3 tablespoons cold water
- 1 teaspoon salt
- 1/2 teaspoon black pepper

**Method:**

1. **Dough:** Mix flour and salt. Make a well; add egg and water. Mix to a shaggy dough; knead 8–10 minutes until smooth and elastic. Wrap; rest 30 minutes.

2. **Filling:** Combine pork, beef, grated onion (squeeze out excess water first), cold water, salt, and pepper. Mix well until combined. Refrigerate while making dough.

3. **Forming:** Roll dough very thin (1–2mm) on a floured surface. Cut into 7–8cm circles. Place 1 teaspoon filling in center of each circle. Fold dough over into a semicircle; press edges firmly to seal (crimp if desired). Bring the two pointed ends of the semicircle together; press to join.

4. **Boiling:** Bring a large pot of salted water to a rolling boil. Add pelmeni in batches (don't crowd). Cook until they float, then 3 more minutes (5 minutes total from fresh; 10–12 from frozen). Remove with a slotted spoon.

5. **Serving:** Serve immediately with smetana (sour cream) and black pepper. Or serve in a bowl of the cooking broth with fresh dill.

*Freeze uncooked pelmeni on a flour-dusted tray until solid (2 hours), then transfer to bags. They keep frozen for 3 months and cook directly from frozen.*
