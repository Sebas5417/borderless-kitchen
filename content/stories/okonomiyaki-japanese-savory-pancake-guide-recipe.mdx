Okonomiyaki restaurants in Osaka give you a hot teppan griddle at your table and bring you the ingredients — you cook it yourself. This is intentional: the name literally means "grilled as you like it," and the participatory, customizable nature of the dish is part of its identity.

In Hiroshima, 400 kilometers away, they make it completely differently and consider the Osaka version a simplified shortcut.

Both are right, and understanding why requires understanding what each version is actually doing.

## Osaka Style (Kansai-style)

The Osaka version is a true mix-and-cook pancake: all ingredients go into the batter together, and the batter is cooked as one unit.

**Batter:** Flour, dashi, eggs, and nagaimo (mountain yam, grated to a slimy paste that adds lightness and binding). The nagaimo is the technical element that makes Osaka okonomiyaki lighter and fluffier than the Hiroshima version.

**Cabbage:** Finely shredded, folded into the batter in large quantity — more cabbage than batter by volume. The cabbage steams inside the pancake as it cooks.

**Protein additions:** Pork belly (most common), shrimp, squid, octopus, or any combination — mixed into the batter.

**Cooking:** Pour onto hot griddle; cook until the edges set and the bottom browns; flip carefully once; cook until done through.

The result is thick, slightly fluffy, with the cabbage soft and steamed inside and a browned crust on both sides.

## Hiroshima Style

Hiroshima's version is layered, not mixed. This is the more complex and controversial technique:

1. A thin crepe of batter is made first (much thinner than Osaka)
2. Cabbage and bean sprouts are piled on top and covered to steam
3. Noodles (yakisoba or udon) are cooked separately on another part of the griddle
4. The protein is cooked separately
5. An egg is fried flat on the griddle
6. The crepe is flipped and placed on the noodles; the protein goes on top; the egg goes on the very top

The result is a layered construction — crepe + steamed cabbage + noodles + protein + egg — rather than a mixed pancake. It is more filling (the noodles make it substantial), more complex in texture (the crisp noodles vs the soft steamed cabbage), and requires more technique to cook.

Hiroshima people will tell you Osaka okonomiyaki is just a pancake. Osaka people will tell you Hiroshima is overthinking it.

## The Toppings

Both versions receive the same finishing toppings:

**Okonomiyaki sauce:** A thick, sweet-savory sauce similar to tonkatsu sauce but slightly different — Otafuku brand is the standard. Painted on in a back-and-forth pattern.

**Japanese mayonnaise:** Kewpie mayo, which is richer and slightly sweeter than Western mayo, applied in zigzag lines or a spiral. This is not optional — the mayo cuts the sweetness of the sauce and adds richness.

**Katsuobushi (bonito flakes):** Dried, shaved skipjack tuna. These wave from the heat rising from the surface — one of the distinctive visual elements.

**Aonori:** Dried green nori powder, scattered over.

**Benishoga:** Pickled red ginger, on the side.

**Recipe: Osaka-Style Okonomiyaki (Serves 2)**

**Batter:**
- 100g all-purpose flour
- 120ml dashi (or water with 1/2 teaspoon dashi powder)
- 1/2 teaspoon soy sauce
- 1/4 teaspoon salt
- 50g nagaimo, grated (or substitute 1 extra egg + 1 tablespoon yogurt for lightness)
- 2 eggs

**Filling:**
- 300g green cabbage, very finely shredded
- 100g pork belly slices (or 150g shrimp)
- 2 stalks green onion, sliced

**Toppings:**
- Okonomiyaki sauce (or mix: 2 tbsp Worcestershire, 1 tbsp ketchup, 1 tbsp soy sauce, 1 tsp sugar)
- Japanese mayo (Kewpie)
- Katsuobushi (bonito flakes)
- Aonori

**Method:**

1. Whisk flour, dashi, soy sauce, and salt together. Add grated nagaimo (or egg substitute) and eggs; whisk until smooth. Batter should be slightly thick.

2. Fold in shredded cabbage and green onion. The batter will barely coat the cabbage — this is correct.

3. Heat a large skillet or griddle over medium-high heat. Brush with oil.

4. Divide batter into 2 portions. Pour one portion onto the griddle; shape into a thick round disc about 15cm diameter. Lay pork belly slices across the top.

5. Cook 4–5 minutes until the edges are set and the underside is golden brown. Flip carefully — use two spatulas if needed — so the pork belly is now underneath.

6. Cook another 4–5 minutes until cooked through. (Press gently in the center — it should feel set, not liquid.)

7. Transfer to a plate. Apply okonomiyaki sauce, then mayo in zigzag lines. Top with katsuobushi and aonori. Serve immediately.

*Test for doneness: insert a toothpick in the center — it should come out clean. The interior should be soft and slightly springy, not wet.*
