Shabu-shabu (しゃぶしゃぶ) takes its name from the sound of the cooking technique: the Japanese onomatopoeia for the swishing sound of thin beef sliced through hot broth — *shabu-shabu*. It's a description that captures the entire dish: Japanese hot pot distilled to its simplest, most refined expression.

Where Korean hot pot (*jeongol*) and Chinese hot pot (*huoguo*) are often intensely flavored with chili and spices, shabu-shabu keeps the broth deliberately minimal — a clean kombu dashi — so the natural flavor of the beef (often premium wagyu) and vegetables is the point. The dipping sauces provide the seasoning; the broth is a cooking medium, not a flavor delivery vehicle.

---

## What Shabu-Shabu Is

**The setup:** A pot of simmering kombu broth in the center of the table. Plates of paper-thin sliced raw beef (and sometimes pork or chicken), plates of vegetables, tofu, and mushrooms, and two dipping sauces — ponzu and goma dare (sesame sauce).

**The technique:** Pick up a slice of beef with chopsticks; swish it through the simmering (not boiling) broth 3–10 seconds until just cooked through (medium-rare for thin beef); dip in sauce of your choice; eat.

**The name:** *Shabu-shabu* (しゃぶしゃぶ) comes from the sound of the swishing motion — the thin beef slice creates a audible *shabu* sound in the broth. The name was invented by Suehiro restaurant in Osaka in the 1950s, which is credited with popularizing the format.

## The Broth

Shabu-shabu broth is intentionally subtle:

**Classic kombu broth:**
- 1 piece kombu (approximately 10g) in 1.5L cold water
- Heat to approximately 60°C; hold 20–30 minutes
- Remove kombu just before simmering begins; the broth is ready

The broth starts clean and accumulates flavor throughout the meal as beef and vegetables release their juices into it. By the end of the meal, the broth has become a rich secondary dashi — usually consumed or used as the base for a noodle finish course.

**Additions:**
Some restaurants and home cooks add a small amount of sake to the kombu broth (approximately 2–3 tablespoons per liter), which helps neutralize any gaminess from the beef and adds depth. Some add very thin slices of ginger.

**Temperature control:** The broth should simmer gently — active but not vigorously boiling. Vigorous boiling produces turbulence that makes the thin beef difficult to handle and can overcook it.

## The Beef

Shabu-shabu beef is sliced paper-thin — 1–2mm — by machine (not knife). The extreme thinness is essential: at that thickness, the beef cooks in 3–7 seconds of contact with hot broth, allowing it to be served at perfect medium-rare or pink.

**Recommended cuts:**
- **Ribeye (*rosu*, ロース):** The most common and most popular shabu cut. Good fat marbling maintains moisture at the thin cut.
- **Sirloin (*hire*, ヒレ):** Leaner; tenderness preserved despite the thin cut.
- **Short plate/brisket (*bara*, バラ):** More fat; assertive beefy flavor; less expensive.

**Grade:** Higher marbling (higher grade) produces better shabu-shabu because the thin cut has no muscle mass to carry flavor — the flavor comes almost entirely from intramuscular fat. A5 wagyu shabu-shabu, where the fat content is extreme, produces an experience where the beef slice barely needs to touch the broth before it cooks.

**Pork shabu:** Pork shoulder or loin, similarly thinly sliced, is common in everyday home shabu-shabu — more affordable than wagyu; requires slightly longer cooking (5–10 seconds) to ensure full doneness.

## The Dipping Sauces

The two standard shabu-shabu dipping sauces:

### Ponzu (ポン酢)
A citrus-based soy sauce: soy sauce + citrus juice (*yuzu*, *sudachi*, or *kabosu*) + dashi. Bright, acidic, clean. Cuts through the beef's fat; preferred for lighter, leaner cuts.

**Commercial ponzu:** Mizkan and Kikkoman produce widely available ponzu. The commercial version is entirely acceptable for shabu-shabu.

**Accompaniments for ponzu:** Grated daikon (oroshi daikon, 大根おろし), sliced green onion, and a pinch of *ichimi* (ground chili) are placed at the table to mix into the ponzu to taste.

### Goma Dare (ごまだれ) — Sesame Sauce
A rich, sesame-forward dipping sauce: ground sesame paste (*neri goma*), soy sauce, mirin, rice vinegar, dashi, and sometimes peanut butter for additional richness.

The goma dare's richness pairs better with fattier cuts of beef and with vegetables. Where ponzu provides acid contrast to fat, goma dare layers richness upon richness — the result is more substantial, more filling.

**Basic goma dare formula:**
- 4 tbsp white sesame paste (or tahini)
- 2 tbsp soy sauce
- 1 tbsp mirin
- 1 tbsp rice vinegar
- 1 tbsp water to thin
- Optional: 1 tsp peanut butter, 1/2 tsp toasted sesame oil

Whisk until smooth. Thin to desired consistency. Add minced garlic and ginger for additional depth.

## The Vegetables and Add-Ins

Standard shabu-shabu vegetables, chosen for their ability to cook quickly in simmering broth without falling apart:

- **Napa cabbage (*hakusai*, 白菜):** Wilts in 30–60 seconds; absorbs broth
- **Enoki mushrooms (*enoki*, えのき):** Cooks in 30 seconds; mild, springy texture
- **Shiitake mushrooms:** Adds umami to the broth; 2–3 minutes
- **Tofu (*tofu*):** Firm tofu in slices; needs 3–4 minutes to warm through
- **Chrysanthemum greens (*shungiku*, 春菊):** Slightly bitter; wilts in 15–20 seconds. Classic shabu green.
- **Green onion:** Thin slices; added raw to personal dipping bowls or placed in broth
- **Narutomaki / fish cakes:** Occasionally included

**Order of adding to pot:** Add longer-cooking ingredients (shiitake, tofu) first; add delicate greens last. Don't add too many ingredients at once — it drops broth temperature.

## The締め (Shime) — Finishing Course

After the meat and vegetables are finished, the broth has become a rich secondary stock enriched by everything cooked in it. The *shime* (締め, "finish") uses this broth:

**Zōsui (雑炊):** Cooked rice added to the broth, simmered until the rice absorbs the stock and the mixture thickens. A lightly beaten egg stirred in at the end. This creates an immediate completion — the broth that started the meal finishes it.

**Udon:** Thick udon noodles cooked in the broth — a more substantial finish, particularly when pork was the protein.

## Shabu-Shabu vs. Sukiyaki

The most common confusion: shabu-shabu and sukiyaki are not the same dish.

| | Shabu-Shabu | Sukiyaki |
|---|---|---|
| Broth | Clear kombu dashi | Sweet soy sauce broth (*warishita*: soy + sake + mirin + sugar) |
| Flavor | Mild; seasoning from dipping sauce | Sweet, heavily seasoned |
| Beef doneness | Briefly swished; medium-rare | Simmered longer in sweet sauce |
| Egg | Not used in cooking | Raw egg used as dipping vessel |
| Vegetables | Blanched in broth | Simmered in sweet sauce |

Sukiyaki's broth is sweet and assertive — the broth is the seasoning, and everything cooked in it acquires its character. Shabu-shabu keeps the broth neutral and outsources seasoning to the dipping sauces. The two produce entirely different eating experiences despite similar table-based format.

---

**The at-home version is entirely achievable:** A portable induction burner (or any stovetop with a portable pot), a package of thinly sliced beef from a Japanese or Korean grocery store, homemade or bottled ponzu, and a basic goma dare. Shabu-shabu's apparent complexity dissolves when you realize the broth is just kombu water, the beef is just thinly sliced meat, and the work of cooking happens in seconds at the table.
