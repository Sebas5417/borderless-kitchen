Andong jjimdak (안동찜닭) is a dish from Andong — a city in North Gyeongsang Province, historically significant as a center of Confucian scholarship and traditional Korean culture, home to the Hahoe Folk Village UNESCO site. The dish involves whole chicken pieces braised in a sauce of soy sauce, sugar, garlic, ginger, and gochugaru, with glass noodles (*dangmyeon*), potatoes, carrots, and green onion absorbing the braising liquid.

The result is a large, communal one-pot dish — deeply savory, slightly sweet, with the glass noodles providing a contrasting silky texture against the tender chicken.

---

## Origin: Andong Jjimdak Street

The story of contemporary andong jjimdak begins not in ancient Joseon dynasty kitchens but in the 1980s. Andong's Guseong-dong market district developed a concentrated cluster of jjimdak restaurants in narrow alleyways that became known collectively as *Andong Jjimdak Street* (안동찜닭 골목).

The dishes served there — soy-braised chicken with glass noodles — became a regional specialty that attracted visitors from across Korea. By the early 2000s, andong jjimdak restaurants had spread throughout Korea, and the dish was firmly established as a national Korean comfort food despite its relatively recent codification.

The dish's connection to Andong's broader identity (traditional, conservative, associated with neo-Confucian values and preservation of old Korean customs) has given it a cultural weight beyond its cooking history.

## The Components

**Chicken:** Whole chicken, cut into pieces (bone-in, skin-on). Traditional andong jjimdak uses the whole chicken including back, neck, and wings. The bones release gelatin into the braise, thickening the sauce and adding body. Thighs and drumsticks work for a simpler version.

**Glass noodles (*dangmyeon*, 당면):** Korean glass noodles made from sweet potato starch — these are the critical texture element. Dangmyeon doesn't dissolve into the sauce the way some noodles do; they remain distinct, silky, and chewy. They're added at the end of cooking and soak up the braising liquid rapidly.

**Soy sauce base:** The braising liquid is primarily soy-based, with a balance of sweet (sugar, cheong-yang or rice syrup), savory (soy, garlic, ginger), and aromatic (gochugaru for red color and mild heat, sesame oil at the finish).

**Vegetables:** Potatoes (starch thickens the sauce), carrots, onion, and jalapeño or cheongyang chili peppers. Green onion is added near the end.

## Andong Jjimdak Recipe

**Serves 4**

### Ingredients

**Chicken:**
- 1.5 kg chicken pieces (bone-in, or whole chicken cut into 10-12 pieces)

**Braising sauce:**
- 5 tbsp soy sauce (*ganjang*)
- 2 tbsp dark soy sauce (*jin ganjang* or Chinese dark soy sauce) — for depth and color
- 3 tbsp sugar (or 2 tbsp rice syrup *mullyeot*)
- 2 tbsp gochugaru (adjust to preference; this level gives color and moderate warmth, not high heat)
- 8 cloves garlic, minced
- 1 tbsp ginger, grated
- 1 tbsp mirin or rice wine
- 200ml water

**Vegetables:**
- 2 medium potatoes, cut into 3-4cm chunks
- 2 medium carrots, cut into 3cm diagonal pieces
- 1 large onion, cut into 8 wedges
- 2-3 cheongyang or jalapeño chili peppers, halved lengthwise
- 4 stalks green onion, cut into 4cm pieces

**Glass noodles:**
- 80-100g *dangmyeon* (Korean sweet potato glass noodles), soaked in warm water 20 minutes until pliable

**Finish:**
- 1 tbsp sesame oil
- 1 tsp sesame seeds

---

### Method

**1. Blanch chicken.**

Place chicken in cold water; bring to a boil. Boil 3-4 minutes; drain and rinse under cold water. This removes surface proteins that cloud the braising sauce.

**2. Mix braising sauce.**

Combine soy sauces, sugar, gochugaru, garlic, ginger, mirin, and water. Stir until sugar dissolves.

**3. Combine and start braising.**

Place blanched chicken in a wide, heavy pot or Dutch oven. Pour braising sauce over chicken; toss to coat.

Bring to a boil over high heat, stirring once or twice.

**4. Add hard vegetables.**

Add potatoes, carrots, and onion. Reduce heat to medium; cover and cook 15 minutes, stirring once halfway through.

**5. Add chili peppers and noodles.**

After 15 minutes, the chicken should be mostly cooked and sauce partially reduced. Add chili peppers and drained glass noodles.

Stir everything together gently — the noodles will absorb braising liquid rapidly. Cook uncovered 5-7 more minutes, stirring regularly, until noodles are completely tender (glass noodles should be transparent and soft throughout) and sauce has thickened to a glazy consistency.

**Critical:** Watch the noodles closely. They absorb liquid fast — if the sauce is disappearing too quickly, add 2-3 tbsp water. If too much sauce remains, increase heat and cook uncovered until it reduces to a glaze.

**6. Finish.**

Add green onion. Drizzle sesame oil and scatter sesame seeds. Toss gently.

**7. Serve.**

Andong jjimdak is traditionally served in the pot directly on the table. Large portions are shared communally, using chopsticks and spoons. White rice alongside for eating with the sauce.

## Sauce Balance Notes

The braising sauce requires balance between sweet, salty, and spicy:

**For a less spicy version:** Reduce gochugaru to 1 tbsp. Omit fresh chili peppers.

**For more spice:** Add 1 extra tbsp gochugaru; use 3-4 cheongyang gochugaru peppers (very hot variety).

**Too sweet:** Add an additional tbsp soy sauce.

**Too salty:** Add 1 tbsp sugar; thin with 2 tbsp water.

**The dark soy sauce question:** Traditional andong jjimdak recipes may not specify dark soy sauce separately, using only regular ganjang in larger quantities. The dark soy shortcut provides richer color and body efficiently; both methods work.

---

## The Glass Noodle Factor

Dangmyeon (*당면*) is often the most memorable part of andong jjimdak for first-timers. These Korean sweet potato glass noodles soak up the braising sauce without losing their texture — they become intensely flavored while remaining slippery, chewy, and distinct.

**Cooking point:** Unlike rice or wheat noodles, dangmyeon doesn't turn mushy with slight overcooking — they have a forgiving quality that makes them ideal for braises. But they will drink all available liquid if left unchecked; watch the sauce level.

**Using more:** Some restaurants and home cooks use substantially more dangmyeon than most recipes call for — making the noodle as prominent as the chicken. If you love the noodle component, use up to 150g (double the standard).

---

## Restaurant Andong Jjimdak

Traditional Andong Jjimdak Street restaurants serve the dish in large aluminum or stainless steel bowls — often over an open flame to keep it hot. The serving size is intended for sharing, and the dish is replenished with additional noodles when the first round finishes (an upgrade at many restaurants).

Andong jjimdak restaurants outside Korea typically offer sizes from 2-person to 4-6-person portions; ordering by person count rather than individual bowls is standard.

Andong jjimdak proves that some of the most culturally significant Korean dishes have relatively recent histories — the dish's association with Andong's traditional identity took root within a generation. What makes it Andong isn't centuries of recipe history; it's the specific market street, the atmosphere of the restaurant cluster, and the way the dish became attached to the city's identity. The food made the place as much as the place made the food.
