The Feast of the Seven Fishes is an Italian-American Christmas Eve tradition. It grew out of *la Vigilia*, the Southern Italian custom of eating no meat on the eve of a feast day, so the table filled up with seafood instead. In Italy it's simply the Vigil dinner; the fixed number seven is mostly an Italian-American convention, and nobody checks.

That flexibility makes it the perfect Christmas Eve for a Japanese-Italian kitchen. Japan is one of the great seafood cultures, and much of its pantry (dashi, ponzu, miso marinades) works beautifully with fish. Every course below keeps its Italian shape and borrows one Japanese idea.

---

## 1. The Crudo: Salmon Tataki with Ponzu

**Fish: salmon.** Italian crudo and Japanese tataki are the same instinct: very fresh fish, barely touched, dressed with something sharp. [Salmon tataki with ponzu](/recipes/japanese-ponzu-tataki-salmon) sears the outside and leaves the centre raw, then dresses it with citrusy ponzu and grated daikon. It takes ten minutes. Buy fish sold for raw eating from a fishmonger you trust. Raw and seared-rare fish isn't advised for pregnant people, young children or anyone immunocompromised. More on the sauce: [what ponzu is and how to use it](/journal/ponzu-sauce-what-it-is-and-how-to-use-it-in-western-cooking).

## 2. The Fritto Misto, in Tempura Batter

**Fish: squid and shrimp.** Fried calamari is on most Seven Fishes tables. Make it with a tempura batter instead: flour and ice-cold sparkling water, mixed for only a few seconds and left lumpy, so it fries thin, lacy and crisp. Tempura itself is usually traced to Portuguese traders and missionaries in 16th-century Japan, so this course has been travelling for a while. Serve with lemon wedges and a little flaky salt. The batter rules are in [tempura vs karaage](/journal/japanese-tempura-vs-karaage), and the fritter version is in our [kakiage guide](/journal/japanese-kakiage-tempura-guide).

## 3. The Clams: Spaghetti alle Vongole with Sake

**Fish: clams.** Japan steams clams in sake (*asari no sakamushi*); Italy steams them in white wine for spaghetti alle vongole. Swap the wine for a dry sake: garlic and chilli in olive oil, the clams and a splash of sake, lid on until they open, then toss with the spaghetti and a spoon of the pasta water. Discard any clams that stay shut. Which sake to cook with is in our [sake types guide](/journal/japanese-sake-types-guide).

## 4. The Anchovy: Spaghetti with Colatura

**Fish: anchovies.** Colatura di alici, the amber anchovy sauce from Cetara on the Amalfi Coast, is Italy's fish sauce, and spaghetti with colatura, garlic and oil is a Christmas Eve dish on that coast. It's the most Japanese-tasting thing in the Italian pantry: pure savoury depth, the same job fish sauce or dashi does. If you serve two pastas, keep this one small, a few forkfuls per person. Its Southeast Asian cousins are in our [fish sauce guide](/journal/fish-sauce-guide-southeast-asian-fermented-seafood).

## 5. The Risotto: Dashi Risotto with Seared Scallops

**Fish: scallops (and bonito in the stock).** [Dashi risotto](/recipes/dashi-risotto) keeps the risotto technique and swaps the stock for kombu-and-bonito dashi and the wine for sake. The flavour turns clean and oceanic, which is exactly what a scallop wants. Sear the scallops hard in a very hot pan, a minute or two a side, and set them on top at the end. Making the dashi: [the complete dashi guide](/journal/japanese-dashi-complete-guide).

## 6. The Main: Miso-Marinated Cod

**Fish: cod.** *Baccalà*, salt cod, is the traditional anchor of the Vigilia. Japan's answer is *saikyo-yaki*: fish marinated for a day or two in sweet white miso, then grilled until the edges caramelise. Black cod (sablefish, not a true cod) is the classic choice; Pacific cod works too. Wipe off most of the marinade before cooking, because the sugar in the miso burns fast under the grill.

For an easier main with the same idea, make [shio koji salmon](/recipes/japanese-shio-koji-salmon), which marinates for a day in salt koji and needs nothing else.

## Counting the Fish

Count the squid and shrimp separately and the menu is already at seven: salmon, squid, shrimp, clams, anchovies, scallops and cod (eight with the bonito in the dashi). Seven is a convention, not a rule, so drop a course if the kitchen is small, or add a plate of [teriyaki salmon](/recipes/japanese-teriyaki-salmon-recipe) for the kids.

## Dessert

After this much seafood, finish light. [Hojicha panna cotta](/recipes/hojicha-panna-cotta) (roasted green tea in cream) sets overnight, and [matcha tiramisu](/recipes/matcha-tiramisu) improves after a night in the fridge. Both are made the day before.

## Make-Ahead Plan

- **Two days before:** order the fish, and ask the fishmonger which of it is suitable for eating raw. Start the miso marinade for the cod.
- **The day before:** make the dessert and the dashi. Coat the shio koji salmon, if making it.
- **The morning of:** check the clams, discarding any with cracked shells or that won't close when tapped, and keep them cold in a bowl under a damp cloth, not sealed or in water. Slice garlic, chop parsley, set up the frying station.
- **The last two hours:** sear the tataki and slice it just before serving. Fry the tempura in batches and serve it at once. Cook the clam pasta, the colatura pasta and the risotto to order, in menu order, and grill the cod last.
