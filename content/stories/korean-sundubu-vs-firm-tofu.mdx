One of the most frequent errors in Korean cooking is using the wrong type of tofu. The types look similar in photos and at Asian grocery stores. But they function completely differently in cooking, and substituting one for another produces a different dish.

## The Tofu Production Spectrum

All tofu starts the same way: soybeans are ground, their "milk" is extracted, and a coagulant (calcium sulfate, magnesium chloride, or vinegar) is added to curdle the proteins. The difference between types is how much the curds are pressed and how long.

More pressing → more water removed → firmer, denser, higher protein per volume.

**The spectrum:**
Uncurdled → Soft → Silken → Medium → Firm → Extra Firm → Dried

## The Four Korean Types

### 1. Sundubu (순두부) — Uncurdled Soft Tofu

What it is: The soy milk is coagulated but the curds are not pressed at all. The result is extremely soft — barely cohesive, with curds that collapse at the slightest pressure.

**Texture:** Silkier than silken tofu. Pourable when warm.

**Packaging:** Sold in soft tubes at Korean grocery stores. You push the tube against the pot and cut sections in directly — scooping would break the curd structure further.

**Used in:** Sundubu jjigae exclusively (essentially). The dish is named after this specific tofu. You cannot substitute firm tofu in sundubu jjigae and produce the same result — the texture is the point.

**Not for:** Any application requiring structure (stir-frying, grilling, marinating).

### 2. Yeon Dubu (연두부) — Soft/Silken Tofu

What it is: Curds formed and just barely pressed. Holds its shape as a block but cuts with a spoon.

**Texture:** Very smooth, almost custard-like. Similar to Japanese silken tofu.

**Used in:**
- Hiyayakko-style cold tofu (white tofu block topped with sauce, eaten cold)
- Doenjang jjigae (dissolves softly into the stew)
- Soups where you want the tofu to be delicate
- Eaten directly as a mild banchan with soy sauce and sesame oil

**Not for:** High-heat cooking (it breaks apart), stir-frying.

### 3. Busan Dubu / Firm Dubu (부침용 두부) — Pan-Frying Tofu

What it is: More pressed than silken. Holds its shape firmly when sliced.

**Texture:** Dense, porous, slightly springy. Absorbs marinades well.

**Used in:**
- Dubu jorim (braised spicy tofu) — the most common Korean tofu preparation
- Pan-fried tofu (*dubu buchim*) — sliced and fried in sesame oil until golden
- Added to kimchi jjigae without falling apart
- Tofu steak

The label *부침용* (*buchim-yong*, for pan-frying) indicates this type at Korean markets.

**Dubu buchim technique:** Slice 1cm thick. Pat completely dry (moisture causes splattering). Fry in sesame oil + vegetable oil over medium-high heat until golden on both sides. Season with soy sauce and sesame. The golden crust makes the interior's texture more interesting.

### 4. Mu Dubu / Dried Tofu (건두부 or 두부피) — Tofu Skin/Dried Sheet

What it is: A pressed, dried form — either very firm compressed tofu blocks or *dubu pi* (tofu skin, the film that forms on heated soy milk).

**Texture:** Dense, chewy, almost meaty.

**Used in:**
- Japchae variations (cut into strips, stir-fried)
- Braised dishes (absorbs flavor during long cooking)
- As a filling material

## The Korean Tofu Hierarchy

**For sundubu jjigae:** Only sundubu (순두부).

**For most soups and stews:** Soft/silken yeon dubu.

**For pan-frying, braising, dubu jorim:** Firm buchim-yong dubu.

**For stir-fries and substantial textures:** Dried or extra-firm.

The most important distinction for Korean cooking is the first one: sundubu for sundubu jjigae, nothing else for sundubu jjigae. Everything else is more flexible.

---

Tofu is a category of ingredients, not a single ingredient — in the same way that fresh pasta and dried pasta are both "pasta" but require different handling and produce different results. Getting the type right is the first skill.
