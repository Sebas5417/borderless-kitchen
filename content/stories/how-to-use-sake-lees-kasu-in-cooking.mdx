When sake is pressed, the liquid — the sake — separates from the solids. Those solids are kasu: a thick, paste-like, off-white mass that smells strongly of alcohol and fermentation and contains everything that didn't make it through the press. Active yeast cells. Enzymes still working. A high concentration of amino acids, including glutamate. Small amounts of alcohol. B vitamins.

Japanese cooks have treated kasu as an ingredient since at least the eighth century, documented in the Taiho Code as a commodity taxed alongside rice and salt. It's not a byproduct to them — it's the source material for a specific category of cooking that no other ingredient can replicate.

---

## What kasu actually contains

Sake fermentation is a dual fermentation process: koji mold (Aspergillus oryzae) converts rice starches into sugars, and yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) converts those sugars into alcohol. When pressing separates the sake from the solids, the kasu retains a significant portion of both the koji enzymes and the yeast — many of which remain active.

The practical consequence is that kasu is enzymatically powerful. Proteases break down proteins; amylases continue to work on residual starches. When you bury a piece of fish or meat in kasu, these enzymes penetrate the surface and begin digesting the protein structure — not enough to collapse it, but enough to tenderize the texture and create new amino acids on the surface that will brown more readily under heat.

The amino acid content is also high. Glutamic acid is present in significant concentration, which means kasu functions as an umami carrier — adding depth to anything it contacts, even in small quantities. The alcohol content (typically 8–10% in fresh kasu) acts as a solvent, helping extract and carry aromatic compounds into the ingredient being marinated.

Fresh kasu is sold in blocks and has a soft, pliable texture. Aged kasu (called neri kasu when blended smooth) is more concentrated and stronger in flavor. Kasu softens and becomes more liquid when warmed, making it easy to incorporate into sauces or soups.

## Kasuzuke: pickling in sake lees

The most direct application of kasu is kasuzuke — burying food in a kasu mixture to pickle and marinate it. The process is simple: the complexity comes from what the kasu does over time.

**Basic kasuzuke marinade:**
- 200g kasu
- 3 tablespoons white miso
- 2 tablespoons mirin
- 1 tablespoon sugar

Mix until smooth. For fish (salmon, cod, black cod), bury fillets completely in the kasu paste and refrigerate for 24–48 hours. For denser cuts of pork or chicken, 2–3 days is appropriate. The surface of the protein will take on a slight amber color from the yeast pigments, and the texture will be visibly more tender.

Before cooking, wipe or rinse the marinade off the surface. Cook over medium heat — the amino acids on the surface will brown quickly, faster than an unmarinated piece, and the sugar content in the marinade means it scorches easily. A lower temperature and more attention than usual.

**What you're actually doing:** The miso adds additional glutamate and acts as an emulsifier. The mirin and sugar balance the fermented sourness of the kasu. The combination creates a paste with enough water activity to facilitate enzymatic penetration while the salinity of the miso controls the process so the protein doesn't over-tenderize. The result in the finished dish is a piece of fish or pork with a distinctly different texture — moister, more yielding, with a savory depth on the surface that isn't present without the marinade.

## Kasu jiru: the soup

Kasu jiru is a miso soup enriched with dissolved kasu — a soup that's warming, deeply savory, and substantially more filling than regular miso soup because of the fat and protein content of the lees. It's traditionally a winter preparation, and it's excellent.

**Method:**
- Make a light dashi (kombu or kombu-katsuobushi)
- Add sliced daikon, burdock root (gobo), carrot, and a few pieces of pork belly or salted salmon
- Simmer until the vegetables are tender
- Dissolve 2–3 tablespoons kasu and 2–3 tablespoons white miso in a ladle of hot broth, then stir back into the pot
- Do not boil after adding the kasu — like miso, it loses its aromatic character at a full boil
- Finish with sliced konnyaku if using and adjust seasoning

The kasu dissolves into the broth but leaves a slight body — the soup is thicker and richer than straight dashi-miso soup, with a background note of fermented sweetness from the sake. Kasu jiru improves significantly when left overnight and reheated — the flavors consolidate.

## Western applications: what kasu does in a non-Japanese context

The enzymatic and umami properties of kasu don't stop working because the dish isn't Japanese. Two applications in particular translate directly.

**Kasu as a marinade for roast chicken.** Mix 100g kasu with 50g unsalted butter (softened), 1 tablespoon white miso, 1 teaspoon lemon zest, and a few thyme sprigs. Rub under and over the skin of a whole chicken and refrigerate uncovered for 24 hours before roasting. The enzymes work on the surface proteins; the butter carries the aromatics; the miso adds salt and depth. Roast at standard temperature — the surface will color faster than a plain chicken, so reduce to 160°C after browning to avoid scorching.

The result is a chicken with notably more developed surface flavor and a crispier skin. The fermented character bakes off during roasting, leaving only the amino acids and browned flavor compounds.

**Kasu dissolved in pasta broth.** For a quick pasta sauce with depth, dissolve 1 tablespoon kasu in 100ml of hot pasta cooking water before using it to finish the sauce. The kasu adds body (from the dissolved proteins and starches) and glutamate without any visible trace in the final dish. It functions in this context exactly as a small amount of miso would — invisible but present in the flavor.

This technique works well with pasta dishes that already use savory elements: a white bean pasta, a broccoli-anchovy sauce, a simple aglio e olio that needs more body. The kasu doesn't make it taste Japanese. It makes it taste more like itself.

## Where to find kasu

Sake lees are available at most Japanese grocery stores, often in the refrigerated or freezer section near miso and tofu. They're occasionally sold at Korean markets as well. Online Japanese grocery retailers stock them year-round.

Fresh kasu is sold in blocks (often vacuum-packed) ranging from 200g to 1kg. Aged or blended kasu (neri kasu) is smoother and more concentrated — start with less when substituting in recipes. Store in the refrigerator, where it keeps for several months; it can also be frozen.

Look for kasu labeled simply as 酒粕 (sake kasu). The quality reflects the sake producer — kasu from premium sake breweries is significantly more aromatic and complex. Gekkeikan and Hakutsuru are widely available brands; regional breweries in Japanese grocery stores often carry more interesting options.

---

The case for kasu is the same case you could make for miso, fish sauce, or aged cheese: these are ingredients that took months to develop their character, and borrowing that character for twenty minutes of marinade time is one of the most efficient moves in cooking. The enzymes do the work; you just have to be patient enough to let them.
