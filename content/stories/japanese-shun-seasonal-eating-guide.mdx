Shun (旬) is the point in the year when a food is at its best: the most flavour, the best texture, and usually the lowest price because there is plenty of it. In Japanese cooking, eating a food during its shun is not a trend or a diet. It is the default. Menus change with the months, shops label produce by season, and many people can tell you roughly when bamboo shoots, saury or mandarins are best.

This guide covers what the word means, how it shapes the way Japan eats, a season-by-season list of signature foods, and how to use the same idea in a Western kitchen.

---

## What Shun Means: Hashiri, Sakari and Nagori

Shun is usually described in three phases.

- **Hashiri (走り), the first arrivals.** The earliest catch or harvest of the year. Quantities are small and prices are high. Hashiri foods are prized because they announce the new season, not because they taste their best. They are often lighter and more delicate than what comes later.
- **Sakari (盛り), the peak.** The food is abundant, at full flavour, and at its most affordable. This is what most people mean when they say something is "in shun."
- **Nagori (名残), the lingering end.** The last of the season. Nagori foods are eaten with a sense of farewell, and a cook may serve one alongside the hashiri of the next season to show the change.

Timing varies. Japan runs a long way from north to south, so the same food can peak weeks apart in different regions, and weather shifts it from year to year. The months given here are approximate, not fixed dates.

## How Shun Shapes the Table

**Kaiseki.** The formal multi-course meal is built around the season. The ingredients, the dishes they are served on and the garnishes all point to the time of year: a few maple leaves on an autumn plate, for example. A kaiseki chef may put a hashiri ingredient and a nagori ingredient in the same meal to mark the turn of the season.

**Izakaya.** Many izakaya have a printed menu plus a handwritten board of daily or seasonal specials, often labelled osusume (recommended). That board is where the shun food is, and it is usually the best thing to order. Our [izakaya guide](/journal/japanese-izakaya-guide) explains how the menu is laid out.

**Home cooking.** At home the idea is simpler. You buy what is plentiful and cheap at the supermarket that week, and you cook it simply so its flavour carries the dish. A plain salt-grilled fish, a bowl of rice cooked with one seasonal ingredient, or blanched greens with soy and bonito flakes are typical shun dishes.

## Spring (roughly March to May)

- **Takenoko (bamboo shoots).** Fresh shoots are boiled, often with rice bran, to remove bitterness. Then they are simmered with wakame (wakatake-ni) or cooked into takenoko gohan, rice cooked with the shoots.
- **Sansai (mountain vegetables).** Wild plants such as fuki-no-to (butterbur buds), tara-no-me (angelica tree shoots) and warabi (bracken). Fuki-no-to and tara-no-me are often eaten as tempura, which tames their bitterness. Warabi has to be soaked with wood ash or baking soda (*aku-nuki*) before eating, because raw bracken is toxic. Some sansai have poisonous look-alikes, so wild plants should only be foraged by people who can identify them with certainty.
- **Nanohana (rapeseed blossoms).** Tender stems and buds, blanched and dressed with mustard and soy (karashi-ae) or served as ohitashi.
- **Hotaru-ika (firefly squid).** Small squid, best known from Toyama Bay. Usually boiled and eaten whole with vinegared miso. See our [hotaru-ika guide](/journal/hotaru-ika-firefly-squid-guide-japan).
- **Sakura sweets.** Sakura mochi, a pink sweet filled with bean paste and wrapped in a salt-pickled cherry leaf, appears around cherry blossom season. In Kansai it's made from coarse glutinous rice (*domyoji*); in Kanto it's a thin crepe (*chomeiji*).
- **Hatsu-gatsuo (first bonito).** The first skipjack of the season, leaner than the autumn fish. Often served as tataki: seared outside, raw inside, with ginger, garlic and ponzu.

## Summer (roughly June to August)

- **Unagi (eel) on doyo no ushi no hi.** The midsummer "day of the ox," which falls on a different date each year, usually in late July. Eel is grilled with a sweet soy glaze (kabayaki) and served over rice. The custom is meant to build stamina for the heat.
- **Edamame.** Young soybeans boiled in salted water and eaten from the pod, most famously with cold beer.
- **Ayu (sweetfish).** A small river fish. Salt-grilled on skewers, often with a vinegar sauce made with water pepper (tade-zu). Fishing seasons open at different times on different rivers.
- **Somen.** Very thin wheat noodles served ice cold with a dipping sauce. See our [somen guide](/journal/japanese-somen-noodles-guide).
- **Eggplant (nasu).** Grilled whole and peeled (yaki-nasu), or glazed with sweet miso as [nasu dengaku](/journal/nasu-dengaku-japanese-eggplant-miso-glaze). Eggplant carries on into autumn, when many people say it is at its best.
- **Cucumber.** Lightly pickled (asazuke), dressed in vinegar as [sunomono](/journal/sunomono-recipe-japanese-cucumber-salad), or served at izakaya as morokyu, raw cucumber with a dab of moromi miso.

## Autumn (roughly September to November)

- **Sanma (Pacific saury).** Salt-grilled whole and served with grated daikon and a squeeze of citrus. Catches have fallen sharply in recent years, so price and availability vary from season to season. See our [sanma guide](/journal/sanma-pacific-saury-japanese-autumn-fish-guide).
- **Matsutake.** An aromatic wild mushroom, and an expensive one, especially when it comes from Japan. Cooked in matsutake gohan or dobin-mushi, a clear broth steamed in a small teapot.
- **Kuri (chestnuts).** Cooked into kuri gohan (chestnut rice), or used in sweets such as kuri kinton.
- **Sweet potato (satsumaimo).** Eaten as yaki-imo, roasted slowly until the inside turns soft and sweet. In some areas it is still sold from trucks.
- **Shinmai (new-crop rice).** The first rice of the harvest, sold with a "shinmai" label. It holds more moisture, so many cooks use slightly less water and eat it plain to taste the difference. Our [short-grain rice guide](/journal/best-short-grain-rice-for-japanese-and-korean-cooking) covers what to buy outside Japan.
- **Modori-gatsuo (returning bonito).** Skipjack on its way back south, fattier than the spring fish. Eaten as sashimi or tataki.

Autumn is also mushroom-rice season more broadly. Our [maitake butter rice](/recipes/japanese-maitake-butter-rice) is an easy version with a mushroom you can buy year-round.

## Winter (roughly December to February)

- **Fugu (pufferfish).** Served as paper-thin sashimi (tessa) or in a hot pot (tecchiri). Parts of the fish are deadly. In Japan it may only be prepared by chefs who hold a fugu licence. Eat it only at a licensed restaurant, and never try to prepare it at home.
- **Buri (yellowtail), especially kanburi.** Winter yellowtail is fatty. Toyama's Himi buri is one well-known example. Eaten as sashimi, teriyaki, or buri daikon, yellowtail simmered with daikon.
- **Daikon.** At its sweetest in winter. Simmered in dashi in [oden](/recipes/oden-japanese-winter-hotpot) and nimono, or grated raw as a garnish.
- **Yuzu.** The zest goes into soups, simmered dishes and ponzu. Some people take a yuzu bath (yuzu-yu) on the winter solstice. See our [yuzu guide](/journal/yuzu-citrus-what-it-is-and-how-to-use-it).
- **Nabe.** Hot pot at the table, built from whatever is in season. Our [guide to nabe types](/journal/japanese-nabe-hotpot-types) covers the main styles, and the [donabe guide](/journal/best-donabe-japanese-clay-pot-how-to-choose) covers the clay pot.
- **Mikan.** Small, easy-peel mandarins, often eaten by the handful at home.

Winter also brings New Year, with its own set of foods, from soba on New Year's Eve to osechi boxes. See [Japanese New Year food](/journal/japanese-new-year-food-toshikoshi-soba-ozoni-osechi) and our [soba guide](/journal/soba-noodle-recipe-cold-and-hot-preparations).

## How to Cook by Shun Outside Japan

You don't need Japanese ingredients to cook this way. The idea travels.

- **Shop by what is cheap and plentiful.** A sudden drop in price is often the sign of a food's sakari. Farmers' markets make the seasons easier to see than supermarkets do.
- **Watch for the first and last of each season.** The first asparagus, the last tomatoes of autumn. Serving a hashiri or nagori food is a simple way to mark the season at dinner.
- **Cook simply at the peak.** Peak produce needs little help. Salt-grill fish, blanch greens and dress them with soy and sesame, cook rice with one seasonal ingredient.
- **Use Japanese methods on local food.** Simmer squash in dashi, make mushroom rice in a donabe, or put local fish and greens in a winter nabe. Our [hot pot setup guide](/journal/best-hot-pot-setup-for-home-electric-vs-induction-vs-butane) covers the equipment.
- **Ask what is local.** A fishmonger or farmer can usually tell you what is at its best this week. Local seasons will not match Japan's, and that is the point.
