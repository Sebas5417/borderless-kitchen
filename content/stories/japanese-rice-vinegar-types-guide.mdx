Vinegar in Japanese cooking is almost entirely rice-based — *su* (酢) is the general word for vinegar, and in the Japanese context it nearly always means rice vinegar. But rice vinegar is not a single product. The various types — clear komezu, seasoned awasezu, black kurozu, and regional varieties — have meaningfully different flavors and applications.

## Komezu (米酢): Pure Rice Vinegar

*Komezu* is the foundational Japanese rice vinegar — made from fermented rice wine (sake or *mirin*-like liquid) converted to acetic acid through bacteria. It is:

**Color:** Pale golden to very light amber — much lighter than most Western cider or wine vinegars.

**Flavor:** Mild, slightly sweet, with clean acid and no sharp harshness. The sweetness distinguishes it from distilled white vinegar — komezu has residual rice sugars that contribute gentle sweetness to the acid.

**Acidity:** Typically 4.2% acetic acid (Japanese standard is minimum 4%, compared to Western vinegars which are often 5-7%). This mild acidity is part of why komezu doesn't overwhelm delicate Japanese preparations.

**Primary uses:**
- Sushi rice (*shari*): The single most important application. Sushi rice is seasoned with a mixture of rice vinegar + sugar + salt dissolved and cooled before mixing into hot cooked rice.
- Sunomono (vinegared salad): Cucumber, wakame, and other vegetables dressed with diluted rice vinegar, soy sauce, and sugar.
- Quick pickles (*pickled vegetables*): A brief soak in diluted rice vinegar with salt and sugar produces tsukemono-style pickles in 30-60 minutes.
- Dressings and sauces: Rice vinegar contributes acid without overpowering.
- Ponzu sauce: Combined with citrus juice and soy sauce.

**Recommended brands:** Mizkan, Marukan, and Kikkoman all produce reliable komezu. Mizkan's Honteri (which is actually a combination product — see below) is widely sold but not pure rice vinegar. For pure komezu, look for "rice vinegar" without added seasoning.

## Awasezu (合わせ酢): Pre-Seasoned Rice Vinegar

*Awasezu* (or *sushi-zu*, sushi vinegar) is rice vinegar with sugar, salt, and sometimes kelp extract (*kombu*) already dissolved in it. It's a convenience product designed specifically for seasoning sushi rice without having to make your own blend.

**Common awasezu blends:**
- Rice vinegar + sugar + salt (basic)
- Rice vinegar + sugar + salt + kombu extract (adds mild umami)

**Use:** Pour directly over hot cooked rice at the ratio specified on the package (typically 2-3 tablespoons per 2 cups uncooked rice). Let the rice absorb and season without further preparation needed.

**When to use vs. making your own:** Awasezu is convenient and consistent. Making your own blend allows adjustment for personal taste (more or less sweet, more or less salty) and quality of ingredients. At a restaurant, most chefs make their own vinegar blend. At home, awasezu is completely acceptable.

## Kurozu (黒酢): Japanese Black Vinegar

*Kurozu* is black rice vinegar — an aged, concentrated rice vinegar made by a different process than standard komezu. The traditional production center is Fukuyama, Kagoshima Prefecture, where *moromi* (fermenting rice mash) in large clay *tsubo* (ceramic urns) is left to ferment and then age outdoors for 6 months to 3 years.

**Color:** Dark amber to nearly black, significantly darker than komezu. The color comes from melanoidins produced during long fermentation and the Maillard-like reactions during aging.

**Flavor:** Much more complex and intense than komezu. Aged kurozu has notes of dried fruit, soy, and an almost balsamic-like depth. Less sharp than standard rice vinegar despite higher concentration. Some aged kurozu has an almost wine-like complexity.

**Amino acid content:** Long fermentation breaks proteins down into amino acids, including glutamic acid. Good kurozu has measurable umami from this amino acid content — another dimension beyond standard rice vinegar.

**Primary uses:**
- **Chinese-influenced cooking in Japan:** Kurozu is associated with Japanese Chinese restaurant cooking — sweet and sour preparations, braised pork.
- **Health tonic:** Kurozu is widely sold in Japan as a health drink, diluted in water. The amino acid and polyphenol content is marketed for various health benefits (evidence is limited but tradition is strong).
- **Braising and reduction:** Kurozu's complexity makes it excellent in braising liquids for pork and short ribs where you want acid with depth.
- **Dressings with presence:** Where komezu disappears into a dressing, kurozu makes a statement.

**Price:** Significantly more expensive than komezu due to long aging. Traditional Fukuyama kurozu is considered premium.

## Sanbaizu and Nihaizu: Classic Dressing Ratios

These aren't vinegar types but rather classic Japanese vinegar dressing ratios — important to know because they appear in recipes without explanation:

**Nihaizu (二杯酢):** Two-cup vinegar — equal parts rice vinegar and soy sauce. A basic dressing for cooked ingredients.

**Sanbaizu (三杯酢):** Three-cup vinegar — equal parts rice vinegar, soy sauce, and mirin. The standard sunomono dressing. The mirin adds sweetness and slight stickiness.

**Ponzu (ポン酢):** Not a ratio but a sauce — rice vinegar + citrus juice (yuzu, sudachi, or lime) + soy sauce + dashi. Can be bought premade (bottled ponzu) or made fresh.

## Other Japanese Rice Vinegars

**Akazu (赤酢):** Red vinegar, made from *sake kasu* (sake lees) rather than rice. The fermentation of sake lees produces a vinegar with deeper color, more complex flavor, and different acid character than komezu. Traditional Edo-period sushi was made with akazu rather than komezu — the brownish color of akazu-seasoned rice gave sushi rice its older characteristic brown tint. Some high-end sushi restaurants in Tokyo still use akazu.

**Junmaishu vinegar:** Made from junmai sake (pure rice sake without added alcohol), producing a particularly clean, pure-tasting rice vinegar.

**Regional barrel-aged rice vinegars:** Some Japanese vinegar producers age their rice vinegar in wooden barrels (similar to balsamic tradition), producing complex aged rice vinegar products.

## How to Choose

| Need | Use |
|------|-----|
| Sushi rice (quick) | Awasezu (pre-seasoned) |
| Sushi rice (preferred) | Komezu + dissolve your own sugar + salt |
| Sunomono dressing | Komezu in sanbaizu ratio |
| Quick pickles | Komezu diluted with water |
| Ponzu sauce | Komezu + citrus juice + soy sauce + dashi |
| Rich braising | Kurozu |
| Health tonic | Kurozu diluted in water |
| Traditional sushi aesthetic | Akazu (difficult to find outside Japan) |

**Essential pantry choice:** One bottle of komezu covers 90% of Japanese vinegar needs. Add kurozu only if you cook Japanese-style braised meat preparations or want to explore aged vinegar character.

---

The mildness of Japanese rice vinegar relative to most Western vinegars is not a lack — it's a precision tool for acidifying food without overwhelming it. Once you've adjusted to cooking with komezu, standard Western distilled vinegar seems harsh, and most recipes that call for rice vinegar have been calibrated for its specific gentleness.
