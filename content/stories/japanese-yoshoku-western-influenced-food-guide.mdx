*Yoshoku* (洋食) translates as "Western food." In practice it means a specific family of dishes that Japan adapted from European and American cooking over the last 150 years: omurice, hamburg steak, pork cutlet, croquettes, curry rice, spaghetti with ketchup.

They started as foreign food. Today they're Japanese comfort food, eaten at home, in school lunches and at restaurants that have served the same menu for decades.

---

## Where Yoshoku Came From

For most of the Edo period (1603–1868), Japan limited contact with the outside world, and eating the meat of four-legged animals was officially discouraged, partly for Buddhist reasons.

That changed after the Meiji Restoration of 1868. The new government set out to modernise the country along Western lines, and diet was part of it. Meat was promoted as a way to build a stronger population and army, and in 1872 the Emperor was publicly reported to eat beef, which is widely cited as the moment the old taboo officially ended.

Western-style restaurants opened in Yokohama, Kobe and Tokyo, first serving foreigners and the elite. Over the following decades, Japanese cooks adjusted the dishes: deep-frying instead of pan-frying, sauces that went with rice, food cut to eat with chopsticks. Restaurants that specialise in this food are called *yoshokuya* (洋食屋), and some of the oldest, such as Rengatei in Ginza (founded 1895), are still open.

## The Classic Dishes

### Omurice

Fried rice seasoned with ketchup, usually with chicken and onion, wrapped in or topped with a thin omelette and finished with more ketchup or a demi-glace sauce. Several restaurants claim to have invented it, including Rengatei in Tokyo and Hokkyokusei in Osaka, and the question isn't settled. Our [omurice guide](/journal/omurice-japanese-omelette-rice-guide) covers both the wrapped style and the soft, split-open omelette version.

### Hambagu (hamburg steak)

*Hanbāgu* is a pan-fried patty of minced beef or a beef and pork mix, bound with breadcrumbs, milk, egg and sautéed onion. It's softer and juicier than a burger patty and is served on a plate, not in a bun, usually with demi-glace or a grated daikon and ponzu sauce, plus rice.

### Tonkatsu and katsu

A pork cutlet coated in flour, egg and panko, then deep-fried. It comes from the European breaded cutlet (*côtelette*, which became *katsuretsu*, then *katsu*). The Japanese version is thick, deep-fried rather than pan-fried, sliced into strips so it can be eaten with chopsticks, and served with shredded raw cabbage, rice, miso soup and a thick, fruity brown sauce. Rengatei is usually credited with an early version, served with shredded cabbage, around the turn of the 20th century. It also became katsudon, simmered with egg over rice, and [chicken katsu](/journal/chicken-katsu-recipe-japanese-fried-chicken).

### Korokke

Croquettes, from the French *croquette*. The common home version is mashed potato with minced meat and onion, breaded in panko and fried. Cream korokke, made with a thick béchamel, is closer to the original.

### Curry rice

Japanese curry is a thick, mild, slightly sweet stew of meat, onion, carrot and potato, thickened with a flour-based roux and served next to rice. It's widely said to have reached Japan via the British, whose navy and trading ships carried a curry-powder version of Indian curry, and the Japanese navy adopted it as a standard meal. The full story is in our [Japanese curry history](/journal/japanese-curry-history-guide). Most households now use boxed roux blocks; we compared the main brands in [Golden Curry vs Vermont vs Java](/journal/best-japanese-curry-roux-golden-curry-vs-vermont-vs-java).

### Hayashi rice

Thinly sliced beef and onions simmered in a demi-glace and tomato sauce, served over rice. The origin of the name is disputed: one popular story ties it to Hayashi Yuteki, founder of the bookshop Maruzen, while another says it's a corruption of "hashed beef."

### Ebi fry

Large prawns, straightened, breaded in panko and deep-fried, usually served with tartar sauce. A fixture of bento boxes and set meals.

### Napolitan

Spaghetti stir-fried with ketchup, onion, green pepper, mushrooms and sliced ham or sausage, often finished with grated Parmesan and Tabasco. It's usually credited to Hotel New Grand in Yokohama during the American occupation after World War II, where the head chef is said to have made a tomato-sauce version inspired by the ketchup spaghetti that American soldiers ate. Versions made with ketchup itself then spread through cafés nationwide.

The name refers to Naples, but there's nothing like it in Naples. It's one of the oldest examples of Japanese-Italian cooking, and a good place to see the idea this site is built on: an Italian shape, cooked with a pantry the cook actually had.

### Doria

Rice, often butter rice or pilaf, topped with a creamy sauce and seafood or chicken, covered in cheese and baked until browned. It's also usually credited to Hotel New Grand, where the Swiss-born first head chef is said to have created it for a guest who was feeling unwell. It's essentially a rice gratin.

## What Makes It Japanese

A few things turn Western dishes into yoshoku.

**Rice.** Almost every yoshoku dish is eaten with rice, or has rice inside it, often with miso soup on the side. Sauces are built to season rice.

**Demi-glace.** The classic French brown sauce, simplified and often sweetened, is the house sauce of many yoshokuya. It goes over hambagu, omurice and hayashi rice.

**Worcestershire-style sauce.** Japan developed its own thick, sweet, fruity brown sauces from British Worcestershire sauce. Bull-Dog is the best-known brand, sold in thin *usutā*, medium *chūnō* and thick tonkatsu styles. A bottle sits on the table for katsu, korokke and fried food generally. You can also [make tonkatsu sauce at home](/journal/japanese-tonkatsu-sauce-recipe).

**Ketchup.** Ketchup is used as a cooking ingredient, not just a condiment: it seasons omurice rice and Napolitan, and its sweetness and acidity cover for tomatoes that weren't widely available. It also adds umami, which is part of why it works; see [the science of umami in Italian-Japanese cooking](/journal/the-science-of-umami-italian-japanese-fusion).

**Panko and shredded cabbage.** Coarse Japanese breadcrumbs give a lighter, crisper crust than fine Western crumbs (more in [panko vs breadcrumbs](/journal/panko-vs-breadcrumbs-difference)), and raw shredded cabbage is the standard side for anything fried.

## Where to Eat It in Japan

**Yoshokuya.** Small, often family-run restaurants that specialise in this food. Old ones in Tokyo, Yokohama and Kobe are worth seeking out. Expect a short menu and a lunch set with rice and soup.

**Kissaten.** Old-style coffee shops, many dating from the postwar decades. They're the natural home of Napolitan, alongside thick toast and curry rice.

**Family restaurants.** Chains such as Gusto and Royal Host serve hambagu, doria, omurice and curry at reasonable prices, and the Italian-style chain Saizeriya is famous for its cheap Milano-style doria. They're open late and have picture menus, which helps if you don't read Japanese.

**Department store food halls and butcher shops.** For korokke and katsu to take away.

## Cook It at Home

Most yoshoku uses ingredients you can buy anywhere. Start with:

- [Tonkatsu](/recipes/japanese-tonkatsu-pork-cutlet): the base for katsu everything.
