Sugar is sucrose — a disaccharide made of one glucose and one fructose molecule bonded together. When sugar melts and continues to heat, the sucrose molecules break apart and the resulting fragments react with each other in hundreds of different ways, producing the complex mixture of compounds we experience as caramel: sweet, bitter, nutty, toasty, slightly acidic, with a characteristic color ranging from pale gold to deep brown-black.

This is caramelization. It has nothing to do with proteins.

---

## Caramelization vs. the Maillard Reaction

These two browning mechanisms are constantly confused. They produce similar colors and similar complexity, but they are chemically distinct:

**Caramelization:** The thermal decomposition of sugars in the absence of proteins. Requires high temperatures (320°F+ / 160°C+). Produces hundreds of flavor compounds including furans, diacetyl, and various aldehydes. The browning of plain sugar syrup, the color in caramel sauce, the crust on crème brûlée.

**Maillard reaction:** A reaction between amino acids (from proteins) and reducing sugars, occurring at lower temperatures (250°F–350°F / 120°C–175°C). Produces melanoidins — the brown polymers that give bread crust, seared steak, roasted coffee, and toasted bread their color and characteristic flavor. This is why bread browns (proteins + sugars in the flour react) while pure sugar syrup does not undergo a Maillard reaction.

In practice, both often occur simultaneously — the crust on a roasted carrot involves both (the carrot's sugars caramelize; the proteins in the carrot undergo Maillard reactions). But understanding which mechanism is operating helps predict how things will behave and why.

## The Temperature Stages of Sugar

As a water-based sugar syrup is heated, the water evaporates progressively and the concentration of sugar increases. Different concentrations (measured by temperature) produce different textures when the syrup cools:

| Stage | Temperature | Behavior When Cooled | Use |
|-------|------------|---------------------|-----|
| Thread | 230–235°F (110–112°C) | Thin threads | Simple syrup |
| Soft ball | 235–240°F (113–116°C) | Soft, pliable ball | Fudge, fondant |
| Firm ball | 245–250°F (118–121°C) | Firm ball | Caramel candies |
| Hard ball | 250–265°F (121–129°C) | Hard ball | Nougat, marshmallow |
| Soft crack | 270–290°F (132–143°C) | Flexible threads | Taffy |
| Hard crack | 300–310°F (149–154°C) | Brittle | Lollipops, brittles, toffee |
| Caramel | 320–350°F (160–177°C) | Amber liquid | Caramel sauce, praline |
| Dark caramel | 350–375°F (177–190°C) | Deep brown | Complex sauces, coloring |
| Burnt | 400°F+ (204°C+) | Black, bitter | Discard |

A candy thermometer is essential for any sugar work beyond simple syrup — the difference between firm ball and hard ball is 15°F (8°C), but the resulting texture of a caramel candy is completely different.

## The Two Methods: Wet and Dry

**Wet method:** Combine sugar and water (typically 4:1 sugar to water by weight) in a heavy-bottomed pan. Heat over medium-high without stirring until the syrup reaches the target temperature. The water slows the process and gives more control; it all evaporates before the caramelization stage anyway.

**Dry method:** Add sugar directly to a dry heavy-bottomed pan and heat over medium heat. The sugar melts unevenly at first — stir gently to distribute heat. Once fully melted, stop stirring and watch carefully; it can burn quickly.

**Why you shouldn't stir:** Stirring a sugar syrup once it has reached about 235°F (113°C) can cause crystallization — sugar molecules nucleate around a seed crystal (a grain of undissolved sugar, a scratch in the pan, or a wooden spoon) and the entire batch seizes into a grainy mass. If this happens, the batch is usually lost. To prevent crystallization:
- Use a clean pan with no residual sugar crystals
- Brush down the sides of the pan with a pastry brush dipped in water to wash away any crystals that form on the sides
- Add a small amount of corn syrup or a few drops of lemon juice (the acid inverts the sucrose, preventing crystallization)
- Do not stir once the mixture is hot and clear

## Making Caramel Sauce

**Wet method caramel sauce:**
1. Combine 1 cup sugar and ¼ cup water in a heavy saucepan
2. Heat over medium-high without stirring until the syrup turns amber (340°F–350°F / 171°C–177°C)
3. Remove from heat immediately — residual heat continues cooking
4. Carefully add ½ cup warm heavy cream (it will bubble violently — the steam is extremely hot)
5. Stir until smooth, add butter and salt
6. Return to low heat if needed to dissolve any seized sugar

**The cream addition:** Adding cold cream to hot caramel causes explosive boiling as water in the cream converts to steam. The caramel also seizes temporarily as it cools below its melting point, then redissolves as stirring and heat continue. Adding warm cream reduces the temperature shock and the violence of the reaction.

**Salted caramel:** Salt amplifies the sweetness of caramel and adds complexity that cuts through the richness of the cream and butter. Add ½–1 teaspoon flaky salt at the end, after the sauce has cooled slightly.

## Dry Caramel: Praline and Brittle

For praline and brittle, sugar is caramelized without adding cream — the caramel is poured over nuts and allowed to set hard. The hard crack stage (300–310°F / 149–154°C) produces a brittle texture; continuing to caramelize past this produces the amber color and flavor without losing the hard texture (the sugar concentration is already above 99% at this stage).

**Baking soda trick for brittle:** Adding ¼ teaspoon baking soda to hot caramel before pouring causes a vigorous bubbling reaction (CO₂ from the alkali reacting with the acidic caramel). The resulting foam sets into a porous, airy texture — honeycomb toffee or "sponge candy" — rather than a dense, glassy brittle.

## Caramelization in Savory Cooking

Caramelized onions: the "caramelization" of onions that takes 45–60 minutes is largely Maillard reaction, not pure caramelization — the proteins and sugars in the onion react at lower temperatures than pure sugar caramelization. True caramelization contributes color and sweetness as the onion's natural sugars reach higher temperatures, but the majority of flavor development is Maillard.

Deglazed pan sauces: the fond (browned bits) on the bottom of a pan after searing meat contains both Maillard products and caramelized sugars from the meat's own sugar content. Deglazing dissolves and incorporates these compounds into the sauce.

Roasted vegetables: the browning and sweetening of roasted carrots, beets, and sweet potatoes is a combination of both mechanisms — their sugars caramelize while their proteins undergo Maillard reactions. Higher oven temperatures (400°F–425°F / 204°C–218°C) accelerate both.

---

Temperature is the tool. Know what temperature produces what result, apply heat deliberately, and the chemistry follows.
