Japanese rice is not a single ingredient with a single behavior. The three main varieties — uruchimai (standard short-grain), mochigome (glutinous rice), and haigamai/genmai (germ rice and brown rice) — behave differently from each other in ways that matter practically. The differences aren't about flavor so much as texture, and the reason for the texture differences comes down to one ratio: amylose to amylopectin.

Understanding that ratio explains why sushi rice stays together in a nigiri, why mochi stretches and becomes elastic, why brown rice remains separate and chewy, and why Italian risotto rice and Japanese sushi rice are essentially the same type of ingredient pursued from different directions.

---

## The starch distinction

Rice starch is composed of two polysaccharides: amylose and amylopectin. Both are chains of glucose molecules, but they're structured differently.

Amylose is a long, linear chain that tends to form a compact, crystalline structure when cooked and cooled. Rice high in amylose cooks to a drier, more separate, firmer texture — the grains stay distinct and firm. Long-grain rice (like jasmine or basmati) is high in amylose (around 23–28%), which is why it cooks fluffy and separate.

Amylopectin is a heavily branched molecule that absorbs water more readily, swells during cooking, and becomes sticky and cohesive when hot. It doesn't form the same crystalline structure as amylose on cooling, so high-amylopectin rice remains cohesive and somewhat sticky even at room temperature.

Japanese short-grain rice falls into two categories based on where they sit on this spectrum:

**Uruchimai** (short-grain standard rice) contains roughly 17–20% amylose, compared to long-grain rice's 23–28%. Lower amylose means more amylopectin, which means stickier, more cohesive grains — grains that cling to each other without becoming a paste.

**Mochigome** (glutinous or sweet rice) contains essentially zero amylose — it is almost entirely amylopectin. When cooked, the starch gelatinizes completely and becomes elastic and sticky. Pounding mochigome mechanically (the mochi-making process) further develops this elasticity into the stretchy, chewy texture of mochi. The name "glutinous" is slightly misleading — it contains no gluten. The glutinous refers to its glue-like behavior when cooked.

## Uruchimai: the everyday short-grain

Uruchimai is what almost all Japanese rice — for rice bowls, onigiri, sushi, and general cooking — refers to. The most common varieties are Koshihikari and Akitakomachi, both developed in postwar Japan and now widely exported.

**How to cook uruchimai correctly:**

The most important step is rinsing. Unrinsed Japanese rice is coated in excess surface starch from milling. Cooking it without rinsing produces gummy, over-sticky rice — the surface starch absorbs water disproportionately and creates a paste layer between each grain. Rinse three to four times in cold water, until the water runs mostly clear.

After rinsing, soak for 30 minutes before cooking. This allows even water absorption before heat is applied, producing evenly cooked grains.

Standard water ratio: 1 cup rice to 1.1–1.15 cups water (slightly less water than the grain itself, because Japanese rice will absorb additional water from steam). Cook covered over medium heat until steam appears, then reduce to the lowest possible heat for 12 minutes, then rest off heat, still covered, for 10 minutes. Do not lift the lid during cooking.

**When to use sushi rice vs standard bowl rice:** Sushi rice is uruchimai seasoned with a vinegar-sugar-salt mixture (sushi-su) and fanned while folded. The vinegar lowers the pH, which affects the texture of the starch slightly and gives sushi rice its characteristic glossy, cohesive quality. The underlying rice is the same; the seasoning changes the surface properties. For everyday rice bowls (gohan), standard unseasoned uruchimai is correct.

## Mochigome: glutinous rice

Mochigome has very different cooking requirements from uruchimai because of its complete amylopectin composition. It absorbs water differently and behaves differently during cooking.

**Soaking is mandatory:** Mochigome must be soaked for at least 4 hours, or ideally overnight, before cooking. Unlike uruchimai, which absorbs water adequately with a 30-minute soak, mochigome's dense, amylopectin-heavy structure requires extended soaking to hydrate evenly. Undersoaked mochigome cooks unevenly, with a hard center and soft exterior.

**Steaming rather than boiling:** Traditional mochigome preparation uses steaming rather than absorption cooking. Soaked and drained mochigome is spread in a bamboo steamer lined with cheesecloth and steamed for 35–40 minutes, with the optional addition of a sprinkle of water halfway through. The result is evenly cooked, glossy, very sticky grains that hold their shape but cling together completely.

**Uses:** Sekihan (red bean rice — mochigome cooked with adzuki beans, the beans' red pigment staining the rice pink), ohagi (sweet rice balls coated in red bean paste), rice dumplings, and as the starting material for mochi. For mochi, cooked mochigome is pounded until smooth and elastic — the mechanical action develops the amylopectin into a cohesive, stretchable mass.

## Haigamai and genmai: germ rice and brown rice

These are less about variety and more about milling degree.

**Genmai** (brown rice) is rice where the bran and germ layers have not been removed. It retains all the fiber, vitamins, and minerals that milling strips away, but it also retains the bran layer that acts as a barrier to water absorption. Genmai requires significantly more water (1:1.5 ratio) and longer cooking time (40–45 minutes) than white rice, and the texture remains noticeably chewier and more separate — the bran layer limits amylopectin swelling and gelatinization, so the grains stay distinct and firm.

**Haigamai** (germ rice) is a middle stage: the outer bran is removed but the germ is retained, preserving some of the nutritional value of brown rice with a texture closer to white rice. It cooks with slightly more water and time than white rice but much less than genmai.

Both are appropriate for dishes where a chewier, more textured rice is preferable — grain bowls, onigiri where the grains are mixed with other ingredients, or any preparation where the nuttier flavor of less-milled rice complements the other components.

## The Italian parallel: risotto rice

Arborio and Carnaroli — the two most common Italian risotto rices — are short-grain varieties with the same structural property that makes Japanese rice cohesive: a high amylopectin-to-amylose ratio.

The risotto technique deliberately exploits this amylopectin content. Adding hot stock in stages while stirring constantly agitates the grains, abrading the surface starch and releasing it into the cooking liquid. That released amylopectin disperses into the broth and, as the liquid reduces and concentrates, forms the creamy, cohesive emulsion that makes risotto thick without the addition of cream.

Carnaroli has a slightly higher amylose content than Arborio (about 24% vs 19%), which is why Carnaroli remains firmer at the center while still releasing enough surface starch to create creaminess — this is the chef's preference for risotto because it allows better control. Arborio releases more starch more quickly, which produces creamier but faster-softening risotto.

The structural logic is identical to what makes Japanese rice suitable for onigiri — high amylopectin content creates cohesion. Japanese cooks pursue that cohesion by cooking the rice carefully to preserve each grain while allowing them to stick together. Italian cooks deliberately develop that cohesion mechanically through stirring. Same material property, opposite techniques.

## Practical summary

The decision tree for Japanese rice is straightforward:

- Everyday rice bowls, onigiri, and sushi: uruchimai (Koshihikari or Akitakomachi), rinsed and soaked
- Mochi, sekihan, dumplings, sweet rice preparations: mochigome, soaked overnight, steamed
- Nuttier flavor, firmer texture, more nutritional value: genmai (brown rice), more water, longer cook
- Middle ground: haigamai (germ rice), closer to white rice with slightly more character
- Risotto: arborio or carnaroli, treated as a controlled amylopectin-release project

Rinsing changes everything in uruchimai. Soaking changes everything in mochigome. The correct water ratio changes everything in genmai. These are not optional steps — they determine whether the starch structure of each variety expresses its intended quality or turns to paste.
