There is a persistent idea that fresh pasta is better than dried pasta. This is wrong in the same way that saying lamb is better than beef is wrong — they are different things suited to different purposes, and choosing between them based on perceived quality hierarchy misses the point entirely.

---

## Fresh Pasta

Fresh pasta is made from soft wheat flour (typically 00 flour or all-purpose) and eggs. The dough is worked until smooth, rested to relax the gluten, then rolled thin and cut into shapes. The result is delicate, silky, slightly eggy, with a tender bite that cooks in 1–3 minutes.

**What fresh pasta is suited for:**
- Delicate, cream-based or butter-based sauces
- Rich, filled pastas (ravioli, tortellini, agnolotti) where the filling is the focus
- Lighter sauces where the pasta's texture is part of the dish — cacio e pepe made with fresh tonnarelli, pappardelle with braised short rib
- Dishes where the pasta is the showcase

**What fresh pasta is not suited for:**
- Chunky tomato sauces with bold aromatics — the delicate texture is overwhelmed
- Pasta al forno (baked dishes) — it becomes too soft
- Heavy meat ragù where the sauce is dominant — dried pasta holds up better

**Making fresh pasta:** The basic ratio is 100g flour to 1 egg per serving. Work the dough until smooth and elastic (8–10 minutes by hand or 3–4 minutes in a mixer), wrap in plastic, and rest for at least 30 minutes at room temperature. The rest allows the gluten to relax so the dough rolls thin without springing back. Roll to setting 5–6 on a pasta machine for tagliatelle or pappardelle; setting 7 for lighter cuts; setting 6–7 for lasagna sheets.

## Dried Pasta

Dried pasta is made from durum wheat semolina and water — no eggs. Durum wheat is a hard wheat with high protein content that produces a firmer, more toothsome texture when cooked. Good dried pasta is bronze-die extruded (pushed through bronze molds that leave a rough surface) and dried slowly at low temperatures. The rough surface catches and holds sauce; smooth, fast-dried pasta lets sauce slide off.

**What dried pasta is suited for:**
- Robust tomato sauces, ragù, and oil-based sauces
- Strongly flavored sauces with garlic, chili, anchovies, capers, olives
- Pasta alla norma, amatriciana, puttanesca, carbonara, all'arrabbiata
- Any dish where the sauce needs a sturdy vehicle

**How to cook dried pasta:**
Use more water than you think — pasta needs room to move. Salt the water aggressively (it should taste like mildly salty seawater — about 10g salt per liter). Bring to a full rolling boil before adding pasta. Cook 2 minutes less than the package indicates; finish cooking in the sauce.

**The pasta water:** Pasta water is starchy, salty, and an extraordinary sauce emulsifier. Reserve a cup before draining. It is not optional for cream- or oil-based sauces.

## Shape Logic: Why Certain Shapes Go With Certain Sauces

The matching of shape to sauce is not arbitrary or traditional for the sake of tradition. It is functional.

**Surface area and sauce:** Flat, thin pasta (linguine, spaghetti, tagliatelle) has less surface area than textured or ridged pasta. They work with lighter, more liquid sauces — olive oil, butter, clam sauce — that coat the strand. Chunky sauces slide off.

**Tubes and texture:** Rigatoni, penne, paccheri have ridged outer surfaces and hollow interiors. Chunky sauces — ragù, vegetables, beans — lodge inside the tube and catch on the ridges. A light butter sauce is wasted on rigatoni.

**Short shapes:** Fusilli, farfalle, orecchiette — their curves and folds trap sauce. Pesto clings to the spirals of fusilli. Orecchiette's bowl shape holds chunky vegetables (as in the classic orecchiette con cime di rapa).

**Broad, flat pasta:** Pappardelle and lasagne are suited for rich, slow-cooked sauces — braised meat ragù, game sauces, slow-cooked mushrooms. The width gives the sauce something to sit on; the pasta absorbs the richness.

**The practical rule:** If the sauce is chunky, use a shape that traps chunks. If the sauce is silky, use a strand or a thin flat shape. If the sauce is in between, use something ridged but not hollow.

## The Critical Technique: Finishing in the Sauce

The single most important technique in Italian pasta cooking is finishing the pasta in the sauce, not serving the pasta with sauce spooned over it.

**Why:** When pasta finishes cooking in the sauce, it absorbs flavor and releases starch directly into the sauce. The sauce thickens and emulsifies. The pasta and sauce become one dish rather than two separate components sitting next to each other.

**The process:**
1. Salt the pasta water heavily and bring to a boil.
2. Start the sauce while the pasta water heats.
3. Cook the pasta 2 minutes less than the package time.
4. Reserve a cup of pasta water before draining.
5. Transfer the pasta (with tongs, not through a colander) directly into the sauce. Carry some pasta water with it — this is fine.
6. Add a splash of pasta water and toss over medium heat for 1–2 minutes until the sauce coats the pasta and everything is glossy.
7. The pasta water starch emulsifies the fat and liquid in the sauce, creating a cohesive coating instead of a pooled sauce.

**This technique is the difference** between restaurant pasta and home pasta. The pasta water is the key. Professionals never drain pasta without reserving water first.

## Salt and Fat: The Two Levers

**Salt:** Pasta water that isn't salty enough produces bland pasta regardless of how good the sauce is. The pasta absorbs water as it cooks — it absorbs salt too. Under-salted pasta cannot be rescued by a well-seasoned sauce.

**Fat:** Fat is the emulsifier and carrier in almost every pasta sauce. Butter, olive oil, rendered guanciale or pancetta fat — fat carries flavor compounds from aromatics into the sauce and creates the glossy, coating texture that characterizes well-made pasta. Pasta sauces should not be low-fat; fat is the mechanism.

## The Classics and What They Teach

**Aglio e olio:** The simplest pasta — spaghetti, olive oil, garlic, chili, parsley. Everything depends on technique: garlic cooked slowly in oil until golden (not browned), pasta water used to emulsify the oil into a coherent sauce, pasta finished in the pan. The simplicity is the lesson.

**Carbonara:** Eggs, guanciale, pecorino, black pepper — no cream, ever. The egg yolks emulsify with pasta water and guanciale fat into a silky sauce. The heat of the pan and pasta cooks the eggs without scrambling them. A temperature lesson.

**Cacio e pepe:** Pecorino romano, black pepper, pasta water. Three ingredients. The technique — building the emulsion slowly with starchy water, never adding the cheese to a pan that's too hot — is the entire dish.

**Bolognese:** A meat ragù that cooks for 3–4 hours, reducing until the meat is tender and the sauce is thick and almost dry. Served with tagliatelle (never spaghetti in Bologna). The shape is wide enough to carry the substantial sauce.

Each classic is a lesson in one or two techniques applied at maximum clarity. Learning them in order is learning the vocabulary of pasta cooking.

---

Pasta is a cheap, fast, infinitely variable ingredient. The ceiling on how good it can be is set almost entirely by technique — the salt in the water, the finishing in the sauce, the pasta water used to build the emulsion. These are not secrets. They are the difference between ordinary pasta and the thing people describe as the best meal they had in Italy.
