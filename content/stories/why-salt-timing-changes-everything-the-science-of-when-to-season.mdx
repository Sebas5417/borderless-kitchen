Ask when to salt something and you'll get different answers depending on who you ask. Salt before, some say. Salt after. Never salt early or you'll dry it out. Always salt early so it penetrates. The contradictions persist because people are giving different answers to slightly different questions — salting a steak is not the same process as salting a cucumber, and both are different from salting pasta water.

The common thread is osmosis, and once you understand that, the timing rules for different ingredients make sense instead of seeming arbitrary.

---

## What salt actually does to food

Salt draws moisture out of cells through osmosis — water moves across a semi-permeable membrane from lower concentration (inside the cell) to higher concentration (the salty exterior). This is step one and it happens quickly, within minutes.

Step two is slower. If you wait, the salt dissolves into the extracted moisture, creating a concentrated brine on the surface. This brine is then drawn back into the food — osmosis reverses as the concentration gradient equalizes. Now the salt is inside the cells, not just coating the surface.

Step three (for proteins): the salt begins to denature some proteins, which reorganizes muscle fibers and changes the texture. This is why dry-brined meat is more tender than meat salted at the last minute — the protein structure has been altered before cooking begins.

These three stages happen in sequence, and each produces a different result if you stop there.

## Meat: dry-brining changes the texture, not just the flavor

The debate about early vs. late salting for meat was largely settled by food scientists around two decades ago, but the myth that salting early dries out meat persists.

Here's what actually happens when you salt a steak early:

**Under 30 minutes:** The osmotic pull draws moisture to the surface — you'll see droplets. If you cook it now, that surface moisture steams the meat instead of allowing a crust to form. This is the worst window: the salt hasn't penetrated, and the moisture is working against you.

**30 minutes to 1 hour:** The surface moisture is partly reabsorbed. You're in a middle zone. Better than nothing but not optimal.

**Over 1 hour (ideally overnight):** Full osmotic reversal. The brine has been drawn back in. The surface is visibly drier than unsalted meat — the moisture has redistributed inward. The proteins have been partially denatured by the salt. Result: meat that seasons evenly from exterior to interior, a drier surface that forms a better crust, and a more tender texture.

**The pellicle.** The dry surface formed after extended salting is called the pellicle. It's not just dried-out meat — it's a surface that has lost free moisture while retaining structural moisture inside. It reacts to heat differently than a wet surface. The Maillard reaction (browning) happens immediately because there's no steam barrier. The crust forms faster, more evenly, and with less moisture loss during cooking.

Dry-brine rule: Salt 24-48 hours before cooking for thick cuts. At minimum, 1 hour. Avoid the 15-30 minute window for searing.

For fish, the window is compressed — fish proteins denature faster and the flesh is more delicate. 15-20 minutes before cooking is enough. Overnight salted fish starts to cure, which may or may not be your intention.

## Pasta water: it should taste like you made a mistake

The standard instruction — "salt the pasta water until it tastes like the sea" — is correct but under-specified. Pasta water should taste aggressively salty, more than you think is reasonable, because pasta absorbs a fraction of the water it's cooked in.

The mechanism: pasta is a porous starch matrix. As it hydrates in boiling water, it absorbs both water and the dissolved salt with it. The amount of salt in the cooking water directly determines the salt content of the cooked pasta. This is why pasta from a properly salted pot tastes seasoned throughout, and pasta from an under-salted pot tastes bland regardless of how well you sauce it.

The ratio: roughly 10g of salt per liter of water — about 2 teaspoons of kosher salt. For a large pot of pasta water (4 liters), that's 40g, which is a substantial amount. Taste it. It should be clearly salty, not pleasant salty — more than you'd salt a soup, close to what you'd imagine seawater to taste like.

The salt should go in after the water boils, not before. This is practical, not chemical — dissolving a large amount of salt in cold water takes time and produces a gritty residue. Add to boiling water, it dissolves immediately.

## Vegetables: salt for texture or flavor, but not both at once

Salting vegetables produces different results depending on when you do it.

**Salt before cooking for texture control.** Cucumbers, zucchini, eggplant, and cabbage have high water content that can make them soggy when cooked or dressed. Salting ahead — 20-30 minutes, sometimes longer — draws out the excess moisture before you add anything else. The vegetables compress slightly, their texture becomes firmer, and they absorb dressings and sauces more cleanly instead of diluting them with released water.

This is the technique behind tzatziki (salted cucumber), ratatouille (salted eggplant), and coleslaw that doesn't turn into a puddle. It's also the basis for tsukemono — Japanese pickled vegetables.

**Salt during cooking for flavor integration.** Adding salt to sweating onions or softening carrots helps them cook faster (osmosis softens the cell walls) and distributes flavor throughout the vegetable rather than coating the surface.

**Salt at the end for presence.** Finishing salt — flaky sea salt over a salad, a pinch of kosher salt over roasted vegetables just before serving — adds sharp, distinct salinity that doesn't integrate into the food. This is a textural and sensory move, not a seasoning move. You taste the salt separately.

The mistake is treating these as equivalent. You can't compensate for under-salting during cooking by adding more salt at the end — the textures and distribution are different.

## The Japanese approaches: shio koji and miso

Japanese cooking extends salt timing beyond the familiar into territory that doesn't have a Western equivalent.

**Shio koji.** Koji (Aspergillus oryzae, the same mold used in sake and miso fermentation) produces enzymes — protease and amylase — that break down proteins and starches. Shio koji mixes koji with salt and a small amount of water, then ferments for 1-2 weeks. Applied to meat or fish, the protease enzymes pre-digest the protein matrix over several hours, tenderizing more thoroughly than salt alone.

Marinating chicken thighs in shio koji overnight produces a different texture than salt-only brining — more yielding, less chewy — because the enzymatic action has worked through the protein before the heat does. The Maillard reaction on the surface is also enhanced because the amylase has freed sugars from the starch content of any coating or marinade.

**Miso as a salt delivery system.** Miso's salt content varies by type (white miso: ~5-10% salt, darker varieties up to 12-14%), but its function as a salt source is secondary to its enzymatic content. Miso-marinated black cod (the Nobu dish that made this technique famous outside Japan) works because the salt preserves and seasons the fish while the miso enzymes — still active at refrigerator temperatures — tenderize the flesh over 2-3 days. The caramelization you see on miso-glazed anything is both Maillard (protein browning) and caramelization (sugar browning), because the miso has introduced sugars to the surface while the salt has dried it out slightly.

## Practical rules, summarized

**Meat:**
- Thick cuts (steak, pork chops): salt 24-48 hours ahead. 1 hour minimum.
- Poultry: salt overnight uncovered in the refrigerator. The exposed skin dries for better crisping.
- Fish: 15-20 minutes before cooking. Not overnight unless you intend to cure it.
- Avoid the 15-30 minute window for searing anything — the surface moisture works against crust formation.

**Pasta and grains:**
- Pasta water: 10g salt per liter, added once boiling. Taste it — should be clearly, uncomfortably salty.
- Rice: salt is not traditional in Japanese rice cooking. If you're adding it (for Western applications), add it to the water before cooking.

**Vegetables:**
- High-water vegetables before cooking or dressing: salt 20-30 minutes ahead, then rinse or press out the liquid.
- Vegetables you're sweating or sautéing: salt as they go in to help them soften.
- Finishing: flaky salt added just before serving, for distinct salinity.

**The fermented salt extension:**
- Shio koji: use instead of salt for overnight marination of chicken, pork, or white fish. Enzymatic tenderizing plus salt seasoning.
- Miso marinade: 2-3 days for fatty fish, 1 day for chicken. Wipe off before cooking to prevent burning.

---

The underlying principle across all of these: salt moves through food over time. The question is always what you want the movement to accomplish — flavor distribution, texture modification, moisture management, or surface development. Choosing when to salt is choosing which of these outcomes you want, and whether you want them at all.
