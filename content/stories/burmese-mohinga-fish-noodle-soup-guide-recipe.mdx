Most national dishes are lunch or dinner foods. Mohinga is breakfast — eaten before dawn by monks, before work by laborers, before school by children. Street vendors begin serving mohinga in the early morning hours across Myanmar's cities and towns, from Yangon to Mandalay to Bagan. The smell of lemongrass and fish broth over an open fire is one of the earliest sensory memories for most Burmese people.

The dish is conceptually simple — fish soup over noodles — but the execution involves multiple layers of complexity: a paste of lemongrass, shallots, garlic, and ginger; fish boiled and flaked; banana stem that thickens the broth and adds texture; chickpea flour and roasted rice flour that give the broth its characteristic body; a constellation of toppings that each mohinga vendor arranges differently.

No two mohinga recipes are identical. Regional variations across Myanmar shift the fish species, the toppings, and the spice level. This is Burmese cooking's equivalent of what different New Orleans chefs do with gumbo — the form is shared; the expression is individual.

## The Broth

The depth of mohinga's flavor comes from three parallel preparations:

**The aromatics paste:** Lemongrass stalks, shallots, garlic, ginger, and sometimes galangal are ground into a paste and fried in oil until fragrant and golden. This is the flavor base.

**The fish:** Typically catfish in Myanmar (a freshwater fish available across the Irrawaddy river system). The fish is poached first, then flaked; the poaching liquid becomes the base for the broth. Fish sauce adds additional depth.

**The thickeners:** Toasted rice flour (rice dry-roasted until golden, then ground) and chickpea flour (toasted similarly) are added to the broth and cooked in, giving it a distinctive opaque, slightly thick texture — not gelatinous, but bodied.

## The Banana Stem

One of the most specific ingredients in mohinga: the inner core of the banana plant stem, thinly sliced and cooked in the broth. It absorbs the broth flavor and adds a mild, slightly astringent vegetal note. Outside Myanmar and Burmese communities, it can be found at some Southeast Asian grocery stores.

Without banana stem: hearts of palm is the most common substitute, approximating the texture.

## The Toppings

Mohinga toppings vary by vendor, but typically include:
- Split pea or chickpea fritters, sliced (pe kyaw)
- Boiled eggs, halved
- Crispy fried shallots
- Fish sauce
- Lime
- Dried chili flakes
- Fresh coriander

**Recipe: Mohinga (Serves 6)**

**The paste:**
- 3 lemongrass stalks, white part only, roughly chopped
- 6 shallots, roughly chopped
- 6 cloves garlic
- 2cm fresh ginger
- 1 teaspoon turmeric
- 1/2 teaspoon paprika

Pound or blend into a paste.

**The broth:**
- 500g catfish fillets (or other mild freshwater fish; tilapia, snapper)
- 2 liters water
- 200g banana stem inner core, sliced thin then cut in short pieces (or hearts of palm)
- 3 tablespoons fish sauce
- 3 tablespoons toasted rice flour (dry toast rice in a pan until golden, grind)
- 2 tablespoons chickpea flour, toasted
- 3 tablespoons vegetable oil
- Salt

**For serving:**
- 400g thin rice vermicelli noodles, cooked per package and rinsed
- Boiled eggs, halved
- Crispy fried shallots
- Lime wedges
- Fish sauce
- Dried chili flakes
- Chickpea fritters or crackers (optional)

**Method:**

1. Poach fish in 2 liters water for 10 minutes. Remove fish; flake, removing bones. Reserve poaching liquid.

2. Heat oil in a large pot. Fry the paste over medium heat, stirring, 8–10 minutes until golden and very fragrant.

3. Add fish poaching liquid to the pot. Bring to a simmer.

4. Add flaked fish and banana stem. Cook 15 minutes.

5. Whisk toasted rice flour and chickpea flour with a little cold water to form a slurry. Add to broth, stirring. Simmer 10 minutes; the broth will thicken and turn slightly opaque.

6. Add fish sauce. Taste; add salt if needed.

7. To serve: place noodles in each bowl. Ladle hot broth over them. Add boiled egg half, crispy shallots. Serve with lime, fish sauce, and chili on the side.
