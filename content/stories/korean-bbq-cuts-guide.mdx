Korean BBQ restaurants make it look complicated: a dozen cuts on the menu, a grill in the table, a server with scissors. At home it comes down to one question. Which cut goes on the grill, and how is it prepared?

Korean butchery divides an animal differently from American butchery, so names don't always match one to one. The US equivalents below are the closest common matches, not exact translations. When in doubt, show your butcher a photo.

Two rules cover almost everything. Marinated cuts are sliced thin and cooked over medium-high heat, because the sugar burns. Plain cuts are about the quality and fat of the meat, and they get salt, a dip and very little else.

## Beef

### Galbi (bone-in short rib)

The best-known Korean BBQ cut. Traditional galbi is beef short rib cut so the meat is butterflied or "accordion" cut away from the bone into one long, thin sheet that stays attached at one end. **US equivalent:** bone-in beef short ribs, English cut. Most butchers won't butterfly it for you, so it's easier to buy pre-cut at a Korean market. **Marinade:** yes, usually soy sauce, sugar, garlic, sesame oil and grated Asian pear. **Thickness:** about ¼ inch once unrolled. **To grill:** medium-high heat, turning often, until browned at the edges. Watch for burning sugar. For the easier flanken version, see LA galbi below.

### LA galbi (flanken cut)

The same short rib, cut across the bones instead of along them, so each strip has three or four small oval bone sections (as in the photo above). It's the easiest galbi to find in the US. **US equivalent:** flanken-cut short ribs. **Marinade:** yes, the same as galbi. **Thickness:** roughly ⅓ to ½ inch. **To grill:** a few minutes per side over medium-high heat until caramelized. Cut between the bones with scissors to serve. Full method: [Korean galbi recipe](/recipes/korean-galbi-grilled-short-ribs) and the [galbi story](/journal/korean-galbi-recipe).

### Bulgogi (thin ribeye or sirloin)

Thin slices of tender beef in a sweet soy marinade. **US equivalent:** ribeye or sirloin; top round is a cheaper option. **Marinade:** yes, soy, sugar, garlic, sesame oil, onion and pear. **Thickness:** very thin, about ⅛ inch or less. Partly freezing the meat for an hour or so makes it much easier to slice at home. **To grill:** on a solid pan or a fine grate so the slices don't fall through; it cooks in a couple of minutes. See the [bulgogi guide](/journal/korean-beef-bulgogi-guide) and [bulgogi recipe](/recipes/korean-bulgogi-recipe).

### Chadolbaegi (thin brisket)

Brisket shaved paper-thin, usually from frozen, so the slices curl. **US equivalent:** beef brisket, ideally the fattier point end; Korean and Asian markets sell it pre-shaved, often labelled "brisket for BBQ" or "beef brisket slice." **Marinade:** no. **Thickness:** as thin as you can get it. This isn't a home-knife job. **To grill:** lay slices on a hot pan for a few seconds per side. They're done almost immediately. Dip in sesame oil with salt and pepper, or wrap with [ssamjang](/journal/ssamjang-korean-bbq-dipping-sauce-guide).

### Kkotsal and other marbled cuts

Kkotsal means "flower meat," named for its heavy marbling. The term is used loosely in restaurants for very marbled beef, often boneless short rib. **US equivalent:** boneless short rib, or a well-marbled ribeye cap; ask for the highest grade the butcher has. **Marinade:** no, salt at most. **Thickness:** about ¼ inch. **To grill:** hot and fast, a minute or so per side, and keep it pink. For premium Korean hanwoo and Japanese wagyu, see our [wagyu and hanwoo guide](/journal/wagyu-hanwoo-premium-beef-guide).

### Deungsim (ribeye/loin)

Deungsim is the Korean term for the loin area, and in practice it usually means ribeye (strip loin is chaekkeut, often grouped with it). **US equivalent:** ribeye or New York strip. **Marinade:** no. Season with salt and maybe a little pepper. **Thickness:** restaurants serve it anywhere from about ¼ inch to a thick steak that gets cut with scissors on the grill. At home, ½ inch is a good middle ground. **To grill:** high heat, sear both sides, then cut into bite-size pieces and finish to your preferred doneness.

### Anchangsal (outside skirt)

A beefy, loose-grained cut that is chewy in a good way. **US equivalent:** skirt steak, ideally outside skirt (the diaphragm muscle). **Marinade:** usually plain, sometimes a light salt-and-sesame-oil rub. **Thickness:** grill the strip whole or in ½-inch pieces. **To grill:** very high heat, a short time, medium-rare to medium. Cut across the grain to serve, or it gets stringy.

## Pork

Korean BBQ pork is served fully cooked. The USDA minimum for whole cuts is 145°F (63°C) with a three-minute rest, and 160°F (71°C) for ground pork. Thin slices browned on both sides and opaque throughout are well past that.

### Samgyeopsal (pork belly)

The most popular cut at Korean BBQ. **US equivalent:** skinless pork belly, unsmoked and uncured. It is not bacon. **Marinade:** no. **Thickness:** about ¼ inch for quick cooking, up to ½ inch or thicker for a juicier, restaurant-style cut. **To grill:** medium heat, letting the fat render until both sides are golden and crisp, then cut into pieces with scissors. Grilling kimchi and garlic in the rendered fat is a classic move. Our full [samgyeopsal guide](/journal/korean-samgyupsal-guide-pork-belly-bbq) covers the meal around it.

### Moksal (neck/shoulder)

Leaner than belly but still marbled, with more chew. **US equivalent:** pork collar or the neck end of the Boston butt (pork shoulder). Ask for "pork collar" first. **Marinade:** often plain; also good in a gochujang marinade. **Thickness:** about ½ inch. **To grill:** medium heat until cooked through and browned, then cut into strips.

### Hangjeongsal (pork jowl)

A small, prized cut from the area around the jaw and neck, with a crunchy, springy texture. **US equivalent:** pork jowl (fresh, not cured), sometimes sold as "pork neck" or "pork jowl meat" at Asian markets. Labels vary, so check with the butcher. **Marinade:** no, salt and pepper. **Thickness:** about ¼ inch. **To grill:** medium-high heat until golden and cooked through.

### Dwaeji galbi (marinated pork ribs)

The pork version of galbi. **US equivalent:** pork spareribs or pork shoulder; some restaurants use shoulder meat attached to the rib. **Marinade:** yes, either sweet soy (like beef galbi) or spicy gochujang. **Thickness:** about ¼ inch. **To grill:** medium heat, because the sugar burns before thick pork cooks through. Turn often until cooked through.

## Chicken: Dakgalbi

Despite the name, dakgalbi isn't ribs. It's boneless chicken, usually thigh, in a spicy gochujang sauce, cooked with cabbage, sweet potato and rice cakes, usually on a large flat pan rather than a grill. Cut thighs into bite-size pieces, about ½ inch thick, and cook to 165°F (74°C).

## Where to Buy

**Korean and Asian grocery stores** are the easiest source. H Mart and similar stores sell most of these cuts pre-sliced in the meat case: shaved brisket, bulgogi beef, LA galbi, thick- and thin-cut pork belly, and often pre-marinated galbi and bulgogi. Our [Korean grocery store guide](/journal/korean-grocery-store-guide-what-to-buy) covers what else to pick up there.

**A regular butcher** can cut most of this if you ask clearly. Use the US names: "flanken-cut short ribs about half an inch thick," "skinless pork belly sliced a quarter inch," "pork collar." Very thin cuts like chadolbaegi need a slicer, so call ahead.
