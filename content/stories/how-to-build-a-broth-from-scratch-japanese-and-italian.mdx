Both are called broth. Both are clear liquids used as the foundation of a cuisine. But Japanese dashi and Italian brodo are built on entirely different principles, extract entirely different things from their ingredients, and taste so fundamentally unlike each other that calling them the same thing is a little like calling a violin and a cello the same instrument because they're both made of wood and strings.

Dashi is made in 20 minutes. Brodo takes hours. Dashi uses two dried, shelf-stable ingredients. Brodo uses bones, meat, and aromatics. Dashi tastes clean, mineral, and oceanic. Brodo tastes rich, savory, and deeply animal.

These differences are not accidents of cultural preference. They are the result of each tradition optimizing for a specific set of compounds and flavors that reflect the cuisine built around them.

---

## What dashi is extracting

Dashi is classically made from two ingredients: kombu (dried kelp) and katsuobushi (dried, smoked, fermented skipjack tuna flakes). These two ingredients together produce what may be the most efficient expression of umami in any cooking tradition.

Kombu is dense with glutamate — the free amino acid that stimulates the umami receptors on the tongue. A single sheet of dried kombu can contain 2,000–3,000 mg of glutamate per 100 grams of dry weight, one of the highest natural concentrations of any food. When kombu soaks in cold water, glutamate dissolves into the water. The process requires no heat — in fact, cold-steeping kombu overnight produces a lighter, more delicate dashi than heating it, because high heat (above 60°C / 140°F) can release bitter, mucilaginous compounds from the kombu alongside the glutamate.

Katsuobushi contributes inosinate — specifically, inosine monophosphate (IMP) — another umami compound. On its own, inosinate creates mild umami. But when inosinate and glutamate appear together, they don't simply add; they synergize. The combination of the two compounds produces umami perception roughly eight times stronger than either alone. This synergy is why dashi tastes more intensely savory than the individual ingredients suggest it should.

Dashi extracts almost nothing else of significance. It contains no gelatin (no collagen is present in the ingredients), no fat, and very little beyond the two key umami compounds plus a handful of mineral flavors from the kombu and smoke compounds from the katsuobushi. The broth is clear, light, and nearly flavorless in the conventional sense — until you taste it alongside food, at which point everything around it tastes more intensely of itself.

## What brodo is extracting

Italian brodo is the opposite extraction in nearly every dimension.

Traditional brodo is made from bones (often a mix of chicken, beef, and/or veal), meat scraps, and aromatics (onion, carrot, celery, parsley, peppercorns, bay). It cooks for two to four hours at a gentle simmer, and over that time it extracts three main categories of compounds.

**Gelatin:** Bones contain collagen, particularly in joints, knuckles, and cartilage. At sustained heat, collagen breaks down into gelatin. Gelatin gives brodo its characteristic body — the way a properly made broth becomes lightly jelled when refrigerated, and the way it coats the mouth when hot. Dashi has none of this. Brodo's richness is substantially gelatin-derived.

**Fat:** Meat and bones both release fat into the simmering liquid. Properly made brodo is skimmed regularly to remove excess fat, but some remains, contributing richness and carrying fat-soluble flavor compounds that would be absent in a purely water-based extraction like dashi.

**Maillard compounds:** If the bones are roasted before making brodo (as in the darker, richer version sometimes called *brodo scuro* or brown stock), the dry heat creates Maillard reaction products — hundreds of flavor compounds produced when proteins and sugars interact at high temperatures. These compounds give roasted-bone brodo its depth and color, and also contribute free glutamate from the browned meat. Unroasted, or *bianco* brodo, skips this step for a lighter result.

Italian brodo also contains natural glutamate from the meat and bones, but at lower concentrations than dashi. Its savory depth comes primarily from gelatin, fat, and (if roasted) Maillard compounds — not from a single dominant umami-amplifying mechanism the way dashi does.

## When to use each

These differences in composition map directly to different culinary roles.

**Use dashi when you want clean, transparent, amplified flavor.** Dashi is not trying to add its own flavor to a dish — it's trying to make the dish's existing flavors taste more intensely of themselves. Clear soups, delicate braises, dipping sauces, ochazuke (rice with tea or broth poured over it), chawanmushi (steamed egg custard) — all of these benefit from dashi's clarity. Adding brodo to chawanmushi would make it muddy and heavy; the egg custard's subtle flavor would be lost under the brodo's richness.

**Use brodo when you want body, richness, and depth.** Risotto needs brodo because the gelatin helps bind the starch and creates the silky, cohesive texture that a rice dish cooked in water or dashi would lack. Braised meats benefit from the gelatin-rich liquid for the same reason — it concentrates into a glossy, coating sauce. Italian wedding soup, tortellini en brodo, stracciatella — these dishes are showcasing the brodo directly, and its body and richness are the point.

## Dashi: basic recipe

**Ingredients**
- 1 liter cold water
- 20g kombu (one large piece, roughly 15 × 10 cm / 6 × 4 inches)
- 30g katsuobushi (loosely packed flakes)

**Method**

Place the kombu in the cold water. If you have time, let it soak for 30–60 minutes before heating — this cold-steep extracts clean glutamate. If not, proceed immediately to heating.

Heat the kombu and water together over medium-low heat. Watch the pot. As small bubbles begin to form at the bottom of the pot and the water begins to steam — around 60°C (140°F) — remove the kombu and discard it. Do not let the kombu boil. Boiling releases bitter compounds.

Bring the water to a full boil. Add the katsuobushi all at once. Remove the pot from the heat immediately. Let the flakes steep for 2–3 minutes — they will gradually sink as they absorb water.

Line a fine-mesh strainer with a paper towel or clean kitchen cloth and pour the dashi through. Do not press or squeeze the katsuobushi; pressing extracts bitter compounds. Discard the flakes.

Use immediately, refrigerate for up to 3 days, or freeze for up to 1 month. The dashi is finished. Total time: 20–30 minutes.

**Technique notes:** The strained liquid should be pale gold and clear, smelling faintly of the ocean and smoke. If it smells strongly of fish or tastes bitter, the kombu boiled or the katsuobushi steeped too long. For *ichiban dashi* (first dashi — the most delicate), this recipe is correct. The spent kombu and katsuobushi can be used to make *niban dashi* (second dashi), a lighter broth suitable for simmered dishes and soups where the first dashi would be wasted.

## Italian chicken brodo: basic recipe

**Ingredients**
- 1.5 kg chicken pieces (backs, necks, and/or a whole cut-up chicken — bones and skin included)
- 1 medium yellow onion, halved
- 2 medium carrots, broken in half
- 2 stalks celery with leaves
- 1 handful flat-leaf parsley stems and leaves
- 1 bay leaf
- 8 black peppercorns
- 1 piece Parmigiano-Reggiano rind (optional but highly recommended — 5–8 cm / 2–3 inches)
- 3 liters cold water
- Salt to finish

**Method**

Place the chicken pieces in a large pot. Cover with the cold water. Bring slowly to a boil over medium heat — this slow rise gives the proteins time to coagulate gradually and rise to the surface as foam, which is easier to skim than if you start with a violent boil.

As the water approaches a boil, gray-brown foam will form at the surface. Skim it thoroughly with a spoon or ladle — this foam is coagulated proteins and impurities that would cloud the broth and add off-flavors if left in.

Once the water reaches a full boil and has been skimmed clean, reduce the heat to a bare simmer — the surface should barely shiver, with a bubble breaking through every few seconds. Add the onion, carrots, celery, parsley, bay leaf, peppercorns, and Parmesan rind if using.

Simmer uncovered for 2.5–3 hours. Resist the temptation to stir or disturb the pot; agitation clouds the broth. Skim the surface again every 30–45 minutes if you want a clear result.

Strain through a fine-mesh strainer into a clean container. Discard the solids. If you want a very clear brodo, line the strainer with damp cheesecloth. Let cool, then refrigerate. Once cold, the fat will solidify on the surface and can be lifted off with a spoon. Season with salt only after the fat is removed and you can assess the final flavor.

A properly made chicken brodo should be golden-amber, with a noticeable body when warm and a light gel when cold. The Parmesan rind contributes glutamate and a subtle richness. Total active time: 30 minutes. Total elapsed time: 3–3.5 hours.

**Timing note:** Chicken brodo extracts most of its gelatin and flavor in the first 2 hours. The third hour deepens the flavor but the gelatin is largely extracted by then. Unlike beef or veal brodo, which can simmer for 6–8 hours, chicken brodo turns slightly bitter if cooked beyond 4 hours.

## The hybrid: dashi-enhanced chicken broth

This is not a traditional preparation from either cuisine. It is simply what happens when you apply the logic of dashi — glutamate amplification — to an already-made Italian chicken brodo.

**Method**

Take 1 liter of finished chicken brodo. Add one 15 × 10 cm strip of kombu. Heat gently to 60°C (140°F) — use an instant-read thermometer, or watch for the moment before the first bubbles appear. Hold at that temperature for 20 minutes, then remove and discard the kombu.

The resulting broth has the body and richness of brodo with the umami-amplifying effect of dashi's glutamate. It tastes more intensely of chicken than either preparation alone. The kombu doesn't make it taste Japanese — it tastes like an unusually good chicken broth.

Use this for risotto where you want exceptional depth, for the braising liquid of chicken thighs, or as the base of a sauce that needs to be reduced and concentrated. The extra glutamate becomes more pronounced as the liquid reduces, which means the final sauce carries more flavor per tablespoon than brodo alone would.

The technique works with beef brodo as well. Add kombu to finished beef broth, hold at 60°C for 20 minutes, remove the kombu. The result is what you'd use for the most compelling French onion soup you've made, or for braised short ribs that finish with a sauce of unusual intensity.

## The underlying principle

Dashi and brodo represent two strategies for making water taste like something.

Dashi's strategy is precision: identify the specific compounds that make food taste more intensely of itself (glutamate and inosinate), source them efficiently (kombu and katsuobushi), and extract only those compounds in minimal time. The result is a broth that improves everything it touches without adding its own flavor identity.

Brodo's strategy is accumulation: extract everything that the ingredients contain — gelatin, fat, flavor compounds, Maillard products — over sufficient time and heat to coax it all out. The result is a broth with a strong flavor identity of its own.

Neither is better. They are answers to different questions about what a broth is for.

The hybrid exists because the two strategies are not mutually exclusive. A broth can have gelatin body and glutamate amplification at the same time. A technique developed in one cuisine, once understood, can be applied anywhere the underlying chemistry holds — which is everywhere.
