In the Philippines, arroz caldo is the food of the inaalagaan — the person being cared for. When someone is sick, a family member makes arroz caldo. When someone recovers from an operation, arroz caldo is the first solid food. When there is a fever, arroz caldo. The dish functions as both medicine and comfort, with the ginger providing actual physiological warming and anti-nausea properties and the easily digestible rice providing nutrition without burdening the stomach.

The Spanish name is colonial: Spain ruled the Philippines from 1565 to 1898, and many Filipino dishes carry Spanish names or hybrid names from that period. *Arroz caldo* in Spanish literally means 'broth rice' or 'hot rice broth.' The dish itself draws more heavily from Chinese congee traditions brought by Chinese immigrant communities (*Sangley*, as Chinese traders in the Philippines were called) — the rice-cooked-in-broth-to-porridge method is Chinese. But the ginger-forward flavor, the fish sauce, the calamansi garnish, and the crispy garlic topping are distinctly Filipino.

## How Arroz Caldo Differs From Other Congees

| | Arroz Caldo (Filipino) | Zhou/Congee (Chinese) | Okayu (Japanese) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ginger** | Heavy — 4-6cm fresh ginger | Light or absent | Absent or minimal |
| **Broth** | Chicken broth, flavorful | Water or simple broth | Water or dashi |
| **Garnishes** | Crispy garlic, scallion, calamansi, fish sauce, egg | Varied (century egg, pork floss, etc.) | Umeboshi, pickles, nori |
| **Texture** | Very thick, almost stodgy | Ranges thin to thick | Soft, distinct grains visible |
| **Character** | Warming, gingery, savory-acidic | Neutral base | Clean, mild |

## The Rice

**Glutinous rice (malagkit):** Some arroz caldo recipes use glutinous rice — it breaks down faster and more completely than regular rice, creating a particularly thick, sticky texture. The glutinous rice version is thicker and more cohesive.

**Regular white rice:** More common; the result is still very thick from the starch release during the long cooking, but slightly less sticky.

**The ratio:** Much more liquid than in normal rice cooking — approximately 6–8 parts liquid to 1 part dry rice. The rice cooks until completely swollen and broken down.

---

## The Ginger

Fresh ginger, sliced into coins or julienned — a significant amount added to the broth at the beginning of cooking. It infuses the entire broth with ginger flavor. Some recipes additionally add finely grated ginger at the end for a fresher ginger hit.

The ginger quantity in arroz caldo is 3–4 times what most other Asian congees use. It is the dominant flavor.

---

## The Garnishes: The Four Essential

**1. Crispy fried garlic (bawang prito):** Thinly sliced garlic fried in oil until golden — not brown, golden. Drained and stored crispy. Added at serving. The contrast between the crispy garlic and the thick soft congee is essential.

**2. Sliced scallions/green onions:** Added fresh at serving.

**3. Calamansi:** The small Filipino citrus fruit, squeezed over the arroz caldo at the table. Calamansi is sourer and more fragrant than lime; lime or lemon is the substitute. The acid brightens the rich broth.

**4. Fish sauce (patis):** Drizzled at the table, not cooked in. The patis adds saltiness and umami depth at the individual level.

**5. Hard-boiled egg (optional but traditional):** Added whole or halved at serving.

## The Complete Recipe

**Serves:** 4 | **Time:** 1 hour

### Ingredients
- 200g regular white rice or glutinous rice (malagkit)
- 800g bone-in chicken pieces (thighs and drumsticks preferred)
- 1.5 liters chicken broth or water
- 6–8cm piece fresh ginger, peeled and sliced into coins
- 1 medium onion, diced
- 4 garlic cloves, minced
- 1 tablespoon fish sauce (patis)
- Salt and white pepper
- 2 tablespoons neutral oil

### Crispy Garlic Topping
- 6 garlic cloves, very thinly sliced
- 4 tablespoons neutral oil

### To Serve
- Sliced scallions
- Calamansi or lime halves
- Fish sauce
- Hard-boiled eggs

### Method

**1. Sauté base:** Heat oil in a large pot over medium heat. Add onion; cook until softened. Add minced garlic; cook 1 minute. Add sliced ginger; stir 1 minute.

**2. Brown chicken:** Add chicken pieces; cook 3–4 minutes per side until lightly golden.

**3. Add rice and liquid:** Add rinsed rice and chicken broth. Bring to a boil; skim foam; reduce to a simmer. Cover; cook 30–40 minutes, stirring occasionally, until rice is completely broken down and the consistency is thick porridge. Add water if too thick.

**4. Remove chicken:** Take out chicken pieces; remove skin and bones; shred meat; return meat to pot.

**5. Season:** Add fish sauce, salt, and white pepper. Adjust consistency (should flow slowly when stirred but be thick).

**6. Crispy garlic:** In a small pan, heat oil over medium. Add sliced garlic; fry, stirring, until golden (3–4 minutes). Remove immediately; drain on paper towels.

**Serve:** In deep bowls; top with crispy garlic, scallions, and a hard-boiled egg. Serve calamansi halves, fish sauce, and white pepper at the table for individual seasoning.
