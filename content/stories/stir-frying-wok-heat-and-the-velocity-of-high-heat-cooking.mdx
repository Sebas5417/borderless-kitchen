A restaurant wok burner produces 100,000–200,000 BTUs. A home gas range produces 8,000–15,000. This gap explains why restaurant stir-fry tastes different from home stir-fry even when the recipe is identical. The technique was designed for heat that most home kitchens cannot replicate.

This is not a reason to give up — it is a reason to understand the physics and adapt accordingly.

---

## Wok Hei: What It Is and Why It Matters

Wok hei (鑊氣) translates roughly to "wok breath" — the slightly smoky, charred, complex flavor that characterizes well-executed stir-fry. It comes from the rapid combustion of oil droplets, food sugars, and proteins at very high temperatures, the partial charring of food surfaces in milliseconds of contact with searing metal, and the Maillard reactions happening faster than any other cooking method can achieve them.

Wok hei requires three things: extreme heat, the right pan, and enough space for rapid evaporation of moisture. Without all three, you get steamed vegetables in soy sauce — edible but not stir-fry.

**At home:** A carbon steel wok preheated until smoking over the highest gas flame achieves adequate wok hei for home cooking. The adaptation: cook in smaller batches, give each ingredient more room, keep the heat as high as possible. The result isn't restaurant wok hei, but it is genuinely different from a covered pan of vegetables.

## The Wok

Carbon steel is the correct material for stir-frying. It heats rapidly, reaches very high temperatures, and develops a seasoned, non-stick surface over time from the oil and heat. A properly seasoned carbon steel wok releases food easily at high heat.

**Cast iron** holds heat but responds slowly to heat changes — too sluggish for the rapid temperature adjustments stir-frying requires.

**Nonstick** coatings degrade at the temperatures needed for wok hei and are not suitable.

**Stainless** works adequately but does not develop seasoning and tends to have more sticking issues at high heat without careful technique.

**Size:** A 14-inch wok for home use. Large enough to toss ingredients; small enough to heat quickly on a home range.

**Shape:** Round-bottomed woks sit in a wok ring over a gas burner and have maximum surface area for the flame. Flat-bottomed woks are necessary for induction or electric ranges and work well on gas too.

**Seasoning a new carbon steel wok:** Scrub off the factory coating with soap and hot water (only time you use soap on carbon steel). Heat dry over high heat until the metal shows color changes — blues, purples, light gold. Add a thin layer of oil with high smoke point, wipe the excess with a paper towel (it should be barely visible), and heat again until smoking. Repeat 2-3 times. The wok is now ready.

## Mise en Place: The Non-Negotiable

Stir-frying is too fast to stop and chop anything. Everything — every vegetable cut, every sauce component measured, every protein marinated — must be ready before the wok goes on the heat. The cooking phase of a stir-fry takes 3–5 minutes; the prep takes 20.

**Prep order:**
1. Mix the sauce (cornstarch + liquid ingredients + aromatics if going in the sauce)
2. Marinate the protein (soy, rice wine, cornstarch — at minimum 15 minutes)
3. Cut all vegetables to similar thickness so they cook at similar rates
4. Mince garlic, ginger, scallion (these go in first and cook fast)
5. Arrange everything in order of addition at the stove

**The mise en place rule for stir-fry:** If you can't identify the order of addition before you start, don't start yet.

## The Technique: Sequencing and Velocity

**Preheat aggressively.** Heat the dry wok over maximum heat until it begins to smoke visibly. Then add oil (not before — the wok needs to be hot first). Swirl to coat. The oil should shimmer immediately.

**Cook the aromatics first, briefly.** Garlic and ginger go in first. They take 15–30 seconds — long enough to bloom and infuse the oil, short enough not to burn. This is the fastest part. If they burn, start over.

**Protein next, in a single layer, untouched.** The protein goes in and is left alone for 30–60 seconds to sear. Then tossed or stirred. The goal is Maillard browning on at least one face. A protein that is continuously stirred from the moment it enters the wok steams instead of sears.

**Remove protein before the vegetables.** This is the critical step most home cooks skip. The protein comes out of the wok while the vegetables cook. Both get optimal heat; neither overcooks the other.

**Vegetables in order of density.** Dense vegetables (broccoli, carrots, cauliflower) go in first. Medium-density (bell pepper, snap peas, mushrooms) go in after 1–2 minutes. Delicate greens (bok choy leaves, spinach) go in last — 30 seconds is enough.

**The sauce goes in near the end.** Pour around the perimeter of the wok (the hottest zone, not on top of the food), then toss everything together. The cornstarch in the sauce will thicken as it hits the heat. 30–60 seconds of tossing integrates everything.

**Return the protein.** Add it back, toss once more, serve immediately.

## The Small-Batch Rule

The single biggest mistake in home stir-frying: too much food in the pan at once.

When food is crowded in a wok, it releases moisture faster than the heat can evaporate it. The wok temperature drops. The food simmers in its own liquid. You get steamed vegetables, not stir-fried ones.

**The rule:** For a standard home wok on a home range, stir-fry in batches no larger than 2 servings. For a solo meal, this is fine. For 4 people, it means 2 separate wok-loads cooked sequentially and combined before serving.

This is not a limitation — it is the adaptation that makes wok cooking work at home. Professional kitchens cook in much larger batches only because their heat source is proportionally more powerful.

## Sauce Structure

Most stir-fry sauces follow a ratio:

- **Soy sauce:** umami and salt (2-3 tablespoons for 2 servings)
- **Rice wine (Shaoxing wine):** acidity and depth (1 tablespoon)
- **Sesame oil:** aromatic finish, added at the end (1 teaspoon — it burns at wok heat)
- **Oyster sauce:** sweetness and body (1 tablespoon — optional)
- **Cornstarch:** thickening agent (1 teaspoon dissolved in the liquid)
- **Sugar:** balance (1/2 teaspoon)

The cornstarch dissolved in liquid (called a slurry) thickens the sauce in seconds when it hits the heat, creating the glossy coating that characterizes Chinese restaurant stir-fry. Without it, the sauce slides off; with it, it clings.

Mix the sauce completely before cooking begins. Never add dry cornstarch directly to the wok.

## Protein Velveting

Restaurant stir-fried chicken and beef have a notably silky, tender texture that home-cooked protein doesn't achieve. The technique is velveting: marinating the protein in a mixture that includes baking soda (or egg white and cornstarch), which denatures the surface proteins and produces extreme tenderness.

**Basic velvet marinade:** For 250g chicken breast or flank steak: 1 teaspoon soy sauce, 1 teaspoon Shaoxing wine, 1/2 teaspoon baking soda (for baking soda method), 1 teaspoon cornstarch, 1 tablespoon water. Marinate 15–30 minutes. Rinse off the baking soda before cooking.

The baking soda raises the surface pH of the meat, preventing the proteins from contracting as sharply when heated. The result is chicken that stays tender even when cooked at high heat — the opposite of the rubbery home stir-fry result that comes from overcooked unstretched protein.

---

Stir-frying at home is a compromise — the heat is lower than ideal, the equipment is larger and less responsive. But understanding why the restaurant version is different (heat, batch size, velveting, mise en place as a discipline rather than a suggestion) allows you to close most of the gap. The resulting dish is faster to cook than almost anything else in the kitchen and, when the technique works, produces textures and flavors no other method can replicate.
