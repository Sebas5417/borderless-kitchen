Sundubu jjigae (순두부찌개) is built on a simple technique — blooming dried chili in fat before building the broth — and takes about 20 minutes from cold pan to boiling bowl. The stew is served at a full boil in a heavy pot, with a raw egg cracked in at the table. That egg finishes in the residual heat. You eat it immediately.

## What Sundubu Jjigae Is

The name breaks down: "sundubu" (순두부) means uncurdled or soft tofu — specifically the barely-set, silky variety that comes in tubes or sealed plastic packets. It is not the same as regular soft tofu, which holds a firmer block shape. "Jjigae" (찌개) means stew — a category of Korean cooking characterized by a boldly seasoned broth cooked and served in the pot, eaten alongside rice.

Sundubu jjigae is spicy, savory, and intensely flavored. The broth is built from gochugaru (Korean red chili flakes) sautéed in sesame oil, anchovy or dashi stock, garlic, and gochujang. The soft tofu goes in last, broken into large irregular pieces. A raw egg is cracked directly into the boiling stew at serving — the egg white firms slightly from the heat while the yolk stays soft, adding richness to the already rich broth.

It is one of the fastest substantial Korean dishes to make from scratch. Start to finish in 20 minutes is realistic once you have your pantry stocked.

## The Key Technique: Blooming the Gochugaru

Gochugaru is Korean red chili flakes — different from Italian peperoncino or Chinese chili flakes. Korean gochugaru is coarser, less fiery, and has a distinctive fruity sweetness behind its heat. It is not a substitute for any other chili flake.

The first step in sundubu jjigae is sautéing gochugaru in sesame oil. This step is not optional. Fat-soluble flavor compounds in dried chili — including the capsaicin itself and the carotenoid pigments that produce the red color — only dissolve in fat, not in water. Sautéing the gochugaru in oil releases these compounds into the oil, and from there they distribute evenly through the entire broth. Skipping this step and adding gochugaru directly to water produces a flat, thin-tasting result where the chili flavor sits on the surface rather than permeating the liquid.

Use 1 tablespoon sesame oil in the pot. Add 2 tablespoons gochugaru. Medium heat. Stir constantly for 60–90 seconds until the chili is fragrant and the oil is vividly red. Don't burn it — the goal is bloomed, not bitter. Then add your protein and garlic.

## The Broth Base

The stew's depth comes from the anchovy stock. Dried anchovies (myeolchi) and dried kelp (dasima) simmered in cold water for 10–15 minutes produce a Korean dashi equivalent — lighter and more mineral than Japanese dashi but with the same inosinate + glutamate umami compound combination. This is the stock used for most Korean jjigae.

Quick anchovy stock: combine 10–12 dried anchovies (heads and guts removed, or use the pre-cleaned variety), 1 piece dried kelp (about 10cm), and 3 cups cold water. Bring to a simmer over medium heat, simmer 10 minutes. Remove the anchovies and kelp. The stock is done.

If you don't have dried anchovies, Japanese dashi or a light vegetable stock works. The flavor won't be identical, but the structure of the stew holds.

## Protein Options

**Classic (ground pork):** The most common home version. Brown 100g minced pork in the gochugaru oil after blooming the chili. The pork fat renders into the oil and adds another layer of richness to the broth. Season with garlic (3 cloves, minced) and a teaspoon of gochujang. Add the anchovy stock and bring to a boil.

**Seafood (haemul sundubu):** The restaurant standard. After blooming the gochugaru, add garlic and gochujang, then pour in the stock. Add 100g clams (manila or littleneck, scrubbed), 100g medium shrimp (peeled), and optional oysters. The seafood adds inosinate from a different source — shellfish release more of it during cooking than pork does, producing a distinctly oceanic, briny umami note. This is the version to make with fresh shellfish.

**Vegetarian:** Omit the anchovies from the stock (use kelp only, or add dried shiitake mushroom to the water). Use mushrooms as the protein — sliced shiitake and king oyster mushrooms work well. The dried shiitake adds glutamate independently, partially compensating for the absent anchovy inosinate.

## The Soft Tofu

Sundubu comes in squeezable tubes or sealed packets. The texture is barely set — like very soft silken tofu, but even less firm. Handle it gently.

Add the soft tofu by squeezing or scooping it directly into the boiling broth in large, irregular pieces. Do not cube it neatly. The rough, torn surfaces create more surface area in contact with the spicy broth, and the crevices catch the broth as the tofu cooks. Gentle pieces, roughly the size of a large spoonful.

Let the tofu cook in the boiling broth for 2–3 minutes until it is heated through and slightly firmer on the edges. It should not dissolve or fall apart — it holds its shape when handled carefully.

## The Kimchi Variation

Many home cooks add 2–3 tablespoons of ripe kimchi (at least 2–3 weeks old, preferably older) to the stew along with the garlic and gochujang. The fermented kimchi adds acidity and complexity that cuts through the richness of the tofu and the fat in the broth. The kimchi sourness acts as a counterpoint to the heat of the gochugaru — the effect is similar to adding a splash of vinegar to a tomato sauce. This is not a departure from the recipe. It is a common variation that improves the broth.

## The Dolsot and the Egg

Traditional sundubu jjigae is served in a dolsot — a Korean stone pot, typically made from earthenware or stone, that retains heat intensely and distributes it evenly. The pot is preheated before the stew goes in. The stew arrives at the table at a full, active boil.

The raw egg goes in at the table. Crack it directly into the center of the boiling stew. The white sets almost immediately from the retained heat. The yolk remains soft, jammy, and partially cooked. Stir it slightly to incorporate — the yolk enriches the broth the way a pasta egg emulsifies a sauce.

You have 60–90 seconds before the egg overcooks. Eat immediately.

At home, a small cast iron pot, a heavy ceramic pot, or any pot that holds heat well and can go directly to the table works. Preheat it on the burner for a few minutes before adding the stew. The preheated surface helps the stew stay at a boil through serving.

## Rice Is Not Optional

Sundubu jjigae is never eaten alone. A bowl of steamed white rice on the side is part of the meal. The rice is eaten alongside the stew, used to moderate the intensity of the spice and the richness of the broth. The logic is that the stew is too intense to eat in large amounts without a neutral ballast — the rice extends the palate across the meal and allows you to eat more of the stew before the spice accumulates.

This is the same logic behind serving bread with a bold olive oil-based stew. The neutral carbohydrate regulates the intensity of the main.

## Sundubu Jjigae vs. Italian Acqua Pazza

Italian acqua pazza — "crazy water" — is a Neapolitan preparation: fish (typically a whole white fish or fillets) poached in a broth of olive oil, garlic, white wine, cherry tomatoes, and fresh chili. The name comes from the unconventional use of water as a cooking medium for fish, elevated with aromatics. The fish poaches in the liquid; the liquid absorbs its flavor.

The parallels with sundubu jjigae are structural. Both use a protein (seafood or tofu) cooked directly in a spiced, aromatic broth. Both are served in the cooking vessel — the stew arrives in the pot. Both build flavor from the interaction between the protein and the broth as they cook together. Both include heat from chili — acqua pazza uses fresh chili or peperoncino, sundubu uses gochugaru and gochujang. Some acqua pazza recipes include an egg poached directly in the broth, finishing in the residual heat at the table — an exact parallel to the sundubu jjigae egg.

The structural divergence is in the flavor foundations. Acqua pazza uses white wine and tomato for acid and body — the acid brightens the broth, the tomato thickens it slightly. Sundubu jjigae uses gochujang (fermented chili paste, which adds acidity through fermentation) and anchovy stock for fermented heat and umami body. The Italian version uses olive oil as the fat foundation; the Korean version uses sesame oil. Both fats carry the chili compounds and give the broth its character.

Different materials, identical structure. A spiced broth, a protein cooked in it, a cooking vessel brought directly to the table, and an egg that finishes in residual heat. The acqua pazza and the sundubu jjigae are the same dish arrived at independently by two entirely different culinary traditions.
