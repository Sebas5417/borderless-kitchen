Most recipes that call for dashi treat it as an ingredient with a single form: steep kombu, add katsuobushi, strain, done. But dashi is more accurately a set of techniques for extracting flavor from dried marine ingredients, and each method produces a distinctly different liquid — not just stronger or weaker, but structurally different in what flavor compounds it contains.

Understanding why each method works the way it does makes it possible to choose the right one for a given dish — or, more interestingly, to apply dashi logic to ingredients outside the Japanese pantry.

---

## The science of umami extraction

The core flavor compound in kombu dashi is glutamic acid, a free amino acid present in high concentrations in dried kelp. Kombu (Saccharina japonica, or the related Laminaria varieties) accumulates glutamate during its natural growth process, and drying concentrates it further. When kombu is submerged in water, glutamate dissolves freely — it is water-soluble and does not require heat to extract.

What heat does is accelerate extraction and introduce secondary compounds. Above 60°C, kombu begins releasing alginic acid and other polysaccharides that contribute a slightly viscous, slippery mouthfeel to the stock. Above 80°C, volatile aromatics that give kombu its faint oceanic character begin to escape. This is why cold extraction produces a cleaner-tasting, more transparent result than hot extraction — fewer compounds are pulled into solution.

Katsuobushi (dried, fermented, and smoked skipjack tuna) contributes inosine monophosphate (IMP), a nucleotide that acts synergistically with glutamate. The combination of glutamate and IMP produces an umami response roughly eight times stronger than either compound alone — this is the synergistic effect first documented by Akira Kuninaka in 1960, and it is the reason dashi made with both kombu and katsuobushi tastes more than the sum of its parts.

Dried shiitake mushrooms contribute guanosine monophosphate (GMP), another nucleotide with umami-synergistic properties, along with pronounced earthy flavor compounds that kombu and katsuobushi don't contain.

## Method 1: Ichiban dashi — first draw

Ichiban means "first" or "number one." The name refers to the first extraction from fresh kombu and katsuobushi, which yields the most delicate, transparent, and aromatically precise dashi.

**Ingredients:**
- 1 liter cold water (filtered preferred)
- 15g dried kombu (roughly a 10cm × 10cm piece)
- 20g katsuobushi (loosely packed)

**Method:**

Place the kombu in the cold water and let it soak for 30 minutes. This begins glutamate extraction before any heat is applied, protecting the delicate character of the final stock.

Transfer the pot to the stove over medium-low heat. Bring the water slowly to 60–65°C — this takes roughly 10-12 minutes on low heat. Use a thermometer. Hold at this temperature for 10 minutes, then increase to medium heat.

Watch for the moment just before boiling — small bubbles will appear at the bottom and the kombu will begin to swell and soften. Remove the kombu at 80°C, before the water reaches a full boil. Boiling kombu makes the dashi bitter and activates alginic acid extraction, which creates a slimy texture.

Bring the water to a gentle boil, then remove from heat. Add the katsuobushi all at once and allow it to steep, undisturbed, for 2–3 minutes. Do not stir. The flakes will sink as they hydrate and release their compounds.

Strain through a fine-mesh strainer lined with cheesecloth. Do not press or squeeze the katsuobushi — pressing extracts bitter compounds from the spent flakes. Let it drain under gravity.

The result: a pale gold, clean-tasting stock with a pronounced but refined umami character and a subtle smokiness from the katsuobushi processing. It should be clear, not cloudy.

**When to use ichiban dashi:** Miso soup (the dashi flavor is prominent because the dish is simple), clear soups such as suimono, chawanmushi (savory steamed egg custard), and anywhere the dashi needs to be tasted directly rather than functioning as background.

**Italian application:** Use ichiban dashi as a cooking liquid for risotto in place of chicken stock. The clean, high-glutamate profile amplifies the rice's own savory depth without introducing competing flavors. The technique is direct substitution — same ratio, same method.

## Method 2: Niban dashi — second draw

Niban uses the already-steeped kombu and katsuobushi from an ichiban batch, extracting the remaining compounds through longer, hotter extraction. The result is more robust, darker, slightly more mineral, and better suited to preparations where dashi is a background note rather than the featured flavor.

**Ingredients:**
- Spent kombu and katsuobushi from ichiban dashi
- 1 liter cold water
- Optional: 5g fresh katsuobushi (added at the end to lift the flavor)

**Method:**

Place the spent kombu and katsuobushi in cold water. Bring to a simmer over medium heat and hold at a gentle simmer (85–90°C) for 10–15 minutes. Longer extraction times pull out more minerals and secondary compounds that weren't released in the first draw.

Strain, pressing the solids lightly this time — the first draw's delicate compounds are gone, and pressing extracts the remaining body from the spent ingredients. If using a small amount of fresh katsuobushi, add it to the hot strained liquid off heat, steep 2 minutes, then strain again.

The result: darker, more assertive, with a more pronounced mineral character. Less aromatics, more body.

**When to use niban dashi:** Nimono (simmered dishes where vegetables or proteins absorb the stock), braising liquid for daikon or kabocha, soup bases that will be heavily seasoned with miso or soy.

**Italian application:** Niban dashi makes an excellent braise liquid for tougher cuts or root vegetables. It functions similarly to a light meat broth — enough body to carry a braise without dominating it. Use it to deglaze after browning pork shoulder or lamb shanks, then finish the braise in the oven with the niban dashi instead of wine-and-stock.

## Method 3: Cold kombu dashi — 30-minute cold infusion

Cold extraction produces a completely different flavor profile: the glutamate is present in high concentration, but the volatile marine aromatics, alginic acid, and heat-generated compounds are entirely absent. The result is the cleanest, most transparent expression of kombu's umami — almost invisible as a flavor itself, functioning as pure savory depth.

**Ingredients:**
- 1 liter cold water
- 20g dried kombu

**Method:**

Submerge the kombu in cold water. For a quick version, steep at room temperature for 30 minutes. For a more developed version, refrigerate for 8–12 hours (overnight). The longer cold steep extracts more glutamate without introducing any heat-associated compounds.

Remove the kombu and use immediately, or refrigerate for up to 3 days.

The result: crystal-clear water with a faint oceanic suggestion and a strong umami backbone. It reads almost like minerally drinking water — the savory effect is felt rather than tasted.

**When to use cold kombu dashi:** Anywhere you want umami without the flavor of dashi — in salad dressings, as the water component in bread dough, as a base for cold noodle dishes (hiyayakko, soba tsuyu), or in cocktails and beverages where glutamate amplifies perceived flavor without adding stock taste.

**Italian application:** Use cold kombu dashi as the liquid in pasta dough. The added glutamate intensifies the flour's natural savory notes without any perceptible "Japanese" taste. Use it in vinaigrette in place of water. Use it as the braising liquid for canned white beans, which absorbs a significant amount of cooking liquid and benefits from the depth.

## Practical notes

**Kombu quality matters more than quantity.** High-quality kombu (usually labeled Rishiri or Rausu, from Hokkaido) has visible white powder on the surface — this is the dried glutamate crystals, and it should not be washed off. Wipe the surface with a barely damp cloth to remove dust only.

**Never boil kombu.** The maximum temperature for kombu is 80°C before removal. Above that, bitterness and sliminess develop quickly.

**Katsuobushi freshness.** Pre-shaved katsuobushi sold in large bags is acceptable for niban dashi but loses aromatic quality quickly once opened. For ichiban dashi, use a fresh bag or shave your own from a block (katsuobushi blocks keep for months).

**Water hardness.** Hard water competes with glutamate extraction and produces cloudier dashi. If your tap water is hard (above ~150mg/L TDS), use filtered or lightly mineralized bottled water.

---

The three methods are not interchangeable — they're three different tools for three different jobs. Ichiban for presence, niban for background, cold for invisible depth. Once you've made all three and tasted them side by side, the difference is not subtle. Each one makes a different argument about what savory flavor should do in a dish.
