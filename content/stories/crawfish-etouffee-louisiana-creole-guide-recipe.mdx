Louisiana produces 90% of the crawfish consumed in the United States, harvested from the Atchafalaya Basin and rice paddy crawfish farms in Acadiana. Between November and July, crawfish are a staple protein in Louisiana cooking — boiled with corn and potatoes for parties, turned into bisques, stuffed into pies, and perhaps most elegantly, cooked in étouffée.

The dish originated in the Breaux Bridge area of St. Martin Parish — "the crawfish capital of the world" — in the early 20th century. It made it to New Orleans restaurant menus in the 1950s, and from there to national awareness. Like gumbo and jambalaya, étouffée exists in Creole and Cajun versions, though they are closer to each other than in those other dishes.

## Creole vs Cajun Étouffée

**Creole version (New Orleans):** Often includes tomatoes or tomato paste. Lighter in color. May include more cream. Associated with restaurant cooking.

**Cajun version (rural Louisiana):** No tomatoes. Darker, more rustic. Relies more heavily on the crawfish fat and butter. The "purée" of fat from crawfish heads is essential — the orange fat inside crawfish heads is scooped out and stirred into the sauce, adding intense shellfish flavor.

Both are correct. The version in this recipe leans Cajun.

## The Crawfish

**Fresh Louisiana crawfish:** Available whole (live or boiled) or as "crawfish tails" — the peeled tail meat, sometimes with "crawfish fat" (the orange fat from the heads) included in the package. The fat is gold.

**Frozen crawfish tails:** Louisiana crawfish tails are widely available frozen outside Louisiana. Check the origin — Chinese crawfish have a slightly different flavor and texture. Louisiana-origin frozen tails are acceptable for year-round cooking.

**Do not substitute:** Shrimp works in the same sauce and is sometimes called "shrimp étouffée," but it is a different dish. Crawfish have a slightly sweeter, more delicate flavor than shrimp.

## The Technique

Étouffée begins with a light roux (butter and flour cooked together briefly), then the trinity, then aromatics, then stock, then crawfish. The sauce is thickened by both the roux and the natural proteins from the crawfish as they cook. The finish is a large amount of butter stirred in at the end — this is a rich dish.

The crawfish cook for a very short time — 5–7 minutes. Overcooked crawfish become rubbery.

**Recipe: Crawfish Étouffée (Serves 4)**

- 500g crawfish tails, peeled (with fat if available)
- 120g unsalted butter, divided
- 3 tablespoons all-purpose flour
- 1 medium onion, finely diced
- 3 stalks celery, finely diced
- 1 green bell pepper, finely diced
- 4 cloves garlic, minced
- 250ml shellfish stock or shrimp stock (or substitute chicken stock)
- 1/2 teaspoon cayenne pepper
- 1/2 teaspoon paprika
- 1/4 teaspoon dried thyme
- Salt and black pepper
- 4 green onions (scallions), sliced
- Fresh parsley, chopped
- Cooked long-grain white rice to serve

**Method:**

1. In a heavy-bottomed pan or Dutch oven, melt 60g butter over medium heat. Add flour; cook, stirring constantly, for 2–3 minutes until the roux is golden blonde and smells nutty (not dark — this is a lighter roux than gumbo).

2. Add onion, celery, and bell pepper. Cook in the roux over medium heat until vegetables are very soft, about 8–10 minutes.

3. Add garlic, cayenne, paprika, and thyme. Cook 1 minute.

4. Add stock gradually, whisking to prevent lumps. Bring to a gentle simmer; cook 5 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the sauce has thickened.

5. Add crawfish tails and any crawfish fat. Season with salt and pepper. Cook 5–7 minutes until crawfish are heated through and just cooked. Do not overcook.

6. Remove from heat. Add remaining 60g butter in pieces, stirring to incorporate into the sauce. (This is the finishing butter — it adds richness and gloss.)

7. Taste; adjust seasoning and cayenne.

8. Serve over white rice, garnished with green onions and parsley.

*Étouffée is best served immediately — the crawfish become firmer as they sit. If making ahead, finish the sauce without crawfish, refrigerate, and add crawfish just before serving.*
