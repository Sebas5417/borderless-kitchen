Klepon appears on almost every traditional Javanese snack spread — the collection of kue tradisional that accompany weddings, ceremonies, and Indonesian afternoon tea. It is also sold by street vendors and at pasar (traditional markets) throughout Java and Bali in small banana leaf cups or woven baskets.

The combination that makes klepon distinctive — pandan-scented rice dough, molten palm sugar center, fresh coconut coating — is a classic example of how Indonesian traditional cooking balances texture (soft and slightly chewy dough, liquid interior, moist shredded coconut exterior) with flavor (herbal pandan, earthy sweet palm sugar, fresh tropical coconut).

## The Three Elements

**Pandan dough:** The dough is made from ketan (glutinous rice flour, not regular rice flour), colored and scented with pandan — the aromatic green leaf that appears across Southeast Asian cooking. Fresh pandan juice, extracted by blending pandan leaves with water and straining, produces the deep jade color and the characteristic herbal-sweet fragrance. Pandan paste or extract can be substituted, but fresh juice produces both better color and more complex flavor.

Ketan flour produces a soft, slightly chewy, somewhat sticky dough when combined with water. It is different from regular rice flour — the glutinous variety has more starch and creates the characteristic mochi-like texture.

**Palm sugar filling:** Gula jawa or gula merah — dark Indonesian palm sugar — is chopped or grated into small pieces and enclosed inside the dough. When the klepon are boiled, the palm sugar melts into a liquid center. This is the critical element: the klepon must be sealed perfectly, or the liquid sugar will escape during boiling. The moment you bite in and the liquid caramel palm sugar floods out is the defining experience.

**Fresh coconut:** Freshly grated coconut (not desiccated or dried), lightly salted, and sometimes briefly steamed to prevent fermentation. The fresh coconut provides moisture, slight sweetness, and a specific fresh tropical flavor that dried coconut cannot replicate. Outside tropical regions where fresh coconut is unavailable, frozen fresh grated coconut (available at Southeast Asian grocery stores) is the acceptable substitute.

## The Technique

Klepon are boiled, not steamed or fried. They cook in boiling water for 3–5 minutes; the moment they float to the surface, they are done and should be removed immediately. Overcooking causes them to burst and the palm sugar filling to leak out.

The critical step is sealing: after enclosing the palm sugar inside the dough, pinch and smooth the opening until completely sealed, and roll gently between the palms until a smooth sphere forms with no visible seams.

**Recipe: Klepon (Makes 20–24 pieces)**

**Dough:**
- 200g ketan flour (glutinous rice flour — Thai "sticky rice flour" works)
- 10–12 pandan leaves, or 1 teaspoon pandan paste
- 80–100ml warm water (from pandan extraction or plain)
- Pinch of salt

**Filling:**
- 100g gula jawa or gula merah (palm sugar), finely chopped or grated
- (Each klepon needs about 1/2 teaspoon of palm sugar)

**Coating:**
- 150g freshly grated coconut (or frozen fresh grated coconut, thawed)
- 1/4 teaspoon salt
- Banana leaf cups or small paper cups for serving (optional)

**Method:**

1. **Pandan juice:** Blend 10–12 pandan leaves with 100ml water. Strain through a fine sieve, pressing to extract all green juice. You need about 80–90ml of pandan juice.

2. **Dough:** Combine ketan flour and salt. Add pandan juice gradually, mixing until a soft, pliable dough forms. It should not be sticky or crumbly — adjust with a little more water or flour as needed. The dough should be the consistency of soft playdough.

3. **Prepare coating:** If using fresh grated coconut, mix with salt and optionally steam for 5 minutes to extend shelf life. Spread on a plate.

4. **Forming:** Pinch off 1 tablespoon of dough. Flatten in your palm. Place 1/2 teaspoon of palm sugar pieces in the center. Bring the edges up around the filling and pinch firmly to seal completely. Roll gently between palms into a smooth sphere, about 2.5cm diameter. Ensure no cracks or openings — the palm sugar will leak through any gap.

5. **Boiling:** Bring a large pot of water to a rolling boil. Add klepon in batches — don't crowd. When they float to the surface, cook 30 seconds more, then remove immediately with a slotted spoon.

6. **Coating:** While still hot, roll each klepon in the coconut coating, turning to coat all over.

7. **Serving:** Arrange in banana leaf cups or on a plate. Serve at room temperature.

*Klepon are best eaten the day they're made. The palm sugar inside solidifies as they cool — slightly, not completely. Reheat briefly in a steamer if making ahead.*
