A cooking thermometer can tell you what’s happening inside a piece of food or help you keep track of a pot of oil. Start with the job you do most often. That will tell you whether one thermometer covers it.

## For a quick check, choose instant-read

An instant-read probe gives you a spot reading. It’s a practical first thermometer if you mostly cook cutlets, chops, fish or smaller portions and want to check different pieces quickly. Look for a slender tip, an easy-to-read display and a probe long enough to use comfortably. Remove it after taking the reading unless the manufacturer says the model is designed to stay in.

## For batches of frying, consider an oil thermometer

A clip-on thermometer rated for frying can keep the display visible between batches. Check that the probe still reaches the oil at the depth you normally use and that the clip fits your pot. The probe should sit in the oil without touching the pan. If you usually shallow-fry in a skillet for recipes such as [chicken katsu](/journal/chicken-katsu-recipe-japanese-fried-chicken), check the model’s minimum immersion depth before buying.

## For a long roast, consider a leave-in probe

An oven-rated probe with an alarm lets you watch one spot while the roast cooks. Check the limits for both the probe and its cable, and check the probe placement instructions for your cut. An instant-read can still help you check another spot before serving. Follow the device’s instructions for direct flame, broiling and other high-heat settings.

## Before you buy

- Choose a model rated for the heat and cooking method you’ll use.
- Check the probe and clip against your most-used pan or pot.
- Start with a single device for the task you do every week.
- Follow the manufacturer’s cleaning and care directions.
