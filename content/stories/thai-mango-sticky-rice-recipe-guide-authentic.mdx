Pad see ew is sold at street stalls throughout Thailand, often from the same cart as pad kra pao and other quick-stir-fry dishes. The cook works with a single wok over a powerful gas burner, the flames visible through the wok's bottom, reaching temperatures that home stoves can barely approach.

"Wok hei" (from Cantonese — "wok breath") is the term for the specific charred, smoky quality that high-heat wok cooking imparts to food. It's the flavor of caramelized soy sauce, slightly charred noodle edges, and the Maillard reaction happening fast. Pad see ew is one of the dishes where wok hei matters most — without it, the dish is flat. With it, there's a depth and complexity that justifies the street food pilgrimage.

## The Noodles

**Sen yai:** Wide (about 1–2cm) flat rice noodles. The width is essential — wider noodles have more surface area for sauce contact and caramelization, and the texture (chewy, slightly slippery) is distinct from thin noodles.

**Fresh:** Fresh wide rice noodles are best. Available at Asian grocery stores in the refrigerated section. If the noodles are stuck together (common when cold), separate them gently before cooking; don't rinse — the surface starch helps sauce adhere.

**Dried:** Dried wide rice noodles (soaked in cold water 30 minutes, then drained) work but are softer and less chewy.

## The Wok Temperature Problem

This is the central challenge. Thai restaurant woks are fueled by burners producing 100,000–200,000 BTU. Home gas burners typically produce 10,000–20,000 BTU. The temperature difference is enormous.

**Home adaptations:**
- Cook in very small batches (one or two servings maximum)
- Preheat the wok until it begins to smoke before adding oil
- Use a carbon steel wok (heats faster and more evenly than non-stick)
- Don't add too much sauce at once — add it in stages

**The parking lot method:** Some cooks use a gas grill's side burner outdoors — these run hotter than indoor ranges and allow more authentic temperatures.

## The Sauce

The sauce is simple:
- Dark soy sauce: sweet, thick, deeply colored
- Oyster sauce: savory, slightly sweet
- Fish sauce: salt and funk
- White sugar (small amount)

The dark soy sauce is what creates the caramelized, slightly bitter-sweet coating on the noodles. Regular (light) soy sauce doesn't have the same coloring power or sweetness.

**Recipe: Pad See Ew (Serves 1–2, cook in batches)**

- 200g fresh wide rice noodles (sen yai), separated
- 150g chicken breast or thigh, thinly sliced (or tofu)
- 2 stalks Chinese broccoli (gai lan), stems cut into 3cm pieces, leaves roughly chopped (or substitute regular broccoli florets + bok choy)
- 2 eggs
- 3 tablespoons vegetable oil
- 2 cloves garlic, minced

**Sauce (mix together first):**
- 2 tablespoons dark soy sauce
- 1.5 tablespoons oyster sauce
- 1 tablespoon fish sauce
- 1 teaspoon white sugar

**Serving:**
- White pepper
- Vinegar with sliced chilies (traditional condiment)
- Lime wedge

**Method:**

1. Mix all sauce ingredients together; set aside.

2. Heat wok over the highest heat possible until smoking. Add 2 tablespoons oil.

3. Add garlic; stir 15 seconds.

4. Add chicken; cook without moving for 1 minute to sear, then stir-fry until cooked through, 2–3 minutes. Push to the side.

5. Add remaining 1 tablespoon oil. Add noodles — don't stir immediately; let them sit against the hot wok for 30–60 seconds to develop color.

6. Add sauce. Toss everything together.

7. Push to the side; crack eggs into the wok. Scramble lightly, then fold into the noodles before fully set.

8. Add Chinese broccoli. Toss 1 minute until stems are tender-crisp.

9. Taste; adjust with more fish sauce (salt) or dark soy (sweetness/color).

10. Plate immediately. Season with white pepper. Serve with the vinegar condiment and lime.
