Korean royalty ate bingsu in the Joseon dynasty period (1392–1897) — blocks of ice stored in royal ice houses (seokbinggo) were shaved in summer and served with pine nuts and fruit. The modern democratic version — piled high with red beans, rice cakes, and condensed milk, served in a massive bowl designed to be shared — emerged in the 20th century and has since been elevated into both a casual street food and a competitive specialty dessert at high-end Korean cafés.

The range of patbingsu today spans from the traditional pat bingsu (simply red beans and ice and condensed milk) to elaborate modern bingsu that can take 20 minutes to construct and cost $20–$30 at specialty cafés. The Joseon royal ancestors would not recognize the mango bingsu towers at Seoul cafés, but would understand the impulse.

## The Ice

This is the technical element that separates Korean bingsu from other shaved ice traditions. Traditional patbingsu is made from milk-based ice — a mixture of milk, condensed milk, and sometimes sugar is frozen into blocks and then shaved on a commercial machine designed for this purpose. The result is not the chunky, crystalline ice of a snow cone or the coarser shaved ice of Hawaiian shave ice — it is feathery, almost powdery, with tiny curls that fall on themselves and have a slightly milky, creamy flavor on their own.

The milk-based ice means the dessert has sweetness and body even before toppings are added. A snow cone covered in red beans would taste icy and slightly off; properly made milk bingsu covered in red beans tastes like a complete dessert.

Home version: blend sweetened milk and freeze in a shallow container. Scrape into shavings with a fork. The result is rougher than commercial bingsu but captures the milky flavor.

## The Red Beans (Pat)

Pat — sweet red bean paste or whole cooked sweetened red beans (팥) — is the essential topping that gives patbingsu its name. The beans are cooked until very soft, then sweetened with sugar. For bingsu they are typically whole beans, not smooth paste, so the texture is present alongside the ice.

Korean sweet red beans (adzuki beans) are cooked longer and more thoroughly than Western preparations, until they have almost a creamy interior texture while maintaining their shape. The sweetness is gentle — enough to define the beans as dessert but not cloying.

## Classic Toppings

Beyond the red beans, classic patbingsu toppings include:

**Condensed milk:** Drizzled generously over everything — provides sweetness and creaminess. This is the sauce element.

**Tteok:** Chewy rice cake pieces — small, soft, slightly sweet. The textural contrast with the ice.

**Roasted soybean powder (injeolmi):** Injeolmi bingsu — one of the most popular modern variants — adds roasted soybean powder (konggaru) over tteok, creating an earthy, nutty sweetness. The soybean powder is specifically the element that makes injeolmi bingsu different from others.

**Fresh fruit:** Strawberries (strawberry bingsu is a major seasonal variant in spring), mango (summer), watermelon.

**Ice cream:** One scoop placed on the peak of the ice mountain.

**Cereal/granola:** Corn cereal (similar to Corn Pops) appears in some traditional preparations.

**Recipe: Classic Patbingsu (Serves 2)**

**Milk ice (make 1 day ahead):**
- 400ml whole milk
- 4 tablespoons sweetened condensed milk
- Pinch of salt
Combine, stir until dissolved. Pour into a shallow container and freeze flat until solid (4–6 hours or overnight).

**Sweet red beans:**
- 200g dried adzuki beans, soaked overnight
- 100g sugar
- Pinch of salt

Drain beans; cover with fresh water and bring to a boil. Drain and rinse. Return to pot with 600ml water. Simmer 45–60 minutes until beans are very tender but holding shape. Add sugar and salt; cook 10 more minutes, stirring. Cool. Beans keep refrigerated 1 week.

**Mochi tteok (or buy at Korean grocery):**
- Small rice cake pieces, about 2cm. Simmer in water until soft (3 minutes), drain.

**To assemble:**
1. Remove milk ice from freezer. Using a fork or the back of a spoon, scrape vigorously across the surface to produce fine, powdery shavings. Pile into a large bowl.
2. Top with sweet red beans.
3. Drizzle condensed milk generously.
4. Add tteok pieces.
5. Optional: add a small scoop of vanilla ice cream on top.
6. Dust with roasted soybean powder (injeolmi style) or matcha powder.
7. Serve immediately — bingsu melts quickly.

*For finer ice: blend the frozen milk mixture in a high-powered blender (Vitamix style) and re-freeze shallowly, then scrape again. Produces finer, more authentic texture than fork-scraping.*
