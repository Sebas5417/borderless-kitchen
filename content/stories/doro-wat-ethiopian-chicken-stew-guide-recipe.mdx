In Ethiopia, doro wat is not everyday food. It is celebration food — served at Ethiopian Christmas (Genna, January 7), at weddings, at Timkat (Epiphany), and at any important family gathering. The labor-intensive preparation, the quantity of spiced butter, and the long slow cooking time mark it as a dish for occasions.

The two key preparations that make doro wat what it is — and that separate authentic doro wat from simplified versions — are **berbere** and **niter kibbeh**.

## Berbere

Berbere is an Ethiopian spice blend: dried red chilies are the dominant ingredient, but the blend includes 15–20 spices — fenugreek, coriander, allspice, cloves, ginger, cardamom, black pepper, bishop's weed (ajowan), and others. The specific proportions vary by household and region.

Good commercially prepared berbere (available at Ethiopian grocery stores and online) is an acceptable starting point. Fresh-ground berbere is more complex. The recipe below includes a simplified berbere; for a full authentic blend, purchasing Ethiopian-made berbere from an Ethiopian store will produce better results.

## Niter Kibbeh

Niter kibbeh is spiced clarified butter — butter clarified (milk solids removed) and infused with onion, garlic, ginger, and spices (turmeric, cardamom, fenugreek, bishop's weed). It is to Ethiopian cooking what ghee is to Indian cooking or smen to Moroccan cooking: the foundational cooking fat that carries the flavors of the cuisine.

Making niter kibbeh takes 30 minutes and produces a batch that keeps refrigerated for weeks. For doro wat, niter kibbeh is not optional — it is where much of the dish's characteristic flavor lives.

## The Onions

Doro wat begins with a spectacular quantity of red onions — 1–2 kg for a pot serving 6–8 people. These are cooked dry (no fat initially), stirring constantly, until they reduce dramatically, caramelize, and become almost paste-like. This "dry frying" of onions is a specific Ethiopian technique that removes moisture before adding fat, producing deeply caramelized, intensely flavored onion base.

**Recipe: Doro Wat (Serves 6–8)**

**Simplified berbere (or use store-bought):**
- 3 tablespoons ground dried red chili (Ethiopian berbere chilies or cayenne)
- 1 tablespoon paprika
- 1 teaspoon ground fenugreek
- 1 teaspoon ground coriander
- 1/2 teaspoon ground allspice
- 1/2 teaspoon ground ginger
- 1/4 teaspoon ground cardamom
- 1/4 teaspoon black pepper
- Mix together; use 3–4 tablespoons in the recipe

**Niter kibbeh (simplified):**
- 200g unsalted butter
- 1/4 onion, roughly chopped
- 3 cloves garlic, smashed
- 1 tablespoon fresh ginger, chopped
- 1/4 teaspoon turmeric
- 1/4 teaspoon cardamom

Melt butter over low heat. Add all aromatics. Simmer 20 minutes on very low heat. Strain through fine sieve. Cool. This is your niter kibbeh.

**Doro wat:**
- 1 whole chicken, cut into 12 pieces (or 1 kg bone-in pieces), skin removed
- Juice of 1 lemon
- 1 kg red onions, finely minced
- 3 tablespoons niter kibbeh (recipe above) + 2 tablespoons vegetable oil
- 4 tablespoons berbere
- 4 cloves garlic, minced
- 1 tablespoon fresh ginger, minced
- 250ml chicken stock or water
- 6 hard-boiled eggs, peeled and scored (cut shallow slits so sauce penetrates)
- Salt

**Method:**

1. Toss chicken pieces with lemon juice and salt. Let sit 30 minutes.

2. In a large heavy pot, cook minced red onions over medium heat DRY (no fat) — stir constantly. They will release liquid initially, then as that evaporates, they will begin to brown and darken. This takes 20–30 minutes. The onions should reduce to about 1/3 their original volume and be very dark and paste-like.

3. Add niter kibbeh and oil. Stir into the onions.

4. Add berbere. Stir to combine with the onion-butter base. Cook 3–5 minutes, stirring constantly, until very fragrant and darkened.

5. Add garlic and ginger; cook 2 minutes.

6. Add chicken pieces; stir to coat with the berbere-onion base.

7. Add stock. Bring to a simmer. Cover; cook over low heat 30–35 minutes until chicken is cooked through and very tender.

8. Add scored hard-boiled eggs. Stir gently to coat with sauce. Cook 10 more minutes so eggs absorb the berbere color and flavor.

9. Taste; adjust salt and berbere.

10. Serve on injera, or with rice. Each person should receive at least one egg.

*Doro wat is traditionally eaten in complete silence for a brief period after the diner begins, as a sign of appreciation for the cook and the meal.*
