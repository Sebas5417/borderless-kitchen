Algeria's culinary tradition sits at the intersection of North African Berber cooking, Arab influence, Ottoman cooking (via 300 years of Ottoman rule), and French colonial cooking (which introduced baguette culture alongside the tagine tradition). Chorba frik belongs to the oldest layer — the Berber and Arab North African tradition of grain-and-meat soups, the kind of food cooked in one pot over fire, designed to nourish and sustain.

**Frik** — dried young green wheat, cracked or whole — is the grain that defines this soup. When dried young, before the wheat is fully mature, it retains a slightly grassy, smoky flavor that is entirely distinct from standard cracked wheat or bulgur. Frik releases starch as it cooks, thickening the broth from within rather than by added thickener.

Chorba frik is also distinguished by dried mint — not fresh, not mint oil, but crumbled dried mint leaves rubbed between the palms and added to the broth. The flavor it produces is warm, slightly dusty, intensely aromatic, and characteristically Algerian.

## The Lamb

Bone-in lamb shoulder or neck is ideal. The bones contribute collagen, which gives the finished broth a silky body. Cut into medium pieces; the meat is cooked in the broth from the beginning, softening over 45–60 minutes.

Lamb leg is leaner and acceptable; bone-in is strongly preferred over boneless for broth quality.

## The Tomato

Peeled tomatoes (fresh, blanched and peeled, or canned) blended into a paste are the base of the broth. The tomato gives chorba its characteristic red-orange color and a light acidity.

## The Spices

Ras el hanout — the North African spice blend (typically including coriander, cumin, cinnamon, ginger, black pepper, turmeric, and floral notes) — is the primary seasoning. Additionally: paprika, cinnamon stick, and dried mint (critical).

**Recipe: Chorba Frik (Serves 6–8)**

- 800g bone-in lamb shoulder or neck, cut into pieces
- 200g frik (broken green wheat; find at Middle Eastern grocery stores)
- 100g chickpeas, soaked overnight and drained (or 1 can, rinsed)
- 400g tomatoes, peeled and pureed (or 1 can crushed tomatoes)
- 1 large onion, grated
- 3 cloves garlic, minced
- 2 tablespoons olive or vegetable oil
- 1 heaped teaspoon ras el hanout
- 1 teaspoon sweet paprika
- 1/2 teaspoon ground cumin
- 1 cinnamon stick
- 1 tablespoon dried mint, crumbled (more to taste)
- Salt and black pepper
- Fresh parsley or coriander for finishing
- Lemon wedges and fresh bread to serve

**Method:**

1. Heat oil in a large pot. Brown the lamb pieces in batches until colored on all sides. Remove; set aside.

2. In the same pot, sauté grated onion and garlic over medium heat until golden.

3. Add pureed tomato, ras el hanout, paprika, cumin, and cinnamon stick. Cook, stirring, for 5 minutes until paste is fragrant and slightly darkened.

4. Return lamb to pot. Add 2–2.5 liters of water. If using dried chickpeas: add now. Season with salt.

5. Bring to a boil; skim any foam. Reduce to a simmer; cook covered for 45 minutes.

6. Add frik (rinse first). If using canned chickpeas: add now. Continue simmering 25–30 minutes, stirring occasionally, until frik is fully cooked and broth is thick.

7. Add crumbled dried mint; stir well. Taste and adjust salt.

8. Serve hot, garnished with fresh parsley or coriander, with lemon wedges and bread. The bread — ideally a baguette, reflecting Algeria's French colonial period — is used to mop the bowl.

*Without frik:* Substitute fine bulgur (add in final 15 minutes, use less water) or small pasta (ditalini). The dried mint and lamb broth remain essential; the grain is a variable.
