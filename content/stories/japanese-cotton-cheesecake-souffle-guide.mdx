Japanese cotton cheesecake (*Japanese soufflé cheesecake*, スフレチーズケーキ) is the dessert that appears in slow-motion videos on social media every few years, wobbling in hypnotic slow motion when tapped. That wobble is real, and it's structural — the result of a specific technique that produces a texture somewhere between cheesecake and cloud.

It is also the most demanding thing in Japanese baking, more technically difficult than shokupan milk bread or mochi. It requires a proper meringue, a precise bain-marie, controlled oven temperature, and careful cooling. When it works, it's an extraordinary dessert. When it doesn't, you have a sunken, cracked, or rubbery disk.

This guide explains the science behind each stage so you understand why things go wrong and how to prevent them.

## What Makes It Different

**Standard American cheesecake:** Dense, firm, rich. Made from cream cheese + eggs + sugar + sour cream. No flour, no meringue. Baked at moderate temperature. Cracked top = acceptable.

**Japanese cotton cheesecake:** Light, airy, jiggly. Made from cream cheese + whipped egg whites (meringue) + a small amount of flour and cornstarch. Baked in a water bath at low temperature. Structure is entirely dependent on the meringue being properly incorporated.

The key difference is the meringue. Standard cheesecake doesn't have one. Japanese cotton cheesecake is built around one — the egg whites are whipped separately to stiff peaks, then folded carefully into the cream cheese base. This is exactly the technique used in a soufflé.

## The Complete Recipe

**Equipment needed:**
- 20cm (8-inch) round springform pan OR regular cake pan with high sides
- Stand mixer or hand mixer
- Large roasting pan (for water bath)
- Instant-read thermometer (helpful)
- Kitchen scale (required — this is baking)

---

### Ingredients

**Cream cheese base:**
- 250g full-fat cream cheese, room temperature
- 55g unsalted butter, room temperature
- 100ml whole milk
- 6 egg yolks
- 60g cake flour (lower protein than all-purpose — essential for lightness)
- 20g cornstarch
- 1 tablespoon lemon juice
- 1 teaspoon vanilla extract

**Meringue:**
- 6 egg whites (cold, no trace of yolk — any fat prevents proper whipping)
- 100g caster sugar (fine granulated)
- ¼ teaspoon cream of tartar (or ½ teaspoon lemon juice — stabilizes the meringue)

---

### Preparation

**Pan preparation:**
Line the base and sides of the springform pan with parchment paper. Grease the pan well. The lining must extend above the sides of the pan by 2-3cm — the cheesecake rises during baking and needs room.

Wrap the outside base of the springform pan tightly with 2-3 layers of heavy-duty aluminum foil to prevent water bath leaking in. This is non-negotiable with a springform pan.

---

### Step 1: Make the Cream Cheese Base

In a saucepan over low heat, combine cream cheese, butter, and milk. Stir continuously until fully melted and smooth — do not let it boil. Remove from heat.

Cool slightly, then whisk in egg yolks one at a time until smooth. Add lemon juice and vanilla.

Sift cake flour and cornstarch into the cream cheese mixture. Whisk until fully incorporated with no lumps. The mixture should be smooth and pourable.

Set aside to cool to room temperature.

---

### Step 2: Make the Meringue

This is the critical step. The meringue must reach the right stage — not too soft, not too stiff.

In a completely clean, dry bowl, whip egg whites with cream of tartar on medium speed until foamy. Begin adding sugar gradually, 1 tablespoon at a time, while whipping. Increase to high speed.

**Target stage: stiff peaks that bend slightly at the tip.** When you lift the beater, the peak should stand up but curve slightly at the top. If the peaks are completely rigid and look dry, you've gone too far — the meringue will be difficult to fold and the cheesecake will crack.

**Test:** the bowl can be held upside down without the meringue falling.

---

### Step 3: Fold Together

This is where most failures happen.

Add one-third of the meringue to the cream cheese base. Whisk it in vigorously — this is the "sacrificial" portion that lightens the dense base so the remaining two-thirds doesn't deflate.

Add the remaining meringue in two additions, folding gently with a rubber spatula. Use the classic folding technique: cut down through the center, sweep under the batter, fold up and over, rotate the bowl 90°. Repeat.

The goal: no white streaks of meringue visible, but still airy. Overmixing deflates the meringue and produces a dense, flat cheesecake. Undermixing leaves unincorporated streaks that create uneven texture.

---

### Step 4: Bake in Water Bath (Bain-Marie)

Pour batter into the prepared pan. Tap gently on the counter to release large air bubbles.

Place the pan in the roasting pan. Add boiling water to the roasting pan until it reaches halfway up the sides of the cheesecake pan.

**Oven temperature:** 160°C (320°F) for 25 minutes, then reduce to 140°C (285°F) for 35-40 minutes. Total baking time approximately 60-65 minutes.

The water bath serves two functions:
1. It moderates the oven temperature immediately surrounding the cheesecake, preventing the rapid temperature swings that crack the surface
2. It keeps humidity high in the oven, preventing the exterior from drying and hardening before the interior sets

**How to tell when it's done:** The top should be golden brown and the cheesecake should wobble as a whole when gently shaken — like set Jello, not liquid. The center should not ripple independently. An instant-read thermometer should read 70-75°C in the center.

---

### Step 5: Cooling (As Important as Baking)

Immediate temperature changes cause collapse and cracking.

Turn off the oven. Leave the cheesecake in the oven with the door slightly ajar (prop with a wooden spoon) for 15-20 minutes. This gradual cooling prevents the rapid contraction that causes cracks.

Remove from oven and water bath. Cool on a rack at room temperature for at least 1 hour before refrigerating.

Refrigerate minimum 4 hours, preferably overnight. The cheesecake firms up significantly during refrigeration.

---

## Why It Goes Wrong (And How to Fix It)

**Sunken center:**
- Caused by underbaking, opening the oven door early, or cooling too rapidly
- Prevention: don't open the oven for the first 45 minutes; gradual cool-down

**Cracked top:**
- Caused by oven too hot, insufficient water in the water bath, or meringue over-whipped
- Prevention: water bath must stay full; check temperature calibration; don't over-whip

**Dense, rubbery texture:**
- Caused by meringue overmixed during folding (deflated) or oven too hot
- Prevention: fold gently, check oven temperature with a separate thermometer

**Collapsed around edges:**
- Caused by water leaking into the springform pan
- Prevention: triple-wrap the pan base with foil; consider using a solid cake pan with a water bath below

## Serving

Japanese cotton cheesecake is served cold or slightly above room temperature, never warm. The structure depends on chilling.

Traditional serving: bare, dusted with a small amount of powdered sugar. The cheesecake is the dessert; it doesn't need embellishment.

It can be served with fresh fruit, a small amount of whipped cream, or a simple fruit sauce — but these should complement rather than obscure.

Keeps refrigerated 3-4 days, though the airy texture is best on days 1-2.

## Where to Find It

In Japan, cotton cheesecake is widely sold at bakeries, supermarkets, and specialty shops. *Uncle Tetsu's* cheesecake chain — famous for selling cheesecakes still hot from the oven, with lines around the block — popularized the "hot jiggly cheesecake" format.

In department store basement food halls (*depachika*), Japanese cheesecake sections are common, with multiple brands selling single slices or whole cakes.

The experience of eating a freshly baked, warm cotton cheesecake is genuinely different from the refrigerated version — more mousse-like, more delicate. Both are worth having.
