Sake is the most versatile Japanese cooking liquid, and it's largely underused in Western kitchens. Most cooks who keep sake around use it for a teriyaki glaze and stop there. That's about 20% of what sake does in Japanese cooking.

The full range: sake deglazes pans and picks up fond like white wine, it tenderizes meat and fish by denaturing proteins at the surface, it eliminates fishy and gamey odors through ethanol volatilization, it contributes umami (sake contains some glutamate from the fermentation process), and it rounds out saltiness in ways that water doesn't. Understanding these functions tells you when to use it and when substitution makes sense.

---

## What sake actually is

Sake is brewed from rice, water, yeast, and koji (*Aspergillus oryzae* — the same mold used in miso and soy sauce production). The koji converts rice starches to sugars; yeast ferments those sugars into alcohol. The result is a rice wine with 15-20% alcohol by volume, significantly higher than regular wine.

Unlike most wine, sake's flavor is intentionally neutral — it should taste clean, slightly sweet, with notes of rice and a mild acidity. The alcohol content is deliberately high because it's meant to evaporate quickly during cooking, leaving behind the flavor compounds without the alcohol.

## What sake does in cooking

**Deglazingand building sauces**

When sake hits a hot pan, the alcohol evaporates immediately, pulling the Maillard compounds (fond) off the pan surface and into the liquid. This is the same function as white wine in French cooking, with a cleaner, less acidic result.

For pan sauces: deglaze with 3-4 tablespoons sake, let it reduce by half, then add your other liquids (dashi, stock, mirin, soy). The sake's initial flash of high-alcohol heat pulls the fond cleanly; the reduction concentrates its flavor.

**Eliminating fishy and gamey odors**

This is sake's most distinctive function and one without a good Western equivalent. The ethanol in sake binds to trimethylamine and other volatile compounds responsible for fishy and gamey notes, then carries them off as the alcohol evaporates during cooking.

The technique: before cooking fish, drizzle with 1-2 tablespoons sake and let rest 5 minutes. Blot dry before cooking. The process removes much of the surface fishiness. The same technique works on chicken liver, lamb, and other gamey proteins.

**Tenderizing**

The alcohol in sake denatures surface proteins slightly, which relaxes muscle fibers and allows marinades to penetrate more effectively. For thin cuts (fish fillets, chicken thighs), 30 minutes in a sake-based marinade is enough. For denser cuts (pork shoulder, beef short rib), overnight in a sake-soy-ginger marinade produces notably more tender results.

**Adding depth without adding flavor**

This sounds contradictory but it's accurate: sake adds something that people often describe as "rounder" or "deeper" without adding an identifiable flavor note. The glutamate and other amino acids from fermentation contribute umami; the alcohol carries volatile aromatic compounds out of the pan, clarifying other flavors. The net effect is that everything in the dish tastes more like itself.

## Cooking sake vs. drinking sake

**Cooking sake (ryorishu)** is sold at Japanese grocery stores and some Asian markets. It's sake with added salt (typically 1.5-2%) and sometimes sweeteners. The salt prevents it from being consumed as a beverage (avoiding alcohol tax regulations) and also means you'll need to adjust salt in recipes that call for it.

**Drinking sake** produces better results in cooking — cleaner flavor, no added salt, more aromatic complexity. The cheapest mid-range drinking sake (Ozeki, Gekkeikan, Sho Chiku Bai) works well for all cooking applications. You don't need premium sake for cooking.

**Which to buy:** If you'll cook with it regularly, a mid-range drinking sake is the better long-term investment. If you'll use it rarely, cooking sake is cheaper and has a longer shelf life once opened (the salt acts as a preservative).

**Storage:** Sake oxidizes after opening. Store in the refrigerator; use within 3 months for drinking sake, up to 6 months for cooking sake. Unlike wine, oxidized sake doesn't develop vinegar-like acidity — it just becomes flat.

## Amounts and ratios

The classic Japanese sauce ratio is a useful starting framework:

**Teriyaki glaze:** 2 tablespoons sake + 2 tablespoons mirin + 2 tablespoons soy sauce (reduces to a coating glaze for 2 portions of protein)

**Nimono (simmered dishes):** 1 cup dashi + ¼ cup sake + 2 tablespoons mirin + 2 tablespoons soy sauce

**Shabu-shabu broth:** 4 cups water + 2 strips kombu + ½ cup sake + 1 tablespoon soy sauce

For non-Japanese applications:
- **As a white wine substitute in deglazing:** 1:1 ratio — if the recipe calls for ¼ cup white wine, use ¼ cup sake
- **In braises:** Replace up to 25% of the liquid volume with sake
- **For fish or chicken odor removal:** 1-2 tablespoons per portion, 5-minute rest before cooking

## Sake in Italian cooking

The functions that make sake useful in Japanese cooking apply directly to Italian techniques:

**Risotto:** Replace the white wine in standard risotto with sake. The initial addition of white wine in risotto serves to add acidity and deglaze the rice — sake does both while contributing a cleaner umami note. The result is a softer, less wine-forward base that works especially well in seafood risotto and mushroom risotto.

**Clam pasta (vongole):** Traditional spaghetti alle vongole uses white wine to steam the clams and build the sauce. Sake works better — it eliminates any marine odor while contributing the same steaming alcohol, and its umami content adds a second flavor dimension the white wine doesn't have.

**Braised short rib:** A standard Italian-style braised short rib uses red wine. Replacing 20-25% of the red wine with sake lightens the braise without diluting it, and the sake's tenderizing effect on the exterior of the meat complements the long braise.

**Aglio e olio:** Adding 2 tablespoons sake after the garlic is softened, before the pasta water goes in, adds a background layer of umami that's indistinguishable as sake but rounds out what can otherwise be a very sharp dish.

## What to substitute when you don't have sake

The right substitute depends on what sake is doing in the dish.

**For deglazing / building sauces:**
- Dry white wine at 1:1 — adds more acidity, which can be appropriate
- Dry vermouth at 1:1 — more herbal, lower alcohol, deglazes less aggressively
- Dry sherry at ¾ the amount — richer flavor, works well in braises and sauces that already have soy

**For odor removal (fish and game):**
- Dry white wine — works, less effective than sake on marine odors specifically
- A splash of rice wine vinegar + water (1 part vinegar, 3 parts water) — the acidity helps, the alcohol is less effective

**For depth in sauces:**
- Dry white wine + a few drops of mirin (for sweetness) — approximates sake's flavor profile
- There is no substitute for sake's specific combination of high alcohol + low acidity + fermentation-derived amino acids — the substitutes approximate parts of it

**For tenderizing marinades:**
- Dry white wine at 1:1 — lower alcohol content means slightly less tenderizing effect, extend marinade time by 30-50%

## Recipes to start with

If you've never cooked with sake, these three build familiarity with its different functions:

**1. Sake-steamed clams:** Steam 500g clams in ½ cup sake with one minced garlic clove and 2 tablespoons butter. The sake steam eliminates marine odor and the residual liquid is a perfect dipping broth. 8 minutes total.

**2. Teriyaki glaze:** Combine 2 tablespoons each sake, mirin, and soy sauce. Reduce by half in a small pan. Brush on chicken thighs or salmon in the last 3 minutes of cooking. This is the fundamental Japanese sauce ratio and builds intuition for how these three liquids interact.

**3. Sake-marinated salmon:** Drizzle 2 tablespoons sake over 2 salmon fillets and let rest 10 minutes. Pat dry. Sear skin-side down in a dry pan until crispy. The sake treatment eliminates the metallic note that salmon often develops in a pan and crisps the skin more cleanly.

After these three, sake starts to feel like a tool with a specific use rather than an exotic ingredient — which is exactly what it is.
