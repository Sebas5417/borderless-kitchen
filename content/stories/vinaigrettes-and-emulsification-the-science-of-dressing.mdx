Oil and water don't mix. Vinegar is mostly water. This is the fundamental obstacle in every vinaigrette, and understanding how to temporarily overcome it — and why it keeps failing if you skip the right steps — is everything.

A vinaigrette is an emulsion: a suspension of two liquids that don't naturally combine. When you shake oil and vinegar together, you get thousands of tiny oil droplets temporarily dispersed through the vinegar. Within minutes, those droplets find each other, merge, and the dressing separates back into distinct layers. This is physics, not failure. The goal is to slow or prevent that separation using an emulsifier.

---

## The Ratio

The standard French ratio is 3 parts oil to 1 part acid. This ratio produces a dressing that is rich without being oily, acidic without being sharp.

**3:1 = correct for most applications.**

Adjust in one direction only based on the acid:
- Strong vinegars (red wine, sherry): 3:1 or even 4:1
- Mild acids (champagne vinegar, rice wine vinegar): 2.5:1 or 2:1
- Lemon juice: 3:1, but cut with a tiny bit of water if the lemon is very intense

The instinct to add more oil to "mellow" a sharp dressing is correct — increase the ratio, don't reduce the acid. Reducing the acid removes the balance point; increasing the oil preserves the acidity while diluting its impact.

## Emulsifiers: What Holds It Together

An emulsifier is a molecule with one end that's attracted to water (hydrophilic) and one end attracted to fat (lipophilic). It inserts itself between oil droplets and the surrounding water, preventing the droplets from merging.

**Mustard** is the most effective emulsifier in a basic vinaigrette. Even half a teaspoon of Dijon contains compounds (mucilage from the mustard seed) that dramatically slow separation. A well-made Dijon vinaigrette can stay emulsified for hours. Whole-grain mustard works similarly.

**Egg yolk** is the emulsifier in mayonnaise and Caesar dressing. Lecithin in the yolk is a powerful emulsifier — a single yolk can hold an entire cup of oil in suspension. This is the extreme end of emulsification.

**Garlic paste** (minced to a fine paste with salt) has mild emulsifying properties. Useful for body and flavor.

**Honey** adds mild emulsification and also thickens the dressing slightly, which slows separation mechanically.

**Without any emulsifier:** the dressing separates in 30 seconds. Add mustard and it holds for hours. This is not a subtle difference.

## The Base Vinaigrette

Makes enough for one large salad or two side salads.

**Ingredients:**
- 3 tablespoons extra-virgin olive oil
- 1 tablespoon red wine vinegar (or other acid)
- ½ teaspoon Dijon mustard
- 1 small garlic clove, minced to a paste with salt
- Pinch of salt
- Freshly ground black pepper

**Method 1 — jar (easiest):**
Combine everything in a small jar with a tight lid. Shake vigorously for 20–30 seconds. Taste on a piece of lettuce, adjust.

**Method 2 — whisk:**
Combine vinegar, mustard, garlic, salt, and pepper in a small bowl. Whisk to combine. Add oil slowly — starting with drops, then a thin stream — whisking constantly. The dressing will emulsify as the oil incorporates.

**Method 3 — food processor or blender:**
For large batches or creamier emulsification, process everything except the oil, then stream the oil in while running. The mechanical action creates a finer, more stable emulsion.

**Tasting:** Always taste vinaigrette on the actual greens you'll dress, not alone. A dressing that tastes aggressively acidic from the jar might taste perfectly balanced on fatty avocado or bitter arugula. Dress a single leaf, taste, adjust.

## Variations

The base framework — acid + emulsifier + oil + aromatics — holds across every variation:

**Lemon-herb:**
Lemon juice + Dijon + garlic + olive oil + fresh thyme/parsley/chives. Excellent on fish, grains, roasted vegetables.

**Sherry-walnut:**
Sherry vinegar + Dijon + shallot + walnut oil (or 50/50 walnut and olive). Deep and nutty, excellent on bitter greens (radicchio, endive, frisée) with blue cheese.

**Honey-Dijon:**
White wine vinegar or champagne vinegar + Dijon + honey + neutral oil. Versatile, crowd-pleasing, slightly sweet. Works on everything.

**Red wine-Dijon (classic French):**
Red wine vinegar + Dijon + garlic + olive oil. The reference dressing. Perfect on butter lettuce, frisée, or a simple green salad.

**Tahini dressing (not a vinaigrette, but same logic):**
Tahini + lemon juice + garlic + water + salt. Tahini is already an emulsifier — it produces a thick, stable dressing without additional emulsifiers. Add water to thin.

**Caesar:**
Lemon juice + Worcestershire + anchovy paste + garlic + egg yolk + olive oil + Parmesan. The egg yolk creates a much more stable emulsion than mustard — this dressing holds for days.

## Acid Choices and Their Characters

**Red wine vinegar:** Sharp, fruity, versatile. The default for most European-style vinaigrettes.

**White wine vinegar:** Lighter than red, less assertive. Good for delicate salads where you don't want the dressing to compete.

**Champagne vinegar:** The mildest wine vinegar. Almost neutral in color, very subtle flavor. Good for herb-forward dressings.

**Sherry vinegar:** Complex, nutty, oxidative — similar to balsamic but drier. Excellent in dressings for bitter or hearty greens.

**Balsamic vinegar:** Sweet and thick. Not a traditional vinaigrette acid — often combined with red wine vinegar to add sweetness without making the dressing syrupy. True aged balsamic is drizzled, not whisked.

**Apple cider vinegar:** Mild and slightly fruity. Good for coleslaw, grain salad, anything with sweetness in it.

**Lemon juice:** The brightest and freshest acid. Always use fresh — bottled lemon juice has a cooked, flat flavor. Lime juice is an excellent substitute in Thai, Mexican, or grain salad contexts.

**Rice wine vinegar:** Very mild. The base for Japanese-style dressings, works well with sesame oil, ginger, and soy.

## Common Mistakes

**Wrong ratio:** Too much acid makes the dressing sharp and unpleasant; too much oil makes it greasy and flat. Start at 3:1 and adjust.

**No emulsifier:** The dressing separates immediately, pools at the bottom of the bowl, and leaves greens drowning in oil while the acid sits elsewhere. Add mustard.

**Over-dressing:** Salad should be lightly coated, not swimming. Dress, toss, taste. The greens should glisten, not weep.

**Dressing warm lettuce:** Heat wilts greens immediately on contact with dressing. Always dress cold or room-temperature greens.

**Using inferior olive oil:** The oil is half the dressing. Extra-virgin olive oil with actual flavor makes a dramatically better vinaigrette than a neutral or oxidized olive oil. Taste your oil before using it in dressing.

---

Vinaigrette is one of the fastest recipes in cooking and one of the highest-leverage skills. A well-made dressing transforms simple greens into something worth eating; a poorly made one turns good ingredients into a puddle. The ratio, the emulsifier, and the quality of the acid and oil — these are the three variables. Get them right and the salad takes care of itself.
