**Short answer:** mirin is a sweet rice wine used for sweetness and gloss. Rice vinegar is a mild vinegar used for acidity. They are opposites in the pan — one sweet, one sour — and they are not interchangeable.

---

## Side by Side

| | Mirin | Rice vinegar |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Sweet rice wine | Vinegar fermented from rice |
| Taste | Sweet, rounded | Sour, mild, clean |
| Job | Sweetness, shine, takes the edge off soy | Brightness and acidity |
| Classic use | Teriyaki, simmered dishes, glazes | Sushi rice, pickles, dressings |
| Watch out for | "Aji-mirin" or "mirin-style seasoning" — lower alcohol, added sugar | "Seasoned" rice vinegar — already contains sugar and salt |

---

## Mirin

True mirin (*hon-mirin*) is brewed from glutinous rice and contains alcohol; most supermarket bottles are *aji-mirin*, a mirin-style seasoning with added sugar. Both work in everyday cooking. Mirin is what makes teriyaki glossy and balances soy sauce in simmered dishes. Our [mirin entry](/culture/mirin) has the detail.

## Rice Vinegar

Milder and less sharp than most Western vinegars. Buy **unseasoned** rice vinegar for general cooking so you control the sugar and salt; the seasoned kind is a shortcut made for sushi rice. See [rice vinegar](/culture/rice-vinegar).

→ [Shop unseasoned rice vinegar on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=unseasoned+rice+vinegar&tag=borderlesskitchen-20)

## Can You Swap Them?

**No.** Mirin in a dressing makes it syrupy; rice vinegar in teriyaki makes it sour. If you are out of mirin, a splash of sake or dry sherry with a little sugar is closer than vinegar. If you are out of rice vinegar, a diluted white wine vinegar or apple cider vinegar is closer than mirin.
