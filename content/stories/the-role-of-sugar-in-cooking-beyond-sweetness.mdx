Sugar appears in recipes that are not, by any obvious measure, sweet. Teriyaki sauce. Tomato pasta. Braises. Cured meats. In each case, the cook who added sugar wasn't trying to make the dish taste like dessert. They were using sugar as a functional ingredient — deploying one or more of its four distinct roles in cooking.

Those roles are: sweetness, caramelization, Maillard browning enhancement, and water-binding. Understanding each one — what it does, at what temperatures it activates, and how different sugars behave differently — makes it possible to use sugar deliberately rather than following a recipe without understanding why the sugar is there.

---

## Role 1: Sweetness and acid balance

The most obvious role of sugar is the least interesting from a technical standpoint, but even sweetness is more nuanced than it seems. Sugar doesn't just add sweet flavor — it modulates the perception of other flavors. The most important of these interactions is acid balance.

Acid (sourness) and sweetness are perceived antagonistically: the presence of one suppresses the perception of the other. This is why a small amount of sugar added to a tomato sauce made from acidic canned tomatoes doesn't make the sauce taste sweet — it makes it taste less sour. The sugar hasn't eliminated the acid; it's reset the perceived balance. You're adding sugar to remove a negative perception, not to add a positive one.

Italian tomato sauce frequently calls for a pinch of sugar or a grated carrot (whose natural sugars serve the same function) for exactly this reason. Japanese dishes accomplish the same balance using mirin — a sweetened rice wine — which brings sweetness alongside a layered, fermented complexity that raw sugar doesn't have. The practical difference: mirin's alcohol carries aromatic compounds that evaporate during cooking, leaving sweetness that integrates into the sauce rather than sitting on top of it.

When to add sugar for balance: late in cooking, so you can taste and adjust precisely after the acid has concentrated and the final balance is clear.

## Role 2: Caramelization

Caramelization is the thermal decomposition of sugar molecules in the absence of proteins — pure sugar chemistry, not the Maillard reaction (more on that distinction below). When sugar is heated above its melting point, the sucrose molecules break down into glucose and fructose, then further into hundreds of different aromatic compounds including diacetyl (buttery), maltol (toasty), and 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (caramel).

The critical fact for cooking is that different sugars caramelize at different temperatures:

- **Fructose** begins caramelizing at approximately 110°C (230°F)
- **Glucose** caramelizes at approximately 160°C (320°F)
- **Sucrose** (table sugar) caramelizes at approximately 160–180°C (320–356°F)

Fructose's lower caramelization temperature is why honey (which is high in fructose) browns so quickly in the oven, and why dishes glazed with honey need lower temperatures or shorter cooking times to avoid burning. It's also why teriyaki sauce — which traditionally uses mirin and soy sauce, both of which contain significant reducing sugars including glucose — caramelizes readily on a hot pan or grill, developing a lacquered glaze without requiring very high temperatures.

Practical implication: when you want caramelization to happen fast on the surface of meat or vegetables without overcooking the interior, use a high-fructose sweetener (honey, mirin, or certain fruit-based glazes). When you want slower, more controlled browning, sucrose is more forgiving.

## Role 3: Maillard browning enhancement

The Maillard reaction is often confused with caramelization, but they are distinct processes. Caramelization involves only sugars. The Maillard reaction requires both a reducing sugar and an amino acid — it's the chemical interaction between these two molecules that produces the hundreds of flavor and color compounds responsible for the brown crust on seared meat, the color of roasted coffee, and the flavor of toasted bread.

Sugar's role in the Maillard reaction is as a reactant and a rate accelerator. More reducing sugars present in a protein-containing food means more Maillard reaction, faster. This is why sugar appears in dry rubs for meat — not to make the meat taste sweet, but to ensure that the surface browns rapidly and deeply when it hits a hot pan or grill. The amino acids in the meat provide the other half of the reactant pair.

This principle explains several otherwise puzzling recipe choices:

**Teriyaki glaze** contains sugar not just for sweetness but to ensure deep, even browning on the protein surface. The sugar interacts with surface amino acids the moment it hits heat.

**Milk and egg washes on bread** brown better than water washes because they contain lactose (a reducing sugar) and proteins — the complete Maillard reactant pair.

**Mirin in Japanese cooking** contributes reducing sugars that enhance browning on yakitori, gyoza skins, and teriyaki, producing the characteristic lacquered appearance of well-made Japanese grilled dishes.

Timing matters: sugars for Maillard enhancement need to be on the food's surface when it hits high heat. A sugar-containing marinade that's wiped off before searing loses most of this function. A glaze applied during cooking keeps replenishing surface sugar.

## Role 4: Water-binding and preservation

Sucrose molecules are hydrophilic — they attract and bind water molecules. At sufficient concentration, this property does two useful things: it lowers the water activity of a food (reducing the availability of free water that microorganisms need to grow), and it alters the texture of foods by competing with proteins and starches for available water.

In preservation, this is why jam, candied fruit, and cured items like gravlax use sugar at high concentrations. The sugar doesn't kill bacteria — it starves them of the free water they need. Traditional Japanese tsukudani (small fish or vegetables simmered in soy sauce and sugar until very concentrated) relies on the same principle for its long shelf life.

In texture, sugar's water-binding competes with starch for hydration during baking, slowing gelatinization and producing a more tender crumb. It's why cookies made with more sugar spread more (bound water keeps the dough fluid longer) and why reducing sugar in a cake recipe without compensation can make it dry.

For savory cooking, the practical water-binding application is in marinades and cures. A sugar-salt brine draws moisture from the protein surface osmotically, then allows that moisture (now carrying the sugar and salt) to be reabsorbed. The result is a protein that seasons more deeply and retains moisture better during cooking than an unseasoned one.

## Mirin vs raw sugar: when the difference matters

Mirin is worth discussing separately because it's frequently misunderstood as a sugar substitute — which it isn't, exactly. Hon-mirin (true mirin) is a fermented rice wine with a residual sugar content of approximately 40–50%, an alcohol content around 14%, and a complex array of amino acids, organic acids, and flavor compounds produced during fermentation.

The alcohol in mirin performs two functions that raw sugar cannot: it carries volatile aroma compounds into the dish as it evaporates, and it tenderizes protein surfaces (alcohol denatures surface proteins, opening them slightly for flavor penetration). The fermentation-derived compounds add a subtle depth — mild umami from amino acids, gentle acidity from organic acids — that raw sugar is entirely missing.

The practical difference between using mirin and using dissolved sugar in a teriyaki or nimono (simmered dish) is real but nuanced. Raw sugar makes the dish sweet. Mirin makes it sweet, aromatic, and slightly complex. For dishes where the sweetener is a background element, the difference is small. For dishes where the sweetener is a primary flavor component — classic teriyaki tare, Japanese simmered fish, mirin-glazed vegetables — using raw sugar instead of mirin produces something noticeably flatter.

The reverse is also worth noting: in applications where you want precise sweetness control without the fermented character, raw sugar is more predictable. In a tomato sauce where you're correcting acidity, a measured pinch of sugar is easier to calibrate than a splash of mirin, which brings additional flavors you may not want.

---

Sugar in savory cooking is always functional. When you see it in a recipe, the question isn't "why is this sweet?" — it's "which of the four functions is this sugar serving?" In most cases, it's more than one simultaneously. A teriyaki tare sweetened with mirin is balancing the salt and soy acid, caramelizing during cooking, enhancing Maillard browning on the protein, and contributing water-binding that gives the glaze its lacquered texture. That's four functions from one ingredient. Understanding them lets you adjust — and substitute — with purpose rather than guesswork.
