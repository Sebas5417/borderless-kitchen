Japanese home cooking needs less equipment than people expect. A sharp knife and a reliable way to cook rice cover most weeknights; everything after that makes one job easier.

This guide is in priority order. Buy from the top and stop when your cooking stops asking for more. Where we already have a full guide to a tool, we link to it rather than repeat it.

## Tier 1: Buy These First

### 1. A gyuto or santoku, and a whetstone

→ [Shop Japanese gyuto knives on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=tojiro+dp+gyuto+210mm&tag=borderlesskitchen-20)

**What it does:** the gyuto is the Japanese chef's knife and does the same everyday jobs as a Western chef's knife, with a thinner blade and a finer edge (keep it off bones and frozen food). The santoku is shorter and flatter, and suits cooks who mostly push-cut vegetables.

**Can you substitute?** Yes. A sharp Western chef's knife handles any Japanese recipe. The Japanese knife is an upgrade, not a requirement.

**What to look for:** a 210mm gyuto or a santoku around 170mm, in a stainless steel such as VG-10 for a first knife. Skip sets. Our [first Japanese knife guide](/journal/japanese-knife-buying-guide-beginners) covers shape and steel, the [knife types guide](/journal/japanese-knives-guide-types-and-how-to-choose) explains nakiri, yanagiba and deba, and [best Japanese knives for home cooks](/journal/best-japanese-knives-for-home-cooks) has picks by budget.

→ [Shop King 1000/6000 whetstones on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=king+1000+6000+combination+whetstone&tag=borderlesskitchen-20)

Budget for the whetstone at the same time. Hard Japanese steel needs a stone, not a pull-through sharpener. A 1000/6000 combination stone is the one to start with; grits and the first-session routine are in our [whetstone guide](/journal/best-whetstone-for-japanese-knives-how-to-choose).

### 2. A rice cooker

**What it does:** cooks short-grain rice the same way every time and keeps it warm for hours.

**Can you substitute?** Yes. A heavy pot with a tight lid makes good rice once you learn the timing. A rice cooker earns its place if you eat rice several times a week.

**What to look for:** a Japanese brand such as Zojirushi or Tiger, a heavy inner pot, and the right capacity. Japanese rice-cooker cups are 180 ml, so a 3-cup model suits one or two people and a 5.5-cup model a family. Models and price tiers are compared in our [rice cooker guide](/journal/best-japanese-rice-cooker-for-home-cooks), and what to put in it is in our [short-grain rice guide](/journal/best-short-grain-rice-for-japanese-and-korean-cooking).

### 3. Cooking chopsticks (saibashi)

→ [Shop saibashi cooking chopsticks on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=saibashi+long+cooking+chopsticks+bamboo&tag=borderlesskitchen-20)

**What they do:** long chopsticks for turning, stirring, beating eggs, plating and frying. Once you're used to them, they replace tongs and a spatula for many jobs.

**Can you substitute?** Tongs and a fork do the same work, less precisely.

**What to look for:** bamboo or wood, roughly 30 cm or longer so your hand stays clear of hot oil. They're cheap, so buy two pairs.

### 4. A shamoji (rice paddle)

**What it does:** turns and serves rice without crushing the grains, and folds vinegar into sushi rice with a slicing motion.

**Can you substitute?** A flat wooden spatula works. Most rice cookers come with a paddle, so check before you buy one.

**What to look for:** a plastic paddle with a textured, dimpled surface sticks less. A wooden one should be wetted before each use. The [sushi kit guide](/journal/best-sushi-making-kit-what-you-actually-need) covers it alongside the hangiri.

## Tier 2: When You Cook Japanese Food Every Week

### 5. A tamagoyaki pan

→ [Shop tamagoyaki pans on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=tamagoyaki+pan+rectangular+nonstick&tag=borderlesskitchen-20)

**What it does:** a small rectangular pan for rolling thin layers of egg into a neat block.

**Can you substitute?** A round non-stick pan works. The roll comes out rounder, and you can shape it afterwards in a bamboo mat.

**What to look for:** non-stick is easiest for beginners. Carbon steel and copper are the traditional choices but need seasoning. A pan of roughly 13 x 18 cm suits two to four eggs. Check that it works on your cooktop; not all are induction-ready. Technique is in our [dashimaki tamago guide](/journal/dashimaki-tamago-recipe-japanese-rolled-omelette) and the [tamagoyaki recipe](/recipes/japanese-tamagoyaki-rolled-egg-bento).

### 6. Oroshigane and other Japanese graters (for daikon and ginger)

→ [Shop Japanese daikon and ginger graters on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=japanese+oroshigane+grater+daikon+ginger&tag=borderlesskitchen-20)

**What they do:** grate daikon into a wet, fluffy mound for tempura dipping sauce, grilled fish and [agedashi tofu](/journal/japanese-agedashi-tofu-recipe), and grate ginger finely into a paste.

**Can you substitute?** A fine rasp grater handles ginger well. Daikon comes out drier and stringier on a box grater; it still works.

**What to look for:** ceramic graters with raised teeth are inexpensive and easy to rinse. Metal graters with a catch tray grate daikon faster. A small ceramic one for ginger and a larger one for daikon is a sensible pair.

### 7. Otoshibuta (drop lid)

→ [Shop otoshibuta drop lids on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=otoshibuta+wooden+drop+lid&tag=borderlesskitchen-20)

**What it does:** a lid slightly smaller than the pot that sits directly on the food. It keeps the simmering liquid washing over the top, lets the liquid reduce gently and stops pieces tumbling and breaking. It's the tool behind good [nimono](/journal/japanese-nimono-simmered-dishes-guide) and [nikujaga](/recipes/nikujaga-japanese-beef-potato-stew).

**Can you substitute?** Yes, easily. A circle of parchment with a hole in the centre, or foil with a few holes poked in it, does the job.

**What to look for:** wood (traditionally hinoki or sugi) should be soaked in water before use so it doesn't absorb the sauce. Adjustable silicone or stainless lids fit several pot sizes and are easier to clean.

### 8. A fine-mesh skimmer

→ [Shop fine-mesh skimmers on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=fine+mesh+skimmer+aku+tori&tag=borderlesskitchen-20)

**What it does:** lifts the grey foam (*aku*) from simmering meat, bones, [dashi](/journal/japanese-dashi-complete-guide) and hot pot so the broth stays clear, and scoops stray bits of batter out of frying oil.

**Can you substitute?** A large spoon removes foam, but slowly, and takes broth with it. A small fine-mesh strainer is close enough.

**What to look for:** a very fine mesh, a flat or shallow head and a long handle. For the table, the small mesh ladles in our [hot pot setup guide](/journal/best-hot-pot-setup-for-home-electric-vs-induction-vs-butane) are a different tool.

### 9. A donburi bowl and a ramen bowl

→ [Shop Japanese donburi bowls on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=japanese+donburi+bowl+set+ceramic&tag=borderlesskitchen-20)

**What they do:** a donburi bowl holds a rice bowl meal such as [gyudon](/recipes/japanese-gyudon-beef-rice-bowl), with room for the topping. A ramen bowl is larger and deeper, so noodles, broth and toppings all fit.

**Can you substitute?** Any deep, wide bowl for donburi. Most Western soup bowls are too small for a full bowl of ramen.

**What to look for:** heavy ceramic that holds heat. Donburi bowls are often around 28 to 32 oz; ramen bowls around 32 to 40 oz. If you buy one set, donburi bowls cover rice dishes and smaller bowls of ramen. Sizes, materials and the preheating habit are in our [ramen bowls guide](/journal/best-ramen-bowls-and-japanese-serving-equipment).

## Tier 3: For Specific Dishes

### 10. A donabe

**What it does:** a Japanese clay pot for hot pot, braises and rice. It heats slowly, holds heat for a long time and goes from stove to table.

**Can you substitute?** A cast-iron or enamelled Dutch oven does most of the same jobs.

**What to look for:** for a first one, a general-purpose donabe about 9 to 10 inches across; a double-lidded rice donabe if rice is the goal. Most need a gas flame and seasoning before first use. Sizes, clay and care are in our [donabe guide](/journal/best-donabe-japanese-clay-pot-how-to-choose).

### 11. Suribachi and surikogi

→ [Shop suribachi and surikogi on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=japanese+suribachi+surikogi&tag=borderlesskitchen-20)

**What it does:** a ceramic bowl with fine ridges inside and a wooden pestle. It grinds toasted sesame seeds, from coarse to a smooth paste, for goma-ae and sesame dressings.

**Can you substitute?** A spice grinder or a smooth mortar will crush sesame. Many cooks find the ridged bowl does it better and releases more aroma; how it compares with other mortars is in [molcajete vs mortar and pestle](/journal/molcajete-vs-mortar-and-pestle).

**What to look for:** a small bowl, around 5 to 7 inches across, is enough for home quantities. The ridges should be sharp and even. Clean them with a brush.

### 12. A mandoline (Benriner)

**What it does:** slices cabbage, cucumber and daikon paper-thin and evenly, and cuts julienne with the extra blades.

**Can you substitute?** A sharp knife and patience. The mandoline is faster, not essential.

**What to look for:** the standard Benriner suits cucumbers and radishes; the wider Super Benriner takes cabbage. Always use the hand guard or a cut-resistant glove. Models and the safety rule are in our [Benriner guide](/journal/best-japanese-mandoline-slicer-benriner).

### 13. A makisu (bamboo rolling mat)

**What it does:** rolls sushi, shapes tamagoyaki after cooking, and squeezes water out of blanched spinach.

**Can you substitute?** A clean kitchen towel lined with cling film can roll sushi, with less control.

**What to look for:** flat, evenly cut slats tied with sturdy string. Wrap it in cling film for inside-out rolls. Dry it fully after washing so it doesn't mould. See the [sushi kit guide](/journal/best-sushi-making-kit-what-you-actually-need).

## What to Skip

- **Knife sets.** You'll use one or two knives. Put the money into one good gyuto and a whetstone.
- **Pull-through and electric sharpeners** for Japanese knives. Most are set for softer Western steel and can chip or over-grind hard Japanese edges.
- **Boxed "complete" sushi kits.** A mat and a paddle bought separately cost less; the rice and nori matter more.
- **Single-bevel knives as a first purchase.** A yanagiba or deba is a specialist tool. Learn on a double-bevel knife.
- **Cheap, light bowls for ramen.** Thin bowls lose heat fast. Buy fewer, heavier ones.
