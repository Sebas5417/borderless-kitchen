An egg yolk begins to thicken at 149°F (65°C). It sets completely by 185°F (85°C). Within that 36-degree window lies the entire world of custard: crème brûlée, crème caramel, pastry cream, lemon curd, ice cream base, pots de crème, and the English sauce known as crème anglaise that dresses everything from poached fruit to steamed pudding.

The principle is simple. The execution requires temperature control.

---

## The Chemistry

Egg proteins are long, folded chains that unwind and link together (coagulate) when exposed to heat. In an egg cooked directly — scrambled, fried — this happens quickly and completely. In a custard, the egg is diluted with dairy and sugar, which raises the temperature at which coagulation occurs and slows the process, giving a larger window for control.

**What dilution does:**
- Milk and cream proteins and water surround the egg proteins, physically separating them and requiring higher temperatures before they can link together
- Sugar increases the coagulation temperature further by competing with egg proteins for water molecules
- Fat from cream slows heat penetration and coats protein strands, contributing to a smoother texture

**The practical result:** A mixture of cream, egg yolks, and sugar will begin to thicken around 160°F–170°F (71°C–77°C) and set to a custard between 175°F–185°F (79°C–85°C). The target for a silky, pourable crème anglaise is around 170°F–175°F (77°C–79°C). The target for a set, sliceable custard is 180°F–185°F (82°C–85°C).

Above 185°F (85°C): The proteins over-coagulate, shrink, and expel water. The result is grainy, curdled custard — the same mechanism as scrambled eggs. This cannot be reversed.

## The Two Custard Families

**Stirred custards (sauce consistency):** Cooked on the stovetop with constant stirring. The motion prevents the proteins from setting into a solid — they thicken the sauce but remain fluid. Examples: crème anglaise (vanilla custard sauce), lemon curd, pastry cream (with added starch for stability), ice cream base.

**Baked custards (set solid):** Cooked in the oven, usually in a water bath (bain-marie), without stirring. The heat reaches the custard from the outside inward; the water bath moderates the oven heat, preventing the outside from overcooking before the center sets. Examples: crème brûlée, crème caramel, pots de crème, flan, bread pudding.

## Crème Anglaise: The Stirred Custard Method

Crème anglaise is the foundational stirred custard — egg yolks, sugar, and milk (or cream), cooked on the stovetop until the sauce coats the back of a spoon. It is served as a sauce and forms the base for French ice cream (churned crème anglaise).

**Method:**
1. Warm the milk or cream with vanilla until it steams (not boiling)
2. Whisk egg yolks and sugar until pale — this dissolves the sugar and prepares the yolks
3. Temper: pour a small amount of warm dairy into the yolk mixture while whisking, to raise the yolk temperature gradually without scrambling
4. Add the yolk mixture back to the pan and cook over medium-low heat, stirring constantly, until the sauce thickens to coat a spoon (draw a finger across the spoon — the line should hold)
5. Strain immediately through a fine-mesh strainer into a bowl set over ice water — stop the cooking immediately

**The nappe test (coat the spoon):** When a wooden spoon is dipped in the sauce and a finger drawn across it leaves a clean, defined trail that doesn't run, the sauce has reached the correct consistency (approximately 170°F–175°F / 77°C–79°C).

**Why it breaks:** Direct high heat scrambles the egg proteins before they have time to thicken the sauce gradually. Low heat and constant movement are non-negotiable.

## Crème Brûlée: The Baked Custard Method

Crème brûlée is cream, egg yolks, sugar, and vanilla, baked in a water bath until set with a slight wobble. The caramelized sugar top (brûlée) is added immediately before serving.

**Method:**
1. Warm heavy cream with vanilla until steaming
2. Whisk egg yolks and sugar until combined (no need to make them pale — less air is better in a baked custard)
3. Temper cream into yolks, strain through a fine-mesh sieve
4. Pour into ramekins in a deep baking dish. Add hot water to the dish until it reaches halfway up the ramekins
5. Bake at 325°F (163°C) until the edges are set but the center jiggles like Jell-O when gently shaken — typically 30–40 minutes depending on ramekin depth
6. Cool to room temperature, then refrigerate at least 2 hours (or overnight)
7. Before serving: sprinkle a thin, even layer of sugar over the surface. Use a kitchen torch to caramelize until deep amber, moving constantly

**The water bath:** Without a water bath, the bottom and sides of the ramekin would reach oven temperature (325°F+) while the center is still raw. The water bath caps the temperature at 212°F (100°C) — the boiling point of water — ensuring the custard heats gently and evenly from all sides.

**The wobble test:** The center of the custard should jiggle as a unit — like Jell-O, not like water. If it ripples in concentric waves, it needs more time. If it doesn't move at all, it is likely overcooked and will be rubbery when cold.

## Pastry Cream: The Starch-Stabilized Custard

Pastry cream (crème pâtissière) is a stirred custard thickened further with cornstarch or flour. The starch creates a gel network in addition to the egg protein network, producing a thick, pipeable cream that holds its shape. It is the filling for éclairs, fruit tarts, cream puffs, and mille-feuille.

**The critical difference:** Because starch stabilizes the custard, pastry cream is cooked past the point where plain custard would curdle — it must be brought to a full boil (212°F / 100°C) to activate the starch completely. At this temperature, without the starch, egg proteins would scramble; with the starch network present, they are supported and cannot curdle.

After boiling: pour into a bowl, press plastic wrap directly onto the surface (prevents a skin from forming), and refrigerate until cold. Pastry cream keeps for 2–3 days.

## Lemon Curd: Acid as a Variable

Lemon curd is a custard where citric acid from lemon juice plays a central role. Acid denatures egg proteins at lower temperatures — this is why raw eggs marinated in citrus juice eventually become opaque and solid (ceviche-style, without heat). In curd, the acid contributes tang and also assists coagulation, producing a custard that sets at a slightly lower temperature than neutral custards.

The fat in lemon curd traditionally comes from butter, added in pieces off the heat after the curd has thickened — the same mounting technique used in pan sauces. The result is silky and rich in a way that dairy fat alone does not produce.

## The Lessons That Transfer

Every egg-based dessert teaches the same thing: eggs are temperature-sensitive, and their sensitivity is predictable. Learn to:
- Temper hot liquid into eggs gradually (never pour all of it at once)
- Use low heat on the stovetop and a water bath in the oven
- Use a thermometer rather than guessing
- Stop the cooking as soon as the target temperature is reached (strain into an ice bath, remove from the oven)

These four habits produce good custard every time, in any recipe, with any ratio of eggs to cream to sugar. The specific recipe matters less than understanding what the heat is doing to the proteins, and why staying within the window — above 160°F (71°C) but below 185°F (85°C) — is the entire skill.
