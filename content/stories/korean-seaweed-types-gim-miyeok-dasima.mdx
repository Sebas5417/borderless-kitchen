Seaweed is everyday food in Korea. It wraps rice, fills the birthday soup and flavours the stock under many soups and stews. The packets look alike on the shelf, but each kind has one main job.

**Short answer:** gim is the thin sheet for rice, miyeok is the soft seaweed for soup, and dasima is the thick kelp for broth. Parae and tot are smaller players for side dishes and rice.

---

## Korean and Japanese Names

Outside Korea, these are often sold under their Japanese names.

| Korean | Japanese | English | Main job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gim (김) | Nori | Laver | Wrapping rice, snacking, garnish |
| Miyeok (미역) | Wakame | Wakame, sea mustard | Soup, salad |
| Dasima (다시마) | Kombu | Kelp | Broth |
| Tot (톳) | Hijiki | Hijiki | Side dishes, rice |
| Parae (파래) / Maesaengi (매생이) | Roughly aonori (loose match) | Green laver | Side dishes, pancakes, winter soup |

The first three line up closely. The green seaweeds are a looser match, so treat that row as a rough guide. For the Japanese side of the same three, see [kombu vs wakame vs nori](/journal/kombu-vs-wakame-vs-nori-difference).

## Gim (Laver)

**What it is:** laver pressed into thin sheets and dried, then usually roasted. It's the Korean counterpart of nori.

**How it's sold:** in two main forms.
- **Plain roasted gim**, often labelled for gimbap. Large sheets, no oil or salt. This is the one for rolling.
- **Seasoned gim** (*jomi-gim*), brushed with sesame or other oil, salted and toasted. Sold in small snack packs and in larger trays. You eat it with rice or straight from the packet.

**How to use it:** plain sheets for [gimbap](/recipes/gimbap-korean-rice-rolls); seasoned gim is oily and salty and doesn't hold a roll well. Seasoned gim goes on the table to wrap bites of rice. Crumbled or cut into strips, gim finishes noodle soups and the [Seollal tteokguk](/journal/seollal-korean-lunar-new-year-food-tteokguk). Our [kimbap guide](/journal/korean-kimbap-guide) covers rolling in more detail.

**How to store it:** gim goes soft and chewy in humid air. Keep opened packs sealed with the desiccant packet inside, and freeze what you won't use soon. A sheet that has gone limp can be crisped by passing it over a low flame or through a dry pan for a few seconds.

## Miyeok (Wakame)

**What it is:** a soft, leafy seaweed eaten as a vegetable. It's the same seaweed sold as wakame.

**How it's sold:** mostly dried, either pre-cut into small pieces or as long folded strands. Korean markets also sell salted fresh miyeok in the chiller.

**How to use it:** soak dried miyeok in cold water until soft, usually around ten minutes. It expands a great deal, so start with a small handful; a little dried miyeok fills a pot. Rinse it, squeeze it out and cut long pieces into bite-size lengths. Salted miyeok needs a good rinse and soak to remove the salt.

Its best-known use is [miyeok-guk](/journal/miyeok-guk-korean-birthday-seaweed-soup), the seaweed soup eaten on birthdays and by new mothers. Our [miyeok-guk recipe](/recipes/korean-miyeok-guk-birthday-soup) is the beef version in the photo above. Miyeok also goes into cold soups and vinegar-dressed side dishes (*miyeok-muchim*).

**How to store it:** dried miyeok keeps for a long time sealed in a cool, dry cupboard. Keep salted miyeok in the fridge and follow the date on the pack.

## Dasima (Kelp)

**What it is:** thick, stiff dried kelp. It's the same as Japanese kombu, and its job is flavour: it gives broth its savoury depth.

**How it's sold:** dried, in flat sheets or pre-cut squares. You will also find fried dasima snacks (*dasima-bugak*), which are a snack, not a broth ingredient.

**How to use it:** put a piece in cold water, heat gently and lift it out before the water comes to a hard boil. Long boiling makes the broth slimy and bitter. Dasima is one of the two bases of Korean anchovy broth, just as kombu is a base of Japanese dashi; see [dashi vs Korean anchovy broth](/journal/dashi-vs-korean-anchovy-broth). Leftover pieces can be sliced thin and braised in soy sauce as a side dish.

**The white powder:** dried dasima is often dusted with a fine white powder. This is mostly mannitol, a naturally sweet compound that comes out of the kelp as it dries, along with some salt. It is part of the flavour, not mould. Wipe the sheet with a dry or barely damp cloth to remove grit, but don't wash it under the tap. Mould looks different: fuzzy, coloured patches, usually after the kelp has been stored damp. If you see that, throw it out.

**How to store it:** sealed, in a cool, dry place. Kept dry, it lasts for months.

## Parae and Maesaengi (Green Seaweeds)

Fine, bright green seaweeds, mostly eaten in winter. Labels and sources don't always agree on the names or species behind them.

- **Parae** is usually sold fresh or frozen. It's dressed with vinegar as a side dish or mixed into savoury pancakes. You will also see *parae-gim*, sheets of gim with green laver pressed in.
- **Maesaengi** is finer still, almost like silky hair. It's best known in a winter soup with oysters. The soup looks like it has stopped steaming but holds a lot of heat, so let it cool before you take a big spoonful.

Outside Korea, both are mostly sold frozen. Store them as the pack says.

## Tot (Hijiki)

**What it is:** a dark, branching seaweed in short, chewy sprigs. It's the same as Japanese hijiki.

**How it's sold:** mostly dried, sometimes fresh or blanched in season.

**How to use it:** soak dried tot until it softens and expands, rinse it well, then blanch it. It's eaten as a side dish dressed with tofu or with a vinegar sauce, and cooked into rice (*tot-bap*).

**A caution:** some food safety agencies, including the UK's, have advised against eating hijiki because it can contain high levels of inorganic arsenic. Soaking and boiling it, then discarding the water, reduces it but doesn't remove it, but if you eat tot, keep it occasional and check the current advice where you live.

**How to store it:** dried tot keeps sealed in a cool, dry place.

## A Note on Iodine

All seaweed contains iodine, and kelp (dasima) contains a great deal of it. The amount varies widely from one batch to another. Using a piece of dasima for broth is normal Korean cooking, but eating a lot of kelp every day is a different matter. Enjoy it in moderation. If you have a thyroid condition, or take thyroid medication, ask your doctor how much seaweed is sensible for you.

## What to Buy First

- **For gimbap:** a pack of plain roasted gim.
- **For miyeok-guk:** a small bag of dried miyeok. It lasts a long time.
- **For broth:** a pack of dasima. One pack covers many pots of stock.
