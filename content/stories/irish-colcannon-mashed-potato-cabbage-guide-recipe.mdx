The potato is Ireland's most politically charged ingredient — the crop whose failure in the 1840s killed one million people and drove another million from the country in the Great Famine. Before the famine, and even after, the potato was what sustained Ireland through its winters. Colcannon is one of the oldest preparations of that potato, combining it with the other reliable winter staple: kale or green cabbage.

Colcannon is a Halloween food in Irish tradition. A large pot is made on Oíche Shamhna (Halloween night), and coins are wrapped in cheesecloth and hidden inside the mashed potato. Children who find coins keep them for luck. The tradition is ancient, linking the dish to Samhain (the Celtic harvest festival that became Halloween) and the idea of the last of the season's harvest being consumed before winter fully arrived.

Today colcannon appears year-round as one of Ireland's most reliable side dishes — served alongside bacon and cabbage (another Irish classic), with sausages, or simply eaten in a bowl with a pool of butter in the center.

## Kale vs Cabbage

**Kale (curly kale):** More traditional; the word colcannon comes from an Irish Gaelic phrase sometimes translated as "white-headed cabbage," though historians debate whether kale or white cabbage was the original.

**Green cabbage:** Produces a milder, more tender colcannon; widely used in modern recipes.

Both are correct. The choice is personal preference. Kale produces a slightly earthier, more mineral-flavored result; cabbage is sweeter and softer.

## The Ratio

Colcannon is potato forward — the greens are an addition, not an equal partner. A ratio of 3 parts potato to 1 part greens produces the correct balance.

**Recipe: Colcannon (Serves 6)**

- 1 kg floury potatoes (Maris Piper, Rooster, King Edward, or Russet), peeled
- 250g curly kale or green cabbage, finely shredded
- 6 green onions (scallions) or 2 leeks (white and light green parts), finely sliced
- 100g unsalted butter (plus more for serving)
- 150–200ml whole milk or cream, warmed
- Salt and white pepper

**Method:**

1. Boil potatoes in salted water until completely tender — a knife should go through with no resistance. Drain; return to the pot over low heat for 1 minute to steam off excess moisture.

2. Meanwhile, blanch kale or cabbage in boiling salted water for 3–4 minutes until tender but still green. Drain well; squeeze out excess water; chop finely if not already.

3. In a small pan, melt 30g butter over medium heat. Cook green onions or leeks until soft and translucent, 3–5 minutes.

4. Mash potatoes vigorously with a potato masher (or push through a ricer for smoother texture). Do not use a food processor — it makes the potato gluey.

5. Add warm milk or cream gradually; mash until smooth and creamy.

6. Fold in cooked kale or cabbage and buttered green onions. Season well with salt and white pepper.

7. Taste; adjust seasoning.

8. Serve in a large bowl with a well in the center. Add remaining butter cut into pieces; it melts into the well as you bring the colcannon to the table.

*The ritual is to eat from the outside in, dipping each spoonful into the butter pool in the center.*

*For Halloween: wrap individual coins in foil or cheesecloth; press them into the colcannon before serving. The finder keeps the coin.*
