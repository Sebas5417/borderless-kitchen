**Short answer:** buy a **wedge of Parmigiano Reggiano with the dotted name visible on the rind**, aged **about 24 months**, and grate it yourself. "Parmesan" in the US isn't a protected name. It can be a good domestic cheese or a poor one, and many pre-grated US tubs contain anti-caking agents.

---

## What the Name Guarantees

Parmigiano Reggiano is a DOP (Protected Designation of Origin) cheese. To carry the name, it must be:

- Made in a defined area of northern Italy: the provinces of Parma, Reggio Emilia and Modena, and parts of Bologna (west of the Reno river) and Mantua (south of the Po).
- Made from raw cow's milk, salt and rennet, with no additives.
- Aged at least **12 months**, then inspected before it can be fire-branded.

In the EU, courts have ruled that "Parmesan" evokes the protected name, so only genuine Parmigiano Reggiano can be sold as Parmesan there. In the US it isn't protected, so "Parmesan" can be made anywhere. The FDA standard only requires about 10 months' aging and basic composition rules, far looser than the DOP's.

---

## How to Read the Rind

- **Dotted lettering.** The words *Parmigiano Reggiano* are stamped in dots all around the rind. If your wedge includes some rind, you should see part of this.
- **Dairy number and production date** are marked on the rind too.
- **A fire brand** (an oval stamp) is applied after the cheese passes inspection.

Pre-cut wedges with no rind, or grated cheese, carry the name on the packaging instead. Look for the consortium's logo.

## Which Age to Buy

| Age | Flavor and texture | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| 12–18 months | Milder, softer, milky | Snacking, salads |
| **22–24 months** | Balanced, nutty, crumbly, the classic | **Grating over pasta, everyday cooking** |
| 30–36+ months | Sharper, drier, more crystals | Eating in chunks, with balsamic |

The white crunchy crystals in older cheese are mostly the amino acid tyrosine. They're a sign of age, not a defect.

## Grate It Yourself

Pre-grated tubs dry out quickly, and many US "Parmesan" tubs contain cellulose to keep the shreds from clumping. They melt less smoothly into sauces like [cacio e pepe](/journal/cacio-e-pepe-guide-recipe-roman-pasta-cheese-pepper). A rasp grater makes fluffy, fine cheese that melts instantly.

→ [Shop rasp graters on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=microplane+rasp+grater&tag=borderlesskitchen-20)

**Keep the rinds.** Drop one into minestrone, a bean soup or a tomato sauce while it simmers. It adds savory depth, the same glutamate that [makes Italian and Japanese cooking such good partners](/journal/the-science-of-umami-italian-japanese-fusion).
