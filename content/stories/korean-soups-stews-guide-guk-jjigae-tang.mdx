Korea is a soup culture. The Korean table is almost never complete without a bowl of something liquid — and that something could be any of three distinct categories, each with its own logic, its own ingredients, its own place in the meal.

*Guk* (국), *jjigae* (찌개), and *tang* (탕) are not interchangeable. They are three different answers to the question of how to make hot liquid food satisfying.

## Guk (국) — Light Soups

Guk is the everyday soup of Korean cooking. It's lighter and more broth-forward than jjigae, with a higher ratio of liquid to solid ingredients. Guk is served in an individual bowl for each diner — it's personal, not shared.

The role of guk at the Korean table: the warm liquid that softens the meal, provides hydration, and provides contrast to the drier components (rice, grilled meats, fried dishes). In the traditional Korean meal structure, guk is placed to the right of the rice bowl.

### The Major Guk Styles

**Miyeok-guk (미역국) — Seaweed Soup:**
Rehydrated dried seaweed simmered in beef broth and seasoned with soy sauce and sesame oil. The definitive birthday soup — Korean tradition is to eat miyeok-guk on your birthday. Mothers also eat it after childbirth because seaweed is high in iodine and calcium. The soup is associated with new beginnings, nourishment, and specifically maternal love.

**Kongnamul-guk (콩나물국) — Bean Sprout Soup:**
A very simple hangover cure — soybean sprouts simmered in anchovy broth with garlic and gochugaru. The sprouts break down and release their asparagine content, which is thought to help process alcohol. Whether or not this works pharmacologically, it does what hangover food should do: it's clean, hydrating, and slightly spicy.

**Egg Drop Soup (계란국):**
A quick egg soup made by pouring beaten egg in thin streams into simmering broth (anchovy or beef), which creates ribbons of cooked egg. Finished with sesame oil and green onion. Ready in 10 minutes.

**Kimchi-guk (김치국):**
A light soup made with well-fermented kimchi simmered in broth. Less substantial than kimchi jjigae — more broth, fewer solid ingredients, lighter flavor. The kimchi brine itself is the primary seasoning.

## Jjigae (찌개) — Thick Stews

Jjigae is the category of Korean food that gets closest to the word "stew" in English. It's thicker than guk, with more solid ingredients and a more concentrated broth. Jjigae is served in a shared communal pot — one large pot in the center of the table, with individual bowls for serving or just eaten directly from the communal pot with a long spoon.

Jjigae is the category that most non-Koreans encounter first when eating Korean food. It's hearty, deeply flavored, and accessible.

### The Major Jjigae Styles

**Doenjang-jjigae (된장찌개) — Fermented Soybean Paste Stew:**
The most important jjigae. Anchovy broth with doenjang (Korean fermented soybean paste) dissolved in, then tofu, zucchini, mushrooms, and garlic. 15 minutes. Tastes like something that cooked for much longer.

The doenjang dissolves into the broth and contributes an extraordinary depth of flavor from its long fermentation. Every Korean home has its own version — different vegetables, different ratios, different doenjang. This is the dish that most clearly separates home-cooked Korean food from restaurant Korean food.

**Kimchi-jjigae (김치찌개) — Kimchi Stew:**
Old, well-fermented kimchi is the essential ingredient — not fresh. The sourness of mature kimchi is what makes the stew's flavor. Simmer kimchi with pork (belly or shoulder), tofu, and enough water or broth to cover. The kimchi's brine seasons the broth. The pork fat makes it rich. The tofu absorbs the flavors.

The universal Korean comfort food. Every Korean has eaten this after a late night, when the only food available was leftover rice and old kimchi. The combination of sour fermented flavor and warm pork richness is precise comfort.

**Sundubu-jjigae (순두부찌개) — Soft Tofu Stew:**
A fiery stew centered on *sundubu* — extra-soft tofu, the silkiest texture, practically pourable. The broth is a deeply spiced seafood-based broth (often with clams, shrimp, or squid) made vivid red with gochugaru. A raw egg is cracked in at the table, stirred into the still-boiling stew, and consumed still slightly undercooked. Served in a stone pot (*dolsot*) that keeps the stew boiling at the table.

The contrast: the silky soft tofu absorbing the spicy broth, the raw egg folding in, the hot ceramic burning your fingers if you pick it up wrong. 

**Budae-jjigae (부대찌개) — Army Stew:**
A modern and distinctive jjigae born from the American military presence after the Korean War. Surplus American processed foods — Spam, canned baked beans, American cheese, hot dogs, instant ramen — combined with Korean gochujang, kimchi, and vegetables in a large communal pot. Originally made because these were the only available ingredients; now a popular comfort food eaten deliberately, often at late-night restaurants.

The combination sounds bizarre and tastes surprisingly cohesive — the processed pork's saltiness and the gochujang's depth work together in a way that honors its origin without being embarrassed by it.

**Haemul-jjigae (해물찌개) — Seafood Stew:**
Various shellfish, squid, and fish simmered in a gochugaru-spiced broth with tofu and vegetables. Briny, spicy, warming. The seafood provides its own broth as it cooks, making this stew self-building in a way that beef-based stews aren't.

## Tang (탕) — Rich Bone Broths

Tang is the most substantial category — long-simmered bone broths, often served as a meal unto themselves rather than as a component of a larger table. Tang takes hours to make and produces broths with body and depth that lighter cooking cannot.

### The Major Tang Styles

**Seolleongtang (설렁탕) — Ox Bone Soup:**
Ox leg bones simmered for 8-12 hours at high heat until the collagen converts to gelatin and the marrow dissolves into the broth, turning it milky white. The broth is unseasoned — each diner seasons at the table with salt and green onion to taste. Served with thin slices of boiled ox meat or tripe, and rice (sometimes added directly to the soup).

Seolleongtang is one of Korea's oldest known dishes. It's food designed for the long winter: rich, warming, deeply nourishing, and flexible enough to accommodate different seasoning preferences.

**Gomtang (곰탕) — Braised Beef Broth:**
Similar to seolleongtang but made from beef short ribs, brisket, and shank rather than bones only. The resulting broth is lighter in color but rich from the dissolved gelatin of the meat. Often sold at 24-hour restaurants because it's the archetypal late-night or early-morning restorative food.

**Samgyetang (삼계탕) — Ginseng Chicken Soup:**
A whole young chicken stuffed with glutinous rice, Korean dates (jujube), and ginseng root, then simmered in water until the chicken is falling off the bone and the broth is rich and slightly bitter from the ginseng. Eaten particularly in the hottest days of summer (a Korean counter-intuitive practice called *iyeolchiyeol* — "fighting heat with heat") and when sick.

**Galbijjim (갈비찜)** — technically a braised dish rather than a soup, but the braising liquid is substantial and it often blurs the line with tang. Beef short ribs braised with soy sauce, sesame, ginger, garlic, and fruit (Asian pear or apple) until falling-off-the-bone tender and glazed.

## The Shared Logic

All three categories — guk, jjigae, and tang — reflect the Korean table's commitment to liquid food as a necessary component of every meal. The liquid element:

- Provides hydration with flavor
- Creates a counterpoint to drier components (rice, grilled meats)
- Provides a vehicle for fermented flavors (doenjang, kimchi) that might be too intense eaten alone
- Warms the meal and the diner simultaneously

The categories scale with occasion: guk for every day, jjigae for weekday dinner, tang for Sunday, celebration, or illness.
