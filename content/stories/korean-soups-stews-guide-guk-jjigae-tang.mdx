Rice is the center of a Korean meal, and something hot and liquid is almost always next to it. A plain weeknight dinner might be rice, kimchi, a couple of side dishes and a bowl of soup. A restaurant lunch is often a bubbling pot of stew and nothing else but rice and banchan.

Korean has several words for these dishes, and they aren't interchangeable. Knowing them makes a menu easier to read and a meal easier to plan at home.

---

## The Four Words

**Guk (국)** is soup in the everyday sense: a fairly thin, often lightly seasoned broth with a few ingredients in it, served in an individual bowl next to the rice. It's meant to be eaten with the meal, a spoonful of broth between bites of rice.

**Tang (탕)** also means soup, and the line between guk and tang is blurry. Tang is often the word for richer, longer-simmered soups, especially bone broths, and for soups that are the main event of a meal. But usage is inconsistent: some dishes go by either name, and *tang* is also the more formal, Sino-Korean word. Don't look for a strict rule.

**Jjigae (찌개)** is a stew: less liquid than guk, more ingredients, more strongly seasoned, usually with doenjang, gochujang, gochugaru or salted seafood. It's salty enough that you eat it with rice, not on its own, and it's traditionally set in the middle of the table and shared.

**Jeongol (전골)** is a hot pot. Raw ingredients are arranged in a wide, shallow pot, broth is poured over, and it's cooked at the table on a portable burner, with everyone helping themselves as it simmers.

## Three Broths Behind Most of Them

- **Anchovy and kelp (myeolchi-dashima yuksu).** Large dried anchovies (heads and guts usually removed, since they can turn the broth bitter) and a piece of dried kelp, simmered briefly. This is the workhorse for doenjang-jjigae, kimchi-jjigae and many everyday guk. How it compares to Japanese dashi is in [dashi vs Korean anchovy broth](/journal/dashi-vs-korean-anchovy-broth).
- **Beef.** Brisket, shank or short ribs simmered for a clear broth, or beef bones simmered for hours until the broth turns milky. Seasoned lightly, often with soup soy sauce (*guk-ganjang*), which is saltier and lighter in colour than regular soy sauce. The differences are in our [Korean soy sauce guide](/journal/korean-soy-sauce-types-guide).
- **Pork bone.** Pork backbones or neck bones, blanched first to remove scum, then simmered long. The base for gamjatang and some styles of haejangguk.

Plenty of jjigae also start with just water, or the water left from rinsing rice, which adds a little body.

## Guk to Know

**Miyeok-guk.** Seaweed soup, usually with beef or seafood, seasoned with soup soy sauce and sesame oil. It's the birthday soup, and it's traditionally given to women after childbirth. More in our [miyeok-guk guide](/journal/miyeok-guk-korean-birthday-seaweed-soup).

**Doenjang-guk.** A thinner, gentler cousin of doenjang-jjigae: soybean paste dissolved in anchovy broth with a single vegetable, such as spinach, napa cabbage or shepherd's purse in spring.

**Kongnamul-guk.** Soybean sprout soup, clear and clean-tasting, sometimes with a little gochugaru. It's cheap, quick, and a well-known hangover cure.

## Tang to Know

**Seolleongtang.** Ox bones and beef simmered for many hours into a milky-white broth. It arrives nearly unseasoned; you add salt, pepper and chopped scallion at the table, and often some of the kimchi or radish kimchi served with it.

**Galbitang.** Beef short ribs simmered with Korean radish into a clear, savory broth, sometimes with glass noodles and an egg garnish. Make the [galbitang recipe](/recipes/korean-galbitang-short-rib-soup).

**Samgyetang.** A small whole chicken stuffed with glutinous rice, ginseng, garlic and jujubes, simmered in its own pot. It's famously eaten on the hottest days of summer, on the principle of fighting heat with heat.

**Gamjatang.** A spicy stew of pork backbone, potatoes, perilla leaves and ground perilla seeds. Despite the *tang*, it eats like a stew. The name is usually taken to mean potato stew, though some say it refers to a part of the pork spine; the origin is disputed.

**Haejangguk.** "Soup to chase a hangover." There is no single version: one common style is beef broth with napa cabbage leaves, bean sprouts and cubes of congealed ox blood (*seonji*), and regions and restaurants each have their own.

## Jjigae to Know

**Kimchi-jjigae.** Sour, well-fermented kimchi simmered with pork belly (or canned tuna) and tofu. The older the kimchi, the better. Our [kimchi jjigae guide](/journal/what-is-kimchi-jjigae-korean-kimchi-stew-guide) covers it in depth.

**Doenjang-jjigae.** Fermented soybean paste in anchovy broth with tofu, zucchini, onion, mushrooms and chili, the stew in the photo above. Probably the most-cooked stew in Korean homes. Make the [classic doenjang-jjigae](/recipes/korean-doenjang-jjigae-classic).

**Sundubu-jjigae.** Extra-soft, unpressed tofu in a spicy broth with seafood or pork, an egg cracked in at the end. It's usually served still boiling. More in our [sundubu guide](/journal/korean-sundubu-guide-deep).

**Budae-jjigae.** "Army base stew," which came out of the years after the Korean War, when ham, sausages and canned meat from US military bases found their way into a spicy kimchi broth. Today it's typically Spam, sausages, kimchi, baked beans, instant ramyeon and a slice of American cheese. It's generally associated with Uijeongbu, a city north of Seoul long home to several US Army camps.

## Jeongol

A jeongol is defined more by how it's served than by what's in it. *Beoseot-jeongol* (mushroom hot pot) layers several kinds of mushrooms with beef, tofu and vegetables in a mild broth; *gopchang-jeongol* uses beef intestines in a spicy one. Noodles or rice are often added to the last of the broth at the end.

At home, any wide shallow pot on a portable gas burner works.

---

## How They Fit the Table

In the everyday Korean meal, the *bansang*, each person traditionally gets their own bowl of rice and their own bowl of guk, while side dishes sit in the middle for everyone. A jjigae goes in the middle too, in one pot, and people dip in with their own spoons. Customs vary by household, and many restaurants now serve jjigae in individual pots. A meal will often have a guk or a jjigae rather than both, though there's no fixed rule. More on how the table is laid out in our [bansang guide](/journal/korean-bansang-meal-culture-guide).

Kimchi is almost always alongside. For bone-broth tang like seolleongtang, the classic partner is radish kimchi; see the [kkakdugi guide](/journal/kkakdugi-korean-radish-kimchi-guide).

## The Ttukbaegi

The *ttukbaegi* (뚝배기) is the small, thick-walled earthenware pot most jjigae are cooked and served in. It heats slowly but holds heat for a long time, which is why stews arrive at the table still bubbling and stay hot through the meal.

Earthenware can crack from sudden temperature changes, so a few habits matter:

- **Heat gradually.** Start on low to medium heat and raise it once the pot is warm. Don't put a cold pot, especially one straight from the fridge, over a high flame.
- **Never heat it empty.** Always have liquid or food in it on the burner.
- **Avoid cold shocks.** Don't pour cold water into a hot pot, and don't set a hot pot on a cold or wet surface. Use a trivet or wooden base.
- **Let it cool before washing.** Then wash with warm water and a soft brush or sponge, and let it dry fully.
- **Check your stove.** Most traditional ttukbaegi are made for a gas flame and won't work on induction.

Some makers recommend soaking or seasoning a new pot before first use; follow the instructions that come with yours, since advice varies by maker. If you're also considering a stone bowl for bibimbap, see [how to choose a dolsot](/journal/best-dolsot-korean-stone-bowl-how-to-choose).
