Samgyeopsal (삼겹살) means "three-layer flesh", for the stripes of fat and meat in pork belly. You will also see it spelled "samgyupsal" on menus and packaging; it's the same thing. It's one of the most common Korean barbecue meals, and one of the easiest to make at home, because there is no marinade. The pork goes on the grill plain, and all the flavour comes from what you wrap it in.

Here's how it works, what to buy, and how to cook it indoors without filling the house with smoke.

---

## What It Is and How It's Eaten

The pork belly is cut into thick slices, usually skinless, and grilled at the table. Once one side is browned, the slices are turned, then cut into bite-size pieces with kitchen scissors right on the grill. Scissors are a normal table tool in Korean barbecue, not a shortcut.

Each piece is eaten as a **ssam**, a wrap. Take a leaf of lettuce or perilla (kkaennip), add a piece of pork, a dab of [ssamjang](/journal/ssamjang-korean-bbq-dipping-sauce-guide), a slice of raw or grilled garlic, a sliver of green chili and some kimchi, then fold it up and eat it in one bite. Many people also dip the pork in a small dish of sesame oil with salt and pepper (gireumjang) before wrapping it.

Perilla is sometimes called a sesame leaf, but it's a different plant. It has a minty, slightly anise flavour. Look for it at Korean grocers; if you can't find it, lettuce on its own is fine.

---

## Which Cut to Buy, and How Thick

Ask for **skinless pork belly**, sliced about 1 cm (around 3/8 inch) thick. That's the usual thickness at Korean butchers, and thick enough to brown outside while the inside stays juicy. Korean and other Asian grocers often sell it pre-sliced and labelled for samgyeopsal. At a regular butcher, buy a slab and ask them to slice it, or partly freeze it for 30 to 60 minutes at home so it slices cleanly.

Look for even layers of fat and meat. Mostly-fat pieces shrink and smoke; mostly-meat pieces dry out.

**Variations:**
- **Ogyeopsal** ("five-layer"): pork belly with the skin left on. The skin turns chewy and crisp at the edges. Score it or cook it a little longer.
- **Daepae samgyeopsal**: very thin, almost shaved slices, often curled from being cut frozen. They cook in a minute or so and get crisp all over. Good if your heat source is weak.

For the other cuts that go on a Korean barbecue grill, see our [Korean BBQ cuts guide](/journal/korean-bbq-cuts-guide).

## How to Cook It Indoors

You need a grill surface and a way to deal with the fat. Pork belly renders a lot of it.

### Grill pan on the stove

→ [Shop Korean BBQ grill pans on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=korean+bbq+grill+pan+stovetop&tag=borderlesskitchen-20)

The simplest option. A cast-iron ridged pan or a Korean-style grill pan with a fat channel works on a normal stove. You lose the shared-grill experience, but it's the least equipment. Our [Korean BBQ grill pan guide](/journal/best-korean-bbq-grill-pan-for-home) compares the main types.

### Portable butane stove at the table

→ [Shop portable butane stoves on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=portable+butane+stove+iwatani&tag=borderlesskitchen-20)

This is a common setup in Korean homes and restaurants: a tabletop gas burner with a grill pan on top, so everyone cooks together. Use it safely:
- Use it in a ventilated room. Open a window, and don't use it in a small closed space.
- Use only the canister type the manufacturer specifies, and click it in properly.
- **Never put a pan on it that is large enough to cover the canister compartment.** Trapped heat can overheat the canister. Check that the pan you buy is sized for your stove.

Our [hot pot setup guide](/journal/best-hot-pot-setup-for-home-electric-vs-induction-vs-butane) covers the same stoves in more detail.

### Electric grill

→ [Shop electric Korean BBQ grills on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=electric+korean+bbq+grill+indoor&tag=borderlesskitchen-20)

A plug-in tabletop grill with a built-in drip tray. No flame and no canisters, which makes it the easy choice for apartments. The trade-off is that many models run cooler than gas, so the pork browns more slowly. Let it preheat fully, and don't crowd it.

### Managing smoke and fat

- **Start with a hot, dry pan.** No oil needed; the belly releases its own fat within a minute.
- **Tilt the pan or use one with a drain.** Many Korean grill pans slope so the fat runs to a channel or cup. Soak up excess fat with a paper towel held in tongs, or turn the burner off and pour it into a heatproof container (never down the sink), so it doesn't smoke.
- **Run the extractor fan and open a window** before you start, not after the smoke arrives.
- **Don't overcrowd.** Too many slices drop the temperature, and the pork steams instead of browning.

### Cook the pork through

Brown the first side well before turning, then cut it and cook until there's no pink left in the meat and the edges are golden and a little crisp. Keep separate tongs for raw pork and for serving, as you would at hot pot. If you want to check with a thermometer, the USDA guideline for whole cuts of pork is 145°F (63°C) followed by a 3-minute rest. Thin slices that are browned on both sides and opaque all the way through are usually well past that.

## Banchan and Sides

Samgyeopsal doesn't need a long list of side dishes. A typical spread:

- **Lettuce and perilla leaves**, washed and dried.
- **Ssamjang**, a dipping paste built on doenjang and gochujang. Make it from our [ssamjang guide](/journal/ssamjang-korean-bbq-dipping-sauce-guide), or buy a tub. For the pastes themselves, see [what doenjang is](/journal/what-is-doenjang-korean-fermented-soybean-paste) and [how to choose gochujang](/journal/best-gochujang-brands-how-to-choose).
- **Raw garlic cloves and green chilies**, sliced. Many people also put whole garlic on the grill to soften in the fat.
- **Kimchi**: well-fermented napa cabbage kimchi is best for grilling. Use [a good store-bought brand](/journal/best-store-bought-kimchi-how-to-choose) or [make your own](/journal/how-to-make-kimchi-complete-beginners-guide).
- **Pajeori**: thin-sliced scallions dressed with gochugaru, vinegar, sesame oil and a little sugar.
- **A simple namul**, like [kongnamul (seasoned bean sprouts)](/recipes/korean-kongnamul-bean-sprout-namul). More ideas in our [easy banchan guide](/journal/korean-side-dishes-easy-banchan-recipes).

### Grilled kimchi in the fat

Once some pork fat has collected at the low end of the pan, put kimchi there. It fries in the rendered fat, softens and caramelises, and turns sweeter and less sour. For many people this is the best part of the meal.

### Fried rice at the end

When the pork is gone, chop the last bits of meat and grilled kimchi on the pan with scissors, add cooked rice, a little gochujang or kimchi juice, and some chopped laver (gim) or sesame oil. Press it flat and let the bottom crisp before serving. It's a quick, rougher version of our [kimchi fried rice](/recipes/kimchi-fried-rice-korean-bokkeumbap).

## Shopping List

For about four people, plan on roughly 150 to 200 g (5 to 7 oz) of pork per person (600 to 800 g, about 1.5 lb, in total), more if it's the only main.

- Skinless pork belly, sliced about 1 cm thick (or ogyeopsal, or thin daepae)
- Red leaf or green leaf lettuce, and perilla leaves
- Ssamjang (or doenjang and gochujang to make it)
- 1 head of garlic and a few green chilies
- Napa cabbage kimchi, well fermented
- Scallions for pajeori
- Sesame oil, salt and black pepper for dipping
- Cooked short-grain rice for the end
- Gim (roasted laver), optional
- Butane canisters, if using a tabletop stove
