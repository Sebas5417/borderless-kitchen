In Armenia, the ability to make good khorovats is a serious social credential for men. The grill master — the khorovatschi — is a role taken with evident pride and some theater: positioning the charcoal carefully, managing the heat through positioning rather than damping the coals, reading the color of the fat dripping into the fire. Women cook everything else in Armenian cuisine; khorovats is the domain where men traditionally cook, publicly and ceremonially.

This is not unique to Armenia — most cultures' public, ceremonial fire-cooking is male-coded. What is distinctive is how central this ceremony is to Armenian social life. Khorovats is not a weeknight meal; it is an occasion food, tied to hospitality, celebration, and gathering.

The cooking philosophy is the opposite of American barbecue's elaborate rub and sauce culture. Khorovats seasoning is almost nothing: salt, pepper, and onion (for the marinade), sometimes a little pomegranate juice or wine. The meat's quality and the charcoal's character are expected to do the work. No sauce is applied before or during; the fat rendering into the charcoal and rising back as smoke is the flavoring agent.

## The Meats

Khorovats is always bone-in. Boneless cuts are not considered real khorovats by traditionalists; the bone contributes flavor, prevents the meat from drying, and gives people something to hold and gnaw on — both practical and culturally appropriate.

**Pork ribs (the most popular):** Pork belly ribs, long-bone, fatty and substantial. The fat renders and chars at the edges.

**Chicken thighs:** Bone-in, skin-on. Never breast; the fat and bone are essential.

**Lamb:** Shoulder chops or neck, bone-in. Lamb khorovats is considered the most traditional and ceremonial version.

## The Marinade (Light)

Traditional khorovats marinade: grated onion (the onion juice acts as a mild tenderizer), salt, black pepper. Some cooks add a little pomegranate juice for acidity. The marinade rests 2–4 hours, not overnight; this is not the long marination of other traditions.

## The Charcoal

Hardwood charcoal, not briquettes. The fire burns down until there are hot embers with no visible flame; khorovats cooks over ember heat, not flame. The grill level is adjusted by moving the grill itself higher or lower from the coals as needed.

**Recipe: Khorovats (Serves 6)**

**The meats:**
- 1 kg pork spare ribs, cut into individual ribs
- 6 chicken thighs, bone-in, skin-on
- 600g lamb shoulder chops, bone-in (optional — substitute more pork or chicken)

**Marinade:**
- 2 large onions, grated on a box grater; squeeze the pulp; use only the juice (about 100ml)
- 1.5 teaspoons salt
- 1 teaspoon black pepper
- Optional: 2 tablespoons pomegranate juice

**For the table:**
- Fresh lavash
- Whole spring onions
- Tomatoes, halved (grill alongside)
- Green peppers, whole (grill alongside)
- Fresh herb bundle: tarragon, basil, parsley, coriander (serve raw as fresh herbs, not cooked)
- Salt flakes for finishing

**Method:**

1. Combine onion juice, salt, pepper, and pomegranate juice (if using). Toss each type of meat separately with the marinade. Marinate 2–4 hours refrigerated.

2. Prepare charcoal: light and burn down to glowing embers with no flame. A light gray ash on the surface of the coal indicates the right temperature.

3. Arrange meats on the grill: pork ribs first (they take longest, about 25–30 minutes), chicken (20–25 minutes), lamb (15–20 minutes for medium doneness).

4. Grill over embers, turning every 5–6 minutes. The goal: deep char on the edges, fat rendered, juicy interior.

5. In the final 5 minutes, grill whole tomatoes and green peppers alongside until charred and softened.

6. Serve on a platter with lavash, grilled vegetables, fresh herb bundles, and salt.

*The traditional eating method: tear lavash; wrap a piece of grilled meat in lavash with fresh herbs; eat as one bite. The lavash also absorbs the meat juices.*
