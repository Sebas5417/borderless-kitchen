All cooking fats are not interchangeable. The differences between them — in smoke point, flavor, saturation, and stability — determine which fat belongs in a high-heat sear, which belongs in a vinaigrette, and which should never see a hot pan.

Understanding fat is not nutrition science. It is cooking science: understanding what heat does to different fats and using that knowledge to make better decisions at the stove.

---

## The Smoke Point

The smoke point is the temperature at which a fat begins to break down visibly — releasing blue-grey smoke and the acrid, bitter compound acrolein. It is not the same as the burn point, but it is the practical upper limit for cooking with that fat.

Cooking fat above its smoke point does several things:
- Destroys beneficial compounds in the fat (antioxidants, polyphenols in olive oil)
- Produces acrolein and other compounds that taste bitter and are not good to eat
- Signals that the fat is burning, not browning the food

**The smoke point is not a fixed number.** It varies based on the fat's purity (refined oils smoke at higher temperatures than unrefined), the presence of impurities and moisture (butter smokes sooner than ghee because of its milk solids), and the age of the fat (older fat smokes at lower temperatures).

**Approximate smoke points (Fahrenheit):**
- Extra virgin olive oil (unrefined): 325°F–375°F / 163°C–190°C
- Butter: 300°F–350°F / 149°C–177°C
- Coconut oil (unrefined): 350°F / 177°C
- Vegetable / canola oil (refined): 400°F–450°F / 204°C–232°C
- Grapeseed oil: 420°F / 215°C
- Avocado oil (refined): 500°F–520°F / 260°C–271°C
- Ghee (clarified butter): 450°F–485°F / 232°C–252°C
- Lard / duck fat: 370°F–400°F / 188°C–204°C
- Refined coconut oil: 400°F / 204°C
- Toasted sesame oil: 350°F / 177°C (do not cook with it — use as a finishing oil)

## The Three Categories of Cooking Fat

**High-heat fats (above 400°F / 204°C):** Refined avocado oil, refined grapeseed oil, refined vegetable and canola oils, ghee. Use for searing, deep-frying, stir-frying, and any application requiring a very hot pan. These fats have been refined to remove the impurities that would burn.

**Medium-heat fats (300°F–400°F / 149°C–204°C):** Extra virgin olive oil, butter, lard, duck fat, unrefined coconut oil. Use for sautéing, sweating vegetables, pan sauces, baking, and moderate-heat cooking. These fats have flavor that matters — using a neutral high-heat oil in their place produces an inferior dish.

**Finishing fats (do not cook — add after heat):** Toasted sesame oil, high-quality extra virgin olive oil drizzled over finished dishes, walnut oil, flaxseed oil. These fats have complex, volatile flavors that evaporate with heat and compounds that degrade at high temperatures. They belong at the table, not in the pan.

## Butter: The Most Complex Cooking Fat

Butter is approximately 80% fat, 16–18% water, and 2–4% milk solids (proteins and lactose). These three components behave very differently under heat, which is why butter is both the most versatile and the most technically demanding cooking fat.

**Whole butter in a pan:**
The water evaporates first (sizzling), then the milk solids begin to brown and toast (the Maillard reaction, same as in meat), creating the nutty, complex flavor of browned butter (beurre noisette). If heat continues, the milk solids burn to a bitter black — beurre noir, which is unusable.

**Brown butter technique:**
Melt butter over medium heat in a light-colored pan (so you can see the color change). The sizzling will subside as water evaporates. Watch closely — the milk solids will turn golden, then amber, releasing a toasted-nut aroma. Remove from heat immediately and use: drizzle over pasta, vegetables, fish, or stir into cake batter. The window between perfect and burned is 30–60 seconds.

**Ghee (clarified butter):**
When butter is melted and the water and milk solids are removed, what remains is pure butterfat — ghee. Without milk solids, it has a much higher smoke point (450°F+) and does not burn in the same way. Ghee can be used for high-heat searing while retaining the butter flavor that neutral oils lack. It is shelf-stable without refrigeration. Make it at home: melt 500g butter over low heat, skim foam, let it settle for 15 minutes, then pour through a fine-mesh strainer lined with cheesecloth, leaving the white milk solids behind.

## Olive Oil: Not Just for Low Heat

Extra virgin olive oil (EVOO) has a smoke point of approximately 325°F–375°F (163°C–190°C), which leads to the widespread belief that it should not be used for cooking — only for dressings and finishing. This is overstated.

Most stovetop sautéing, sweating aromatics, and pan sauce cooking happens below 375°F. A gentle sauté of garlic in olive oil, onions sweating for a sofrito, or vegetables softening for a braise — none of these applications exceed the olive oil smoke point. The issue arises when you heat olive oil in an empty pan at maximum heat for 5+ minutes before adding food. Under those conditions, it will smoke.

**The practical rule:** Use EVOO for flavor-forward Mediterranean-style cooking at medium heat. Use a neutral high-smoke-point oil for high-heat applications where you need a hot, empty pan. Finish dishes with EVOO after the heat is off to get its full flavor and polyphenol benefit.

**Light / pure olive oil vs. extra virgin:** Refined olive oil has a higher smoke point (390°F–470°F) than EVOO but lacks its flavor. It is a reasonable compromise for medium-high heat cooking where you want some olive oil character but need more heat tolerance.

## Saturated vs. Unsaturated Fats and Stability

The saturation level of a fat determines its stability under heat.

**Saturated fats** (butter, lard, ghee, coconut oil) are chemically stable at high temperatures because their carbon chains are fully bonded — there are no double bonds for heat or oxygen to attack. They are solid or semi-solid at room temperature.

**Monounsaturated fats** (olive oil, avocado oil) are moderately stable with one double bond. They hold up well under moderate heat but degrade faster than saturated fats at very high temperatures.

**Polyunsaturated fats** (vegetable oils, flaxseed oil, walnut oil) have multiple double bonds and are the least stable under heat. High-heat cooking with high-polyunsaturated oils degrades them faster. This is why refined versions of these oils (which have had the most reactive compounds removed) have higher smoke points than their unrefined counterparts.

## Fat as Flavor Carrier

Beyond heat stability, fat is a flavor carrier. Fat-soluble compounds — the aromatic molecules in garlic, onions, herbs, and spices — dissolve in fat and are distributed throughout a dish by it. This is why:
- Sautéing aromatics in fat before adding liquids extracts more flavor than adding them to water directly
- A vinaigrette needs fat (oil) to carry the flavor from herbs and garlic through the salad
- Finishing a dish with butter or good olive oil after cooking integrates flavors rather than just adding richness

Water cannot dissolve fat-soluble compounds. A tomato sauce cooked with no fat is flat compared to one where garlic and onion were first cooked in olive oil — the fat has moved flavor compounds from the aromatics into the sauce in a way water cannot replicate.

---

Fat selection is a decision made before you turn on the stove. The right fat for the job is determined by temperature, desired flavor, and what the fat needs to do structurally. Using the wrong fat — butter for a 500°F sear, neutral oil where olive oil's flavor is the point, sesame oil in a hot pan — produces predictably inferior results. Using the right one is one of the quietest and most consistent ways to improve everything you cook.
