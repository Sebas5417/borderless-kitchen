Galbi jjim (갈비찜) — "braised short ribs" — is the Korean dish that appears at the most significant moments: Chuseok (harvest festival), Seollal (Lunar New Year), birthday celebrations, and family gatherings where the occasion justifies hours of cooking. It is also one of the most technically straightforward Korean recipes — it requires no special equipment, no exotic technique, and a small number of ingredients — but it rewards patience in a way that shortcuts cannot replicate.

---

## What Makes It a Celebration Dish

Korean food culture has a hierarchy of effort — dishes that require more time, more skill, or more expensive ingredients are reserved for occasions worth the investment. Galbi jjim fits this hierarchy:
- **Beef short ribs are expensive** relative to the everyday proteins in Korean cooking
- **2–3 hours of braising** means this is never a weeknight last-minute dinner
- **The presentation** — glistening ribs, bright carrots and daikon, pine nut and jujube garnish — is visually distinct from everyday food

The Korean word *jjim* (찜) means "steamed" or "braised" — cooked in liquid in a covered vessel. Galbi jjim is low-and-slow braising in a savory-sweet liquid until the collagen in the short ribs converts to gelatin, which gives the sauce its glossy, coating character.

---

## The Cut: Cross-Cut vs English-Cut Short Ribs

**Cross-cut (flanken) short ribs:**
The traditional Korean galbi jjim cut — the bone is cut perpendicular to the rib, producing short strips with 3–4 small circular bone sections per strip. Each piece is approximately 2–2.5cm thick. This is sometimes labeled "LA-style galbi" at Korean butcher shops. The cross-cut exposes more bone surface, which contributes gelatin to the braising liquid.

**English-cut short ribs:**
Bone-in short rib sections cut parallel to the bone (one long bone per piece, typical of Western butcher cuts). These work for galbi jjim but are not the traditional cut — they take longer to cook and the sauce reduction behaves slightly differently.

**Bone-in vs boneless:** Bone-in is strongly preferred for galbi jjim. The bone contributes significant gelatin and flavor to the braising liquid; boneless short ribs produce a flatter sauce.

---

## The Blanching Step (Why It Matters)

Before braising, the short rib pieces are blanched in boiling water for 5–7 minutes, then rinsed under cold water. This step:
1. **Removes blood and impurities** from the surface of the meat and bones, which would otherwise cloud the braising liquid and produce off-flavors
2. **Tightens the meat surface** slightly, helping it hold together during the long braise
3. **Removes excess fat** from the surface — the blanching water will be visibly fatty; pour it off

This step is non-optional. Korean cooks who skip it consistently produce galbi jjim with murkier sauce and less clean flavor.

## The Marinade/Braising Liquid

The same mixture serves as both marinade (if used in advance) and braising liquid. It is built from:

**The flavor base:**
- Soy sauce: the primary seasoning (about 5–6 tablespoons for 1kg of ribs)
- Sugar (2–3 tablespoons) or *oligodang* (Korean oligosaccharide syrup, 1–2 tablespoons) — the sweetness balances the salt and assists caramelization at the sauce stage
- Sesame oil (1 tablespoon): added at the end, not during braising (heat destroys the flavor compounds)

**The aromatics:**
- Garlic (6–8 cloves, minced): essential
- Ginger (1 tablespoon, minced or grated): cuts the richness
- Green onion (2–3 stalks): for background savory note

**The tenderizer — Asian Pear (*bae*, 배):**
¼ to ½ Asian pear, pureed or grated, is the Korean traditional meat tenderizer in galbi dishes. Asian pear contains cysteine proteases (*actinidin*-like enzymes) that begin to break down muscle proteins in the marinade. This is the same principle as papaya enzymes in Mexican marinades or kiwi in some Western marinades.

**Important:** The enzymatic tenderizing happens during marinating, not braising. If cooking immediately without prior marinating, the pear still contributes sweetness and flavor but less tenderizing effect.

**Alternative tenderizer:** *Kiwi* (contains actinidin, a more powerful protease than pear) — use ¼ kiwi and marinate no more than 2 hours (kiwi enzymes work faster and can make the meat mushy if left too long).

## The Vegetables: Daikon and Carrot

Galbi jjim traditionally includes large chunks of *mu* (무, Korean daikon radish) and carrots, added to the pot during braising. The daikon:
- Absorbs the braising liquid and takes on a deep savory-sweet flavor
- Provides textural contrast to the soft, falling beef
- Also contributes a sweetness of its own to the sauce

Cut daikon into large chunks (5cm cubes or wedges) — small pieces disintegrate during the long braise. Carrots cut into large oblique pieces (2–3cm at the widest point).

**When to add:** Add daikon and carrots when there's approximately 45 minutes remaining in the braise — long enough to cook through and absorb flavor, not so long that they disintegrate.

## Complete Recipe

**Serves:** 4 as part of a Korean meal (with rice and banchan)
**Time:** 3 hours total (30 min active)

### Ingredients
- 1–1.5kg cross-cut beef short ribs
- 5 tablespoons soy sauce
- 3 tablespoons sugar (or 2 tbsp oligodang syrup)
- ¼ Asian pear, grated or pureed
- 6 cloves garlic, minced
- 1 tablespoon fresh ginger, grated
- 2 green onions, roughly cut
- 1 tablespoon sesame oil (add at end only)
- 1 tablespoon sesame seeds
- 200g daikon radish, cut into large chunks
- 2 medium carrots, cut in oblique chunks
- Water (approximately 2 cups to cover)

**Optional garnish:** 5–6 dried jujube dates (*daechu*, 대추), 2 tablespoons pine nuts (*jat*, 잣), soaked and peeled chestnuts (*bam*, 밤)

### Method

**1. Blanch the ribs:**
Place short ribs in a pot, cover with cold water, bring to a boil. Boil 5 minutes. The water will turn grey-brown. Drain, rinse ribs under cold water, clean the pot.

**2. Marinate (optional but recommended):**
Combine soy sauce, sugar, pear puree, garlic, ginger, and green onion in a bowl. Add blanched ribs; mix to coat. Marinate 1–2 hours (up to overnight in the refrigerator). Kiwi: marinate maximum 2 hours.

**3. Braise:**
Place ribs and marinade in a heavy pot (a *ttukbaegi* clay pot is traditional; a Dutch oven works well). Add enough water to partially submerge the ribs (approximately 2 cups). Bring to a boil, then reduce to a low simmer. Cover and braise 1 hour.

**4. Add vegetables:**
Add daikon and carrot chunks. Add jujube dates and chestnuts if using. Continue braising covered for 40–45 minutes.

**5. Reduce the sauce:**
Remove the lid; increase heat to medium. Cook uncovered 15–20 minutes, basting the ribs with the reducing sauce. The sauce should reduce to a thick, glossy coating.

**6. Finish:**
Remove from heat. Stir in sesame oil. Garnish with pine nuts and sesame seeds.

---

## Serving

Galbi jjim is served family-style in the pot or on a large platter — never plated individually. For a proper Korean celebration meal (*Chuseok*, *Seollal*, birthday), it appears alongside other *jeon* (pan-fried pancakes), *namul* (vegetable dishes), *kimchi*, and rice. The jujube date and pine nut garnish signals that this is a formal occasion dish.

**Leftovers:** Galbi jjim improves overnight. The sauce sets to a light gel as it cools (from the collagen/gelatin); reheat gently, adding a small amount of water if needed.

---

There is a specific satisfaction in making galbi jjim for a family occasion — the long, low simmer fills the kitchen with a smell that registers as "celebration" before anyone has tasted anything. It is the food Korean mothers and grandmothers make when someone they love is coming home or when something worth marking is happening. The 3 hours of braising time is not a problem to solve around; it is part of what the dish communicates.
