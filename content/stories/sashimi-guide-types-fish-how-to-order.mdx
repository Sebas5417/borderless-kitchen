Sashimi (刺身) is raw fish or seafood, cut into slices and served on its own. That's the main difference from sushi: sushi is about the vinegared rice, and the fish on top is optional (cucumber rolls and egg nigiri are both sushi). Sashimi has no rice. It's about the fish, the knife work, and a few things served alongside.

In Kansai, around Osaka and Kyoto, it's also called *otsukuri*.

## The Common Types of Sashimi

### Maguro (tuna)

Tuna is the centre of most sashimi platters, and it's sold by cut:

- **Akami**: the lean, deep red meat from the back and upper body. Clean, mineral flavour, firm texture.
- **Chutoro**: medium-fatty belly, between the lean and the fattiest cuts. Pink, softer, richer.
- **Otoro**: the fattiest part of the belly, pale pink and marbled. It nearly melts. The most expensive cut, and the smallest.

Bluefin is the most prized; bigeye and yellowfin are common and mostly lean.

### Sake / salmon

Salmon, usually *saamon* on Japanese menus, is the most familiar sashimi outside Japan, and a fairly recent one in Japan itself, where raw salmon caught on from the 1980s with farmed Norwegian fish. Farmed salmon is what most restaurants serve raw.

### Hamachi and buri (Japanese amberjack / yellowtail)

The same fish under different names by size: it's a *shusse-uo*, a "promotion fish" renamed as it grows, and the names differ by region. *Hamachi* is the younger fish and is often farmed; *buri* is the full-grown adult. Rich, buttery and mild. Wild buri caught in winter, *kanburi*, is prized for its fat.

### Tai (sea bream)

*Madai*, red sea bream, is the classic white fish: firm, clean, slightly sweet.

### Hirame (flounder)

A delicate white fish, often sliced very thin. The fin muscle, *engawa*, is a chewy, fattier treat of its own.

### Saba (mackerel)

Mackerel is rarely served fully raw. It's usually *shime saba*: salted, then marinated in vinegar, which firms the flesh and tames the strong flavour. But the FDA notes that brining and pickling may reduce the parasite hazard without eliminating it, and mackerel is a known carrier of *Anisakis*. Good shops use frozen or carefully inspected fish.

### Hotate (scallop)

The adductor muscle, sweet and tender. Hokkaido scallops are the benchmark.

### Ika (squid)

Served raw, often scored or cut into thin noodle-like strips (*ika somen*) to make it easier to chew. Firefly squid, a seasonal speciality, is a different story and usually eaten boiled; see our [hotaru-ika guide](/journal/hotaru-ika-firefly-squid-guide-japan).

### Tako (octopus)

Usually boiled first, then sliced thin.

### Ebi (shrimp)

*Amaebi*, sweet shrimp, is served raw: soft, almost sticky, and sweet, sometimes with the heads fried alongside.

### Uni and ikura

*Uni* is sea urchin: the creamy, orange-gold gonads, briny and sweet. *Ikura* is salmon roe, usually marinated in soy sauce or salt; the beads pop. Both appear on sashimi platters, though they're more often served on rice.

## How Sashimi Is Served

A sashimi plate comes with a few standard things:

- **Tsuma**: the garnishes. Usually a mound of very finely shredded daikon (*ken*), a shiso leaf, and sometimes seaweed or a carved vegetable. They're edible. The daikon and shiso freshen the palate between pieces.
- **Wasabi**: freshly grated real wasabi in better restaurants; elsewhere a paste that's usually mostly horseradish, mustard and colouring. Check the ingredient list on tubes if it matters to you.
- **Soy sauce**: in a small separate dish. Sometimes there's ponzu, or a sweeter, thicker *sashimi shoyu* (often tamari-style).

How to eat it: put a small dab of wasabi directly on the fish, then dip a corner of the slice lightly in soy sauce. A short dip, not a soak, so you still taste the fish. Mixing wasabi into the soy dish is common and nobody will stop you, but putting it on the fish keeps its aroma sharper. Eat lean and mild fish before rich ones: tai before otoro, not after.

For which soy sauce to keep at home, see [the best soy sauce brands](/journal/best-soy-sauce-brands-how-to-choose).

## Seasonality

Fish have seasons, and in Japan the menu follows them. Katsuo (skipjack) runs twice: *hatsugatsuo* in spring, lean and fresh, and *modorigatsuo* in autumn, fattier on its way back south. Buri is best in winter. Sanma, Pacific saury, is the autumn fish, and very fresh sanma is served as sashimi; more in our [sanma guide](/journal/sanma-pacific-saury-japanese-autumn-fish-guide). The bigger idea, eating each thing at its peak, is in our [guide to shun](/journal/japanese-shun-seasonal-eating-guide).

## How to Order at a Restaurant

- **Moriawase** (盛り合わせ) is an assortment, chosen by the kitchen. Often three, five or seven kinds; odd numbers are customary. It's the best way to see what's good that day.
- **Omakase** means "I leave it to you." At a sushi counter, it's a chef's choice sequence, often mixing sashimi with nigiri. It's usually set-price, so ask the price when you book or sit down if it's not listed.
- **À la carte**: ordering one fish by name. Useful when you know what you like, or want a second round of one thing.

Asking what came in that day is a normal question at a sashimi counter.

## Making Sashimi at Home

### Buying the fish

Buy from a fishmonger you trust, tell them you're eating it raw, and ask whether it was frozen.

"Sushi-grade" and "sashimi-grade" are **marketing terms in the US, not regulated grades**. No agency inspects or certifies fish under those names.

What actually matters for parasites is freezing. The US FDA's parasite-destruction guidance for fish to be eaten raw includes:

- freezing and storing at an ambient temperature of -4°F (-20°C) or below for a total of 7 days; or
- freezing at an ambient temperature of -31°F (-35°C) or below until solid, then storing at an ambient temperature of -31°F (-35°C) or below for 15 hours; or
- freezing at an ambient temperature of -31°F (-35°C) or below until solid, then storing at an ambient temperature of -4°F (-20°C) or below for 24 hours.

The FDA notes these conditions may not suit particularly large fish, such as those thicker than six inches.

A typical home freezer sits around 0°F (-18°C), which doesn't meet these, so look for fish that was commercially frozen to this standard. There are exceptions. The FDA Food Code exempts six tuna species from the freezing requirement: albacore, yellowfin, bigeye, blackfin, and Atlantic and southern bluefin (not skipjack or Pacific bluefin). It also exempts aquacultured fish, such as salmon, raised in net-pens or land-based tanks and fed formulated feed, such as pellets, that contains no live parasites infective to the fish. The FDA's hazards guidance adds that pellet-fed fish that sometimes eat wild prey may still carry parasites. So ask your fishmonger rather than assuming. Freezing controls parasites; it does **not** kill bacteria. Keep the fish cold and eat it the day you cut it.

**Who should skip raw fish:** people who are pregnant, young children, older adults, and anyone with a weakened immune system. For them, a seared or cooked dish is safer.

### The knife

The traditional sashimi knife is the **yanagiba**: long (often 270mm or more), narrow, and single-bevelled, built to cut each slice in one stroke. A long, very sharp **gyuto** (chef's knife) works fine at home. What matters most is sharpness: a dull edge tears the flesh and leaves ragged surfaces. Our [Japanese knives guide](/journal/japanese-knives-guide-types-and-how-to-choose) covers the yanagiba and gyuto, and [choosing a whetstone](/journal/best-whetstone-for-japanese-knives-how-to-choose) covers keeping either one sharp.

### Slicing

Start with a trimmed block (*saku*) and cut across the grain.

- **Hirazukuri** (flat cut): the standard. Hold the knife straight up and down, set the heel on the fish, and pull the knife toward you in one long stroke, using the whole blade. Don't saw back and forth. Slices about 1cm thick suit tuna, salmon and hamachi. Push each slice to the side with the blade as you go.
- **Sogizukuri** (angled cut): tilt the blade and slice at a low angle, pulling through. You get wider, thinner pieces. It's used for firmer white fish like tai and hirame.
- **Usuzukuri**: paper-thin, near-transparent slices of white fish. Hard; save it for later.

Chill the plates. Pile shredded daikon at the back, lean the slices against it, and add a shiso leaf and wasabi.
