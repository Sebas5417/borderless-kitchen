Gyoza and Chinese potstickers start from the same ancestor. Over decades in Japan, the dumpling evolved — thinner wrappers, more garlic and ginger, the steam-fry cooking method that creates the crispy bottom, and a lighter, more delicate filling.

The steam-fry is the technique that defines Japanese gyoza. You fry the bottom first, then add water and cover to steam the top, then uncover to let the water evaporate and crisp the bottom one more time. It's a three-stage process, and every stage matters.

## The Filling

The Japanese gyoza filling has two notable features compared to Chinese versions: it uses significantly more garlic and ginger, and the cabbage is finely shredded rather than roughly chopped.

**Makes filling for about 30 gyoza:**
- 300g ground pork (80/20 fat ratio)
- 2 cups napa cabbage, very finely shredded
- 4 cloves garlic, minced
- 1.5 tbsp fresh ginger, grated
- 2 tbsp soy sauce
- 1 tbsp sesame oil
- 1 tbsp sake (or dry sherry)
- ½ tsp white pepper
- 3 scallions, finely minced

**Salt the cabbage:** Toss shredded cabbage with ½ tsp salt. Let sit 10 minutes. Squeeze out as much water as possible in a clean kitchen towel. This step is mandatory — excess cabbage moisture makes the filling wet and prevents proper browning.

**Mix the filling:** Combine all ingredients. Mix vigorously in one direction with your hand for 1-2 minutes until the mixture becomes sticky and cohesive. This protein development is what holds the filling together.

## The Wrappers

**Use store-bought gyoza wrappers** — they're thinner and more uniform than homemade wrappers for most home cooks. Look for round Japanese gyoza wrappers (not square wonton wrappers, which are thicker).

If you're making wrappers from scratch: 200g all-purpose flour + 90ml boiling water. Knead 5 minutes, rest 30 minutes, roll thin and cut into rounds.

## Pleating Technique

Hold a wrapper in your non-dominant hand. Place 1 tsp filling in the center. Dip one finger in water and wet the rim of the half that faces you.

Fold the wrapper in half to create a half-moon. Press the center point to seal. Working from the center toward one edge, pinch and fold the front half into small pleats while the back remains flat. Repeat toward the other edge. You should have 5-6 pleats total.

The pleat isn't decorative — it creates the flat bottom the gyoza needs to stand upright in the pan.

## The Steam-Fry Method

This is what separates Japanese gyoza from every other dumpling technique.

**Stage 1 — Fry:** Heat 2 tbsp neutral oil in a wide non-stick or cast iron pan over medium-high. Arrange gyoza flat-side down, touching each other. Cook 2-3 minutes until the bottoms are golden brown.

**Stage 2 — Steam:** Pour ⅓ cup water into the pan (it will splatter dramatically). Cover immediately with a lid. Steam 5-6 minutes until the water is almost completely evaporated.

**Stage 3 — Crisp:** Remove the lid. Let remaining moisture evaporate. The sizzling will intensify as the bottoms re-crisp. 1-2 minutes more. The bottoms should be deeply golden and shatteringly crisp.

**Remove:** Slide a thin spatula under the gyoza and flip onto a plate, bottom-side up.

## Dipping Sauce

The standard ratio is equal parts soy sauce and rice vinegar, with a drop of chili oil.

- 2 tbsp soy sauce
- 2 tbsp rice vinegar
- ½ tsp toasted sesame oil
- Chili oil to taste (optional)

## Make-Ahead Notes

Uncooked gyoza freeze perfectly. Arrange in a single layer on a baking sheet, freeze until solid (2 hours), then transfer to a bag. Cook directly from frozen — add 2 minutes to the steaming time. Do not thaw first.
