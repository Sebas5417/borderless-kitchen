In Japan, New Year (*shōgatsu*) matters more than Christmas. Families gather, shops close for the first days of January, and the food follows a script that has barely changed in generations. It comes in three parts: soba on the last night of the year, a mochi soup on the first morning, and boxes of cold, make-ahead dishes to graze on for the first few days.

You don't need to make all of it. Each part works on its own, and each is easier than it looks.

---

## December 31: Toshikoshi Soba

*Toshikoshi soba* means "year-crossing soba," and eating it on New Year's Eve is a custom that goes back to the Edo period. The usual explanations: the long noodles stand for a long life, and buckwheat noodles break easily, so they "cut off" the hardships of the old year. Families eat it in the evening, often before heading to a temple to hear the midnight bells.

It's usually served hot: soba in a dashi-and-soy broth (*kake soba*) with sliced scallion, sometimes topped with shrimp tempura or a mixed vegetable fritter (*kakiage*). In Kyoto the classic is *nishin soba*, topped with sweet-simmered herring. Cold [zaru soba](/recipes/japanese-zaru-soba-recipe) with a dipping sauce counts too.

- How to cook soba both ways, and the broth: [soba noodles, cold and hot](/journal/soba-noodle-recipe-cold-and-hot-preparations)
- The topping: [kakiage, the mixed tempura fritter](/journal/japanese-kakiage-tempura-guide)

**Buying soba:** check the label. Many dried "soba" noodles contain more wheat flour than buckwheat. Noodles with buckwheat listed first taste nuttier; 100% buckwheat (*juwari*) noodles are the most fragile.

## January 1: Ozoni

*Ozoni* is the soup eaten on New Year's morning, with mochi (rice cake) as the one ingredient every version shares. Beyond that it's intensely regional, and many families have their own version:

- **Kantō (Tokyo) style:** a clear broth of dashi seasoned with soy sauce, with **square** mochi that is grilled or toasted first, plus chicken, a leafy green such as komatsuna or spinach, and a slice of kamaboko fish cake.
- **Kansai (Kyoto) style:** a broth of dashi and **sweet white miso**, with **round** mochi that is simmered rather than grilled, and root vegetables like daikon and taro.

The base of either is a good [dashi](/journal/japanese-dashi-complete-guide). For the mochi, the hard, shelf-stable blocks of cut rice cake sold as *kiri mochi* are the easiest; they toast under the grill or in a dry pan until puffed and golden. You can also [make mochi at home](/journal/japanese-mochi-recipe) from glutinous rice flour; leave out the sugar for ozoni, and simmer it rather than toasting it, since it's softer than packaged blocks.

**A safety note:** mochi is very sticky and chewy, and every New Year Japan sees choking cases from it, mostly among older people. Cut the mochi into small pieces for children and older guests, and eat it slowly.

## January 1–3: Osechi

*Osechi ryōri* are the New Year dishes packed in stacked lacquered boxes (*jūbako*). They're cooked before the year ends and eaten cold over the first days of January, partly so the cook gets a holiday too. Many families now buy their osechi from department stores or restaurants, so if you're making a small box at home, you're doing what plenty of Japanese households do: making a few favourites and buying the rest.

Almost every item carries a wish, often through a pun:

- **Kuromame** (sweet black soybeans): *mame* also means diligent and healthy, so they wish for a hard-working, healthy year.
- **Kazunoko** (herring roe): many eggs, for many children.
- **Tazukuri** (candied small dried sardines): the name means "making rice paddies," because the fish were once used as fertiliser; a wish for a good harvest.
- **Datemaki** (a sweet rolled omelette made with fish paste): its scroll shape stands for learning.
- **Kurikinton** (chestnuts in mashed sweet potato): golden, for wealth.
- **Ebi** (shrimp): the bent back of a cooked shrimp stands for living to old age.
- **Kōhaku namasu** (daikon and carrot in sweet vinegar): red and white are celebration colours.
- **Kobumaki** (kelp rolls): *kobu* echoes *yorokobu*, "joy."
- **Nishime** (simmered vegetables): often includes burdock and lotus root, whose holes are said to let you see into the future.

### A small osechi box you can actually make

Pick four or five items that keep well cold and can be made a day or two ahead:

1. **[Dashimaki tamago](/recipes/japanese-dashimaki-tamago-recipe)** in place of datemaki: a dashi-rolled omelette, sliced into thick rounds.
2. **Kōhaku namasu:** matchsticks of daikon and carrot, salted, squeezed dry and dressed in rice vinegar and sugar. It keeps for days in the fridge.
3. **Kinpira gobo:** burdock and carrot stir-fried with soy, mirin and sesame. The full recipe, and how to choose and prep burdock, is in our [gobo guide](/journal/japanese-gobo-burdock-kinpira-guide).
4. **Shrimp**, simmered briefly in dashi, soy and mirin and left to cool in the liquid.
5. **Kamaboko**, bought, sliced and alternated red and white.

Kuromame and kurikinton take longer, and good ready-made versions are sold in Japanese grocery stores in December. Buy those.
