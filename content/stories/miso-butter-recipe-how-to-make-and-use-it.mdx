Miso butter is one of those compound butters that once you make it, you can't understand why it wasn't already in your fridge. White miso and butter share the same logic: both are fat-based, fermented, and rich with glutamate. When you combine them, they don't just add together — they amplify each other.

The base ratio is simple: 2 tablespoons of white miso per 100g (3.5 oz) of unsalted butter. Soften the butter to room temperature, whisk in the miso until completely smooth, and you have a compound butter that tastes like it belongs on a Michelin-starred tasting menu.

---

## The core recipe

**Miso butter — base formula**

- 100g (3.5 oz) unsalted butter, at room temperature
- 2 tablespoons white miso (shiro miso)
- Optional: ½ teaspoon lemon juice or rice wine vinegar (brightens)
- Optional: freshly ground black pepper

Beat the butter with a fork or rubber spatula until smooth and creamy. Add the miso and beat until completely incorporated — no streaks of miso remaining. Taste. Add lemon juice if you want brightness; pepper if you want bite.

Shape into a log on plastic wrap or parchment, roll tight, and refrigerate for up to 2 weeks. Or freeze for up to 3 months.

That's it. Two minutes, two ingredients, done.

---

## Why white miso specifically

You can use any miso for miso butter, but white miso (*shiro miso*) is the right starting point for several reasons:

**Color:** White miso produces a pale butter that looks like regular compound butter. It won't turn your fish or pasta brown. Dark miso (aka miso) works but produces a noticeably darker result.

**Flavor:** White miso is fermented for a shorter time — three months versus up to three years for dark miso. The result is sweeter, less intensely salty, and more delicate. It integrates into the butter's fat without overpowering it.

**Salt level:** White miso is milder in salt than dark miso. This matters in a compound butter because the miso is providing all the salt. With dark miso, you'd need to adjust the butter-to-miso ratio to avoid oversalting.

**When to use dark miso instead:** If you're making a miso butter for a grilled steak or roasted root vegetables, red miso's deeper, more assertive flavor can be a good choice. The richer flavor holds up to stronger ingredients.

## Variations worth making

### Miso-lemon butter

Add the zest of 1 lemon + 1 tablespoon lemon juice to the base recipe. The citrus lifts the miso's fermented depth and makes this version work particularly well on fish and steamed vegetables.

**Best on:** Pan-seared salmon, steamed asparagus, halibut, linguine with clams.

### Miso-garlic butter

Add 2 cloves of roasted garlic (mashed to a paste) + ¼ teaspoon toasted sesame oil to the base recipe. The roasted garlic adds sweetness; the sesame oil adds depth without asserting its own flavor.

**Best on:** Grilled corn, mushroom pasta, garlic bread, roasted chicken.

### Miso-herb butter

Add 2 tablespoons finely chopped chives or parsley + the zest of ½ lemon to the base recipe. This is the compound butter version most similar to traditional *maître d'hôtel* butter — but with fermented depth under the herbs.

**Best on:** Steaks, grilled fish, finishing roasted vegetables.

### Miso-chili butter

Add ½ teaspoon togarashi (shichimi) or ¼ teaspoon ichimi (single red chili powder) to the base recipe. The chili heat plays against the miso's sweetness in a way plain butter with chili flakes doesn't.

**Best on:** Ramen noodles, grilled shrimp, corn, toast.

## How to use miso butter

### On pasta

This is the simplest and highest-reward application. Cook pasta to al dente; drain, reserving a mug of pasta water. Return pasta to the pan over very low heat. Add a 20g (¾ oz) knob of miso butter per serving and a splash of pasta water. Toss until the butter melts and emulsifies with the pasta water into a glossy sauce. This takes 60 seconds and uses no other ingredients.

The result: pasta with a savory, slightly sweet, deeply umami coating that tastes like a restaurant preparation.

**Works with:** Spaghetti, linguine, tagliatelle, any long pasta.

**Add:** Parmigiano for extra depth, black pepper, katsuobushi on top.

See also: [Soy Butter Linguine](/recipes/soy-butter-linguine) for a similar quick pasta technique.

### On fish

Miso butter is the classic accompaniment for pan-seared or grilled fish — it provides salt, fat, and umami in one addition without the need for a separate sauce. Place a slice of miso butter on a just-cooked piece of fish and let it melt over the surface in the residual heat.

**Works with:** Salmon (classic), halibut, cod, black sea bass, mahi-mahi, swordfish.

**Technique:** Sear fish skin-side down in a dry pan until skin is crispy. Flip, cook 2 minutes. Remove from heat. Add miso butter to the pan, spoon over the fish as it melts.

### On corn

Miso butter on corn is not a new idea — it's become a restaurant staple, particularly in the summer. The sweet starch of corn and the fermented salt of miso work exactly the same way the combination works in cooking generally: they make each other taste better.

**Method:** Cook corn any way (grill, boil, oven-roast). While hot, spread miso butter over the cob. Add togarashi and a squeeze of lime if you have them.

### On bread

Miso butter as a bread spread is vastly underrated. It has the savory depth of very good butter with salt added, but with a more complex flavor. Toast bread, spread miso butter while hot. Optionally add sliced radish, cucumber, or a soft-boiled egg on top.

**Also:** Miso butter toast with a fried egg and shichimi togarashi is an excellent two-minute breakfast.

### On grilled or roasted vegetables

Miso butter works as a finishing glaze for almost any roasted vegetable. After roasting, toss vegetables in a knob of miso butter while still hot — the residual heat melts the butter and coats the vegetables in the miso's fermented depth.

**Particularly good with:** Roasted sweet potatoes, grilled eggplant, roasted broccolini, sautéed mushrooms, roasted cauliflower.

### On steak and grilled meat

Compound butters on steak are classical technique (*maître d'hôtel* butter dates to 19th-century French kitchens). Miso butter is a Japanese-Italian version of the same logic: the heat from the rested steak melts the butter, which pools with the meat juices and creates an impromptu sauce.

**Timing:** Place miso butter on a steak immediately after it comes off the heat, before resting. Let it melt during the 5-minute rest period.

## Miso butter in Italian cooking contexts

The flavor logic of miso butter maps directly to Italian compound butters. Italian cooking uses *burro aromatizzato* (flavored butter) extensively — herb butters, anchovy butter, truffle butter — all based on the same principle: add flavor to butter, use butter to finish a dish.

Miso butter is the Japanese version of anchovy butter: both add fermented, salt-forward umami to butter without dominating the dish. The key difference is that anchovy butter has a more assertive, fishier character; miso butter is sweeter and more neutral.

**Swap:** In any Italian recipe that uses anchovy butter — crostini, pasta with anchovy butter sauce, braised lamb — miso butter can substitute. The result will be less oceanic, more broadly savory.

**Combine:** Miso butter + Parmigiano is a legitimate addition to risotto as a mantecatura alternative. Whisk miso butter and finely grated Parmigiano into a finished risotto off the heat, the same way you'd do with regular butter. The glutamate stack (miso + Parmigiano) makes the risotto taste richer than either ingredient alone.

## Storage

Refrigerator: Up to 2 weeks in an airtight container or wrapped tightly in plastic wrap.

Freezer: Up to 3 months wrapped in plastic wrap, then foil. Slice off pieces as needed from the frozen log without defrosting the whole batch.

The miso's fermentation makes compound butter last longer than plain butter in the fridge — the salt and acidity act as mild preservatives.
