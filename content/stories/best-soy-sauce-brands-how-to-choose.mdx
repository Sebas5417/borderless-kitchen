**Short answer:** for everyday cooking, buy a **naturally brewed Japanese koikuchi (dark) soy sauce**. **Kikkoman** is the widely available standard, and **Yamasa** is a good alternative. Add a second bottle only for a specific cuisine: **Korean soup soy sauce** (*guk-ganjang*) for Korean soups, or **Chinese light and dark soy sauce** for Chinese stir-fries and braises.

---

## Brewed vs Blended: Check the Label First

**Naturally brewed** soy sauce is fermented from soybeans, wheat (usually), salt and water, often for months. **Non-brewed** or "chemically produced" soy sauce is made quickly by breaking down soy protein with acid (hydrolyzed vegetable protein), then adding color and flavor. It tastes harsher and flatter.

- Look for **"naturally brewed"** on the label.
- Be wary of ingredient lists with **hydrolyzed soy protein, caramel color and corn syrup** near the top.

## Which Bottle for Which Cooking

| You cook mostly… | Buy | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Japanese, or general use | Japanese koikuchi (dark) soy sauce | The all-purpose bottle |
| Japanese, light-colored dishes | Usukuchi (light-colored) soy sauce | Lighter color but **saltier** than koikuchi |
| Gluten-free | Tamari labeled gluten-free | Not all tamari is wheat-free; see [soy sauce vs tamari](/journal/soy-sauce-vs-tamari-difference) |
| Korean soups and namul | Korean soup soy sauce (*guk-ganjang*) | Lighter in color, saltier, more savory; see [Korean soy sauce types](/journal/korean-soy-sauce-types-guide) |
| Korean braises and marinades | Korean brewed soy sauce (*yangjo ganjang*) or Japanese koikuchi | Interchangeable for most home cooking |
| Chinese stir-fries | Chinese light soy sauce | Salty; the seasoning soy sauce |
| Chinese braises (red-cooking) | Chinese dark soy sauce | Thicker, less salty, mainly for color |

## The Bottles to Know

**Kikkoman (naturally brewed).** The standard Japanese koikuchi in most stores. Consistent and good for nearly everything. Kikkoman also sells a gluten-free tamari and a less-sodium version.

**Yamasa.** Another large Japanese brewer, well regarded by cooks, with a slightly different, rounder flavor than Kikkoman.

**Sempio.** A large Korean maker. Its naturally brewed (*yangjo*) soy sauce is the everyday Korean bottle for braises and marinades, and it also makes soup soy sauce. Check the label: some of its popular *jin ganjang* bottles contain corn syrup and sweeteners.

**Lee Kum Kee and Pearl River Bridge.** Widely sold Chinese brands with both light and dark soy sauces.

**Craft and barrel-aged shoyu.** Small-batch Japanese soy sauces, some aged in wooden barrels, cost several times more. They're best tasted raw: on sashimi, tofu or a finished dish.

## Storage

Once opened, soy sauce slowly oxidizes and darkens, and the flavor dulls. Keep it tightly capped, buy a size you'll finish in a few months, and refrigerate it if you use it slowly. Some Japanese brands sell soy sauce in double-walled bottles with a one-way valve that keeps air out after opening.
