The traditional Japanese breakfast, *asagohan* (朝ご飯), looks like a small version of a traditional Japanese meal: a bowl of rice, a bowl of soup, and a few side dishes, each in its own dish. It's what you get on the breakfast tray at a ryokan, and it's still eaten at home in many households, though not every morning and not by everyone.

It's simpler than it looks. Most of the components are either cooked by a timer, made the night before, or opened from a package.

---

## The Idea: Rice, Soup, Sides

The structure behind it is *ichiju-sansai* (一汁三菜), "one soup, three sides." It describes the basic shape of a home-style Japanese meal: rice at the centre, one soup, and side dishes chosen to vary in cooking method and flavour: typically a grilled main (fish or meat), something simmered, and something raw or dressed, such as a salad or *aemono*. Rice and pickles are assumed and not counted among the three.

Breakfast is the lightest version of it. In practice, many people eat *ichiju-issai*, one soup and one side, on a normal morning, and save the full spread for weekends or hotel stays. That's a reasonable target at home too.

## The Standard Components

### Steamed rice

Plain short-grain white rice, *gohan*. It's the meal; everything else is there to go with it. Japanese rice is a short-grain japonica variety that cooks up slightly sticky and holds together on chopsticks.

### Miso soup

Dashi with miso dissolved into it, and one or two fillings: tofu and wakame, daikon, onion, mushrooms, or clams. The one rule is that the miso shouldn't boil once it's in. Make it from scratch with [real dashi miso soup](/recipes/japanese-miso-soup-from-scratch), and if you're choosing a miso, read [the best miso brands and how to choose](/journal/best-miso-brands-how-to-choose).

### Grilled salted fish

*Yakizakana*, usually a single fillet or small whole fish. Salted salmon (*shiozake*) is the most familiar; mackerel (*saba*) and horse mackerel (*aji*), often split and dried, are common too. Many Japanese stoves have a small built-in fish grill for exactly this. At home, an oven grill, a toaster oven or a dry nonstick pan works. For a richer, slightly sweet version, try [shio koji salmon](/recipes/japanese-shio-koji-salmon); it marinates for a day, so start it the morning before.

### Tamagoyaki

A rolled omelette, cooked in thin layers in a rectangular pan and sliced. Versions vary by region and household: some are sweetened with sugar, others are made with dashi and only lightly seasoned. The dashi version, *dashimaki tamago*, is in our [dashimaki tamago recipe](/recipes/japanese-dashimaki-tamago-recipe). Tamagoyaki keeps well enough to make the night before and eat cold, as it is in bento; the sweeter style holds up best.

### Natto

Fermented soybeans, sold in small single-serving packs that usually come with a sachet of sauce and one of mustard. You stir them until they turn stringy, add the sauce and eat them over rice. The smell and texture divide people, including in Japan. More, including how to eat it if you're new to it, is in [what is natto](/journal/what-is-natto-japanese-fermented-soybeans).

### Pickles and nori

A few slices of *tsukemono*, Japanese pickles, such as pickled daikon (*takuan*), cucumber or a salted pickled plum (*umeboshi*). And a few small sheets of seasoned or toasted nori, for wrapping a bite of rice.

## How People Actually Eat Breakfast

The full tray is the ideal, not the daily norm. A few common alternatives:

**Tamago kake gohan.** A raw egg cracked over hot rice, seasoned with soy sauce and stirred. It takes a minute and it's a breakfast staple. Our [TKG guide](/journal/tamago-kake-gohan-recipe-japanese-raw-egg-rice) covers it in full.

**A note on raw egg:** in Japan, eggs are washed, graded and kept cold under strict hygiene rules, and the best-before date printed on the pack marks how long they can be eaten raw; after that date, cook them. Elsewhere, use pasteurized eggs for TKG. Raw egg is not advised for pregnant people, young children, the elderly, or anyone immunocompromised.

**Toast.** Bread breakfasts are common in Japan, especially thick-sliced white bread, toasted and buttered, with coffee. In many households it depends on the day or the person: toast when time is short, rice when it isn't.

**Okayu.** Soft rice porridge, the standard food when someone is ill or recovering, usually with an umeboshi on top. It's also eaten when your stomach wants something gentle. See our [okayu guide](/journal/okayu-japanese-rice-porridge-guide).

**Leftovers.** Last night's simmered dish, a few onigiri, rice with furikake. Breakfast is often whatever is already in the fridge.

## A 20-Minute Weekday Plan

The trick is to do the slow parts in advance. Nothing here is difficult; it just needs to happen in the right order.

**The night before (about 15 minutes):**
- **Rice.** Rinse the rice, put it in the rice cooker with water, and set the timer so it's ready when you get up. Most Japanese-style microcomputer rice cookers have a delay (preset) timer, though basic one-switch cookers don't; models are compared in our [rice cooker guide](/journal/best-japanese-rice-cooker-for-home-cooks).
- **Dashi.** Put kombu and bonito flakes, or just kombu, in a jar of cold water and refrigerate overnight. Strain in the morning. Cold-brewed dashi keeps 2 to 3 days in the fridge, so make enough for a few mornings. Methods are in our [complete dashi guide](/journal/japanese-dashi-complete-guide).
- **Salmon.** Sprinkle salmon fillets with salt on both sides and refrigerate, uncovered or loosely covered. The salt seasons the fish and draws out some moisture, so it grills firmer. Pat dry before cooking.
- **Optional:** make a tamagoyaki and refrigerate it whole.

**In the morning (about 20 minutes):**
1. **Minutes 0–2:** Turn on the grill or heat a pan. Put the salmon on.
2. **Minutes 2–8:** Bring the dashi to a simmer with your soup fillings. Tofu and rehydrated wakame need only a minute or two; sliced daikon or onion needs a few minutes more.
3. **Minutes 8–12:** Turn the salmon. If you're making tamagoyaki fresh, cook it now; otherwise slice the one from last night.
4. **Minutes 12–15:** Take the soup off the heat and dissolve the miso into it. Don't let it boil again.
5. **Minutes 15–20:** Check the salmon is cooked through. Serve rice, soup and fish, and add natto, pickles and nori straight from the fridge.

Grilling times depend on thickness and heat, so watch the fish rather than the clock. If you skip the salmon on busy days, rice, miso soup, natto and pickles is still a complete Japanese breakfast, and it takes about five minutes once the rice and dashi are ready.
