Jjim (찜) is the Korean culinary category for dishes that are steamed or braised — cooked with liquid in a covered vessel, the cooking liquid remaining as a sauce rather than reducing to a glaze. If jorim is the "reduce until it coats" technique, jjim is the "braise until tender in substantial sauce" technique.

The most famous jjim dish is galbi jjim (갈비찜) — braised beef short ribs with soy, sugar, garlic, ginger, and vegetables. But jjim encompasses a wide range of proteins and preparations.

## Galbi Jjim (갈비찜) — The Centerpiece

Galbi jjim is Korea's special-occasion braised short rib dish — the dish that appears at Chuseok, New Year, birthdays, and celebrations. It is distinct from everyday Korean cooking in both time investment and ingredient quality.

**What makes it distinctive:**
- Cross-cut short ribs with bone (flanken cut) or English-cut short ribs — the bone adds depth
- Long marinade in soy, pear or Asian pear (the fruit enzymes tenderize the meat), garlic, ginger, sugar
- Two-hour braise with chestnuts, jujube dates (대추), dried shiitake mushrooms, and carrot
- The sauce: rich, deep soy-brown, with natural sweetness from the fruit marinade and root vegetables

**Galbi jjim formula:**
- Blanch ribs first (discard water)
- Marinade: 5 tbsp soy, 3 tbsp sugar, 1 tbsp sesame oil, 1 Asian pear (grated), 6 cloves garlic, 1 tsp ginger
- Marinate minimum 2 hours, preferably overnight
- Braise with 1 cup water, chestnuts, jujube, mushrooms, carrot 1.5-2 hours covered
- Finish with sesame seeds and scallion

## Saengson Jjim (생선찜) — Steamed Fish

Whole fish or fish sections steamed over a bed of aromatics — typically ginger slices, scallion, and sometimes tofu. The steam from the aromatics beneath the fish cooks it while the aromatic steam flavors it.

The finished fish is dressed with a sauce poured over: soy, sesame oil, gochugaru, garlic, and julienned scallion. Less assertive than jorim-style braised fish; cleaner in flavor.

## Aehobak Jjim (애호박찜) — Steamed Zucchini

The simplest jjim: Korean green zucchini (애호박, *aehobak*) cut into rounds, lightly salted, steamed or briefly boiled, then dressed with a sauce of soy sauce, gochugaru, sesame oil, garlic, and sesame seeds.

This is a common quick banchan — fast to prepare, a good showcase of the fresh zucchini flavor under the seasoning.

## Agui Jjim (아귀찜) — Braised Monkfish

One of Korea's most intensely flavored dishes. Monkfish (aghwi) is braised in a very spicy sauce of gochugaru, gochujang, soy, garlic, and ginger with bean sprouts and green onion. The dish is bright red, assertively spicy, and complex — the monkfish's gelatinous flesh absorbs the sauce deeply during braising.

Agui jjim is a specialty dish, not an everyday preparation — but its distinctive flavor profile (spicy, gelatinous, intensely savory) makes it a memorable representation of Korean braising at its most powerful.

## Jjim vs. Jorim — The Key Distinction

The simplest distinction:
- **Jorim:** The sauce reduces to a glaze that coats the ingredient. Most of the liquid is cooked away.
- **Jjim:** Braising liquid remains as substantial sauce in the finished dish. The cooking liquid is part of the final dish, not cooked away.

In practice, the distinction is one of proportion and intention: if you want a dish with sauce to eat over rice, you're making jjim. If you want a dish with intensely glazed, coated pieces with minimal liquid, you're making jorim.

---

Jjim dishes represent Korean braising at its most impressive — the long-cooked galbi jjim for celebrations, the quick aehobak jjim for weeknight banchan, the powerful agui jjim for special occasions. The category's range is as wide as the technique is consistent: low and slow, covered, in liquid.
