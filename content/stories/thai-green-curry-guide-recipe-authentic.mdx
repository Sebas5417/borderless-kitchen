Thai green curry (gaeng keow wan, literally "sweet green curry") is named for its color — the fresh green chilies in the curry paste give it the pale jade-green color that distinguishes it from red curry (dried red chilies) and yellow curry (turmeric-heavy).

The "sweet" in the name refers to the color sweet (wan means sweet in the sense of the color being lighter than red), not the flavor — authentic Thai green curry is actually hotter than red curry because fresh green chilies have more immediate heat than dried red chilies.

## The Paste

Thai curry paste is the foundational element. The difference between a curry made from fresh paste and one made from a jar is significant — not because jar paste is bad (Maesri and Mae Ploy make excellent commercial pastes) but because fresh paste has a vibrancy and aromatics that fade quickly after grinding.

**Components of green curry paste:**

**Fresh green chilies:** The primary flavor and heat source. Thai bird's eye green chilies (prik kee nu suan) are the authentic choice — extremely hot, with a clean, bright heat. Long green chilies add color with less heat. The quantity determines the heat level.

**Lemongrass:** The tender inner part only (peel back the outer layers), finely chopped. This is the aromatic backbone of Thai curries.

**Galangal:** Related to ginger but distinct — more piney, earthy, less spicy. Do not substitute fresh ginger; it produces a different flavor. Dried galangal powder is a very poor substitute.

**Kaffir lime zest:** The aromatic skin of the kaffir lime (also called makrut lime). The leaves are used in the curry itself; the zest goes into the paste. Very aromatic, floral-citrus.

**Shallots and garlic:** The base aromatics, included in quantity.

**Coriander root:** The root of the coriander plant — more intensely flavored than leaves. Increasingly difficult to find outside Asian grocers. The base of the coriander bunch (stems and some root) substitutes adequately.

**Cumin seeds and coriander seeds:** Toasted and ground — the "warm" spice element.

**Shrimp paste (kapi):** Fermented shrimp paste provides umami depth that no other ingredient replicates. A small amount goes a long way.

**White pepper:** For additional heat and depth.

Everything is pounded in a mortar (traditional) or blended (modern). The mortar produces a coarser paste with better oil release from the aromatics; a blender requires more liquid (water or coconut cream) but is faster.

## The Coconut Milk Technique

The traditional Thai technique for cooking a curry does not start with oil — it starts with the thick cream from the top of a can of full-fat coconut milk, called "cracking" the coconut cream.

Open the can without shaking; skim the thick cream from the top. Fry this cream in a dry wok over medium-high heat until it separates and the solids begin to toast in the oil that releases. Add the curry paste and fry it in the coconut oil until fragrant — 2–3 minutes. Only then add the rest of the coconut milk and stock.

This step produces a richer, more aromatic curry than adding all the coconut milk at once. It is the step most commonly omitted in Western recipes.

## Seasoning

Thai curries are seasoned with fish sauce, not salt. Fish sauce provides saltiness and umami. Palm sugar provides sweetness to balance. The balance of salty, sweet, creamy, and hot is the entire point — the curry should taste harmonized, not any single note dominant.

Kaffir lime leaves are added during cooking — torn in half to release the aromatic oils.

Thai basil (different from Italian basil — more anise-flavored, darker leaves, purple stems) is added at the end, off heat.

**Recipe: Thai Green Curry (Serves 4)**

**Quick green curry paste (or use store-bought Maesri/Mae Ploy):**
- 10 fresh green bird's eye chilies (adjust to heat preference)
- 3 stalks lemongrass (inner tender parts), roughly chopped
- 4cm piece galangal, peeled and sliced
- Zest of 1 kaffir lime (or substitute 3 kaffir lime leaves)
- 4 shallots, peeled
- 4 cloves garlic
- 1 teaspoon shrimp paste
- 1 teaspoon cumin seeds (toasted)
- 1 tablespoon coriander seeds (toasted)
- Handful coriander root and stems
- 1/2 teaspoon white pepper
Blend with 2–3 tablespoons water to a smooth paste.

**Curry:**
- 400ml can full-fat coconut milk (do not shake)
- 200ml coconut milk or chicken stock
- 4 tablespoons green curry paste (store-bought or above)
- 500g chicken thigh, sliced (or tofu for vegetarian)
- 4 Thai eggplants, quartered (or courgette)
- 4 kaffir lime leaves, torn
- 2 tablespoons fish sauce
- 1 tablespoon palm sugar or brown sugar
- Handful Thai basil
- 1–2 long red or green chilies, sliced (for garnish)

**Method:**

1. Open coconut milk without shaking. Spoon thick cream from top (about half the can) into a wok. Heat over medium-high until cream bubbles and oil begins to separate, 3–4 minutes.

2. Add curry paste. Fry, stirring constantly, 2–3 minutes until fragrant and darkened slightly.

3. Add chicken; stir to coat in paste. Cook 2 minutes.

4. Add remaining coconut milk and stock. Add kaffir lime leaves.

5. Bring to a simmer; cook 10 minutes. Add eggplant; cook 5 more minutes.

6. Season with fish sauce and palm sugar. Taste — it should be salty, sweet, savory, and fragrant. Adjust.

7. Remove from heat; add Thai basil, stir through.

8. Serve over jasmine rice; garnish with sliced chili.
