Most cooks treat dried herbs as a compromise — what you reach for when you don't have fresh. This is wrong, and it causes bad cooking in both directions: overuse of dried herbs where fresh would work better, and fresh herbs added too early where dried ones would have performed better. The two forms are not interchangeable. They are chemically different, and they behave differently under heat.

Understanding why transforms the question from "which is better?" to "which does this job?"

---

## The chemistry of drying

An herb's flavor comes from volatile aromatic compounds — terpenes, terpenoids, phenols, and other organic molecules produced by the plant. "Volatile" means they evaporate easily at or below room temperature, which is exactly why fresh herbs have fragrance: you can smell them without heating them because their aroma compounds are constantly escaping into the air around them.

Drying an herb removes roughly 80–90% of its water weight. This concentrates the aromatic compounds that survive the drying process — by weight, dried herbs are more intensely flavored than fresh, which is why recipes call for roughly one third the quantity of dried to fresh. But not all aromatic compounds survive drying. Highly volatile top-note aromatics — the fresh, bright, herbaceous compounds that make fresh herbs smell green and sharp — are largely destroyed or dissipated during drying. What remains after drying are the more stable, fat-soluble aromatic compounds: the earthy, savory, rounded notes.

The result is that a dried herb and its fresh equivalent taste different, not just stronger or weaker. Dried basil doesn't taste like concentrated fresh basil. It tastes like a different expression of the plant — earthier, more medicinal, less bright.

---

## What this means for timing

Because volatile aromatic compounds are what make fresh herbs bright, fresh herbs are most effective when those compounds are preserved — which means adding them at the end of cooking, or off heat entirely. Heat drives off volatile aromatics in the same way that drying does. Fresh basil added to a sauce at the start and cooked for 20 minutes loses almost all of what makes it worth using. Added as a garnish after the sauce is plated, it retains everything.

Dried herbs behave the opposite. Their volatile top-note aromatics are already gone; what they have left are the stable aromatic compounds that require heat and fat to fully express. Dried herbs added to a sauce in the last minute are largely wasted — they haven't had time or heat to bloom. Dried herbs added at the start, especially when briefly cooked in fat (an Italian soffritto, a Japanese kombu dashi base, any fat-first sauté), release their stable aromatics fully. Blooming dried herbs in fat before adding liquid is not optional — it's how you extract what's in them.

**The practical rule:**

- Dried herbs: add early, especially in fat
- Fresh herbs: add at the end, or as a garnish

## Japanese applications

Japanese cuisine is precise about this distinction in practice, even if it doesn't articulate it in the Western herb vocabulary.

**Shiso (perilla) is always used fresh.** Shiso is among the most volatile-aromatic herbs in any cuisine — its flavor is so dependent on intact, undamaged aroma compounds that it cannot be used dried in any meaningful sense. Even cutting it releases aromatic compounds that begin to dissipate immediately. Chiffonaded shiso goes on top of cold soba, into tataki, over rice bowls — always at the last moment, never cooked. The Japanese know this intuitively because the plant tells you: a bruised shiso leaf blackens within minutes, signaling that its chemistry is already degrading.

**Togarashi, by contrast, is a dried spice compound.** Seven-spice togarashi (shichimi togarashi) blends dried chili, dried citrus peel, dried sesame, dried seaweed, dried ginger, and hemp seeds. Everything in it is heat-stable — designed to be added to hot ramen broth or sprinkled on yakitori. The drying concentrates and stabilizes these compounds, making them exactly right for applications where fresh equivalents would wilt and lose their potency instantly.

**Sansho (Japanese pepper)** is a case where both forms exist and serve different purposes. Dried and ground sansho is used as a condiment, providing the numbing, citrus-electric quality that functions like pepper over finished dishes. Fresh kinome (young sansho leaves) are used as garnish — their volatile aromatics are so fragile they're pressed between the palms before use to release them, and then placed immediately.

## Italian applications

Italian cuisine has arrived at the same underlying logic through practice.

**Dried oregano in tomato sauce.** Fresh oregano is pungent when raw but loses its complexity quickly when cooked — it flattens. Dried oregano, added early to tomato sauce and cooked for 20+ minutes, develops a deep, savory, vaguely Mediterranean quality that is fundamental to Italian-American tomato sauce. The longer cook time and the fat in the oil bloom the stable aromatics in the dried herb. Fresh oregano would largely disappear in the same application.

**Fresh basil on pizza.** Pizza Margherita finishes with fresh basil leaves pressed onto the pizza the moment it exits the oven. Cooking basil in the oven destroys its volatile aromatics completely — you're left with blackened, bitter leaves that contribute nothing. Fresh basil added at exit is warm from the pizza's residual heat, which slightly blooms its aroma without destroying it. The timing is precise: too soon (in the oven) and it's ruined; too late (after cooling) and it's just a garnish that doesn't integrate.

**Dried rosemary vs fresh rosemary** is a useful case study. Fresh rosemary is powerful and resinous — one or two sprigs in a roasting pan with potatoes creates a fragrant, rounded herb note. Dried rosemary, by contrast, can be unpleasant unless it has had extended time in fat or liquid to soften its needle-like texture. The difference here isn't just volatile vs stable aromatics — it's also texture. Fresh rosemary's needles soften during cooking; dried rosemary's needles stay sharp and woodsy unless given substantial time. For short applications, fresh rosemary is almost always preferable. For long braises, dried integrates fully.

## A practical guide

**Always buy dried:**
Oregano, thyme, bay leaf, marjoram, dried chili varieties, spice blends. These herbs have stable aromatic compounds that survive drying and benefit from heat development. Fresh versions are pleasant but rarely superior in cooked applications.

**Always buy fresh:**
Basil, shiso, cilantro, flat-leaf parsley, tarragon, chervil. These herbs are almost entirely volatile aromatics. Dried versions of these herbs exist but are pale approximations that contribute almost nothing in a cooked application — and nothing at all as a finish.

**Buy both, use situationally:**
Rosemary (fresh for short roasting applications, dried for long braises), mint (fresh for garnish and cooling applications, dried for teas and some long-cooked North African or Middle Eastern preparations).

**Handling fresh herbs:** wash and dry them, then store wrapped loosely in a slightly damp paper towel in a sealed bag or container. Most fresh herbs last 5–7 days this way. Basil is an exception — it is cold-sensitive and blackens at refrigerator temperatures. Store basil stem-down in a small jar of room-temperature water on the counter like a flower.

**Reviving dried herbs:** dried herbs that have been sitting for over a year have largely lost their aromatic compounds. Rub a small amount between your fingers and smell — if there's little to no fragrance, discard them. They will add bulk but not flavor.

---

The question was never fresh versus dried. The question is always what the herb is doing and when it's doing it. Once you understand that dried herbs need time and fat to give what they have, and fresh herbs need protection from both, the answer for any given dish becomes obvious.
