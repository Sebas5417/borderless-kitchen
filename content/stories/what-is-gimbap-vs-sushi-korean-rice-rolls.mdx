Gimbap (김밥) is called "Korean sushi" often enough that the comparison has become unavoidable. It's rice rolled in seaweed (*gim*), cut into rounds, and eaten as a snack, lunch, or picnic food. Visually, it resembles maki sushi.

But calling gimbap Korean sushi is like calling a burrito a Mexican sandwich — technically understandable, but missing the point entirely. The ingredients are different, the technique is different, the flavor is different, and the cultural context is completely its own.

## The Name

*Gim* (김) is roasted seaweed — specifically, dried and toasted seaweed sheets. The Korean word for roasted seaweed is gim, not nori (which is the Japanese term). While they're the same genus of seaweed (*Porphyra*), Korean gim is often roasted with sesame oil and salt before packaging, giving it a richer, more savory flavor than plain dried Japanese nori.

*Bap* (밥) is cooked rice. So gimbap literally means "seaweed rice."

## How It Differs from Sushi

**The rice:** Sushi rice (*shari*) is seasoned with rice vinegar, sugar, and salt — it has a distinctive sour-sweet flavor. Gimbap rice is seasoned with sesame oil and salt — rich, savory, no acid. The flavor difference is immediately apparent.

**The fillings:** Gimbap fillings are almost always cooked. The traditional lineup: *danmuji* (yellow pickled daikon), spinach (blanched and seasoned with sesame oil and garlic), *eomuk* (fish cake), *ham* or *spam*, egg strips (*gyeran* — a thin omelette cut into strips), crab stick or real crab, carrots, and sometimes beef or tuna.

Raw fish does not appear in traditional gimbap — the dish is designed for portability and safety at room temperature, which means cooked or preserved ingredients throughout.

**The technique:** Sushi is assembled with the rice on the inside, nori on the outside (for maki), and requires a specific rolling technique that compresses the rice against the fillings. Gimbap uses a bamboo rolling mat (*gimbap mat*) to roll the seaweed-rice-fillings into a tight cylinder. The rolling is easier than sushi; the key is compression.

**The flavor profile:** Where sushi is characterized by the vinegar note of the rice and the varying flavors of raw fish, gimbap has a consistent savory richness from the sesame oil rice, with specific fillings contributing their individual flavors. It tastes Korean — sesame, savory, with the crunch of pickled daikon and the richness of egg.

**Cultural context:** Gimbap is everyday food — picnic food, packed lunch, street food. It's sold at every Korean convenience store, every traditional market, and made at home for outings. Sushi in Japan has a similar everyday presence (particularly onigiri and convenience store sushi), but omakase sushi occupies a fine-dining space that gimbap does not.

## The Major Gimbap Varieties

**Classic gimbap:** The standard combination — ham, egg, spinach, danmuji, carrot, crab stick. This is the baseline from which all variations depart.

**Chamchi gimbap (참치 김밥):** Tuna (canned, mixed with mayonnaise) as the protein. One of the most popular varieties — the Kewpie mayo-dressed tuna is deeply satisfying.

**Bulgogi gimbap:** Marinated beef bulgogi as the filling. A heartier version.

**Kimchi gimbap:** Aged kimchi as a filling, often with pork. The sourness and heat of the kimchi against the sesame rice is a good combination.

**Mayak gimbap (마약 김밥):** "Drug gimbap" — very small rolls (finger-sized), packed with just rice, sesame, and pickled daikon, served with a yellow mustard-soy dipping sauce. The name refers to the addictive quality — once you start, you can't stop. A Gwangjang Market specialty.

**Nude gimbap (누드 김밥):** Inside-out gimbap — rice on the outside, gim on the inside. Similar to a California roll in construction, different in content.

**Samgak gimbap (삼각 김밥):** Triangle-shaped gimbap, sold at Korean convenience stores. The triangular shape is achieved by a specific plastic wrapping technique — the wrapper keeps the gim crisp until you're ready to eat, then peels away in a sequence similar to Japanese onigiri.

## How to Make Gimbap at Home

**What you need:**
- 2 cups short-grain rice, cooked
- 2 tablespoons sesame oil
- 1 teaspoon salt
- Roasted gim sheets (Korean-style, sesame oil and salt)
- Fillings of your choice

**Rice preparation:** Season hot cooked rice with sesame oil and salt while still warm. Mix gently — don't mash. Let cool to room temperature before rolling.

**Rolling technique:**
1. Place a gim sheet on a bamboo rolling mat, shiny side down.
2. Spread a thin, even layer of rice over about 2/3 of the gim (leaving the far edge bare for sealing).
3. Arrange fillings in a horizontal line across the center of the rice.
4. Roll the mat away from you, using the mat to compress the gimbap firmly. Apply even pressure throughout.
5. Seal the bare gim edge with a light brushing of water or a grain of rice.
6. Cut into 1cm rounds with a sharp, slightly wet knife.

**The most common mistake:** Too much rice or filling. Gimbap should be tight and compact — if it's bulging, reduce the rice or filling next time.

## Eating Gimbap

Gimbap is eaten at room temperature — which makes it ideal for packing. Unlike sushi (which should be eaten immediately, at serving temperature), gimbap holds well for 2-3 hours at room temperature. This is by design: it's a portable food, made for carrying.

At a traditional Korean market, gimbap vendors sell individual pieces for ₩300-500 ($0.20-0.40 USD) — some of the most affordable good food anywhere.

---

Gimbap and sushi share seaweed and rice. Beyond that, they're separate traditions that reflect different food cultures. Gimbap reflects Korea's love of communal, portable, everyday food — the kind of thing packed into a picnic basket for a day at the park, bought from a market vendor during a lunch break, or made at home with whatever's in the refrigerator. It doesn't aspire to be sushi any more than sushi aspires to be gimbap. Both are excellent at what they are.
