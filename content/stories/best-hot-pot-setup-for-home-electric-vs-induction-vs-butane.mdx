Hot pot is the easiest dinner party there is. Everyone cooks their own food in a simmering pot in the middle of the table. Japanese shabu-shabu and sukiyaki, Chinese huoguo, Korean jeongol and budae jjigae: same idea, different broth or sauce. The only hard part is the equipment. You need something that keeps a pot simmering on the table for two hours, and a pot that suits it.

Here's how to choose, starting with the heat source, because it decides which pots you can use.

---

## Step 1: Choose the Heat Source

### Option A: Electric hot pot (simplest)

→ [Shop electric hot pots on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=electric+hot+pot+divided+5+quart&tag=borderlesskitchen-20)

An all-in-one pot with a built-in heating element and a temperature dial. You plug it in, fill it and it simmers. Many come with a divider for two broths, the "yin-yang" pot (yuanyang guo in Chinese): a spicy one and a mild one. **Best for:** anyone who wants one purchase and no extra pieces. **Downside:** you can only use that one pot, and cheaper models can be slow to come back to a boil after a load of cold ingredients goes in.

### Option B: Induction burner + a pot you choose

→ [Shop portable induction cooktops on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=portable+induction+cooktop+1800w&tag=borderlesskitchen-20)

A portable induction plate heats fast, holds a steady simmer and has no open flame, and it's common at hot pot restaurants. On a standard US outlet, most top out at about 1,800 watts, so plug it into an outlet without other heavy loads or it may trip the breaker. **The catch:** induction only works with magnetic pots (cast iron, enamelled iron, and stainless steel with a magnetic base; many 18/10 stainless pots aren't magnetic). If a magnet sticks to the bottom of a pot, it works. Clay pots and aluminium don't, unless they are sold as induction-compatible ("IH" on Japanese labels). **Best for:** people who want to choose their own pot and also use the burner for other cooking.

### Option C: Portable butane stove (most traditional)

→ [Shop portable butane stoves on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=portable+butane+stove+iwatani&tag=borderlesskitchen-20)

The tabletop gas burner used in Japanese and Korean homes and restaurants, often from Iwatani. Real flame works with any pot, including traditional clay donabe, and there's no cord across the table. **Use it safely:**
- Use it in a ventilated room.
- Use only the canister brand the manufacturer specifies.
- Never use a pot or griddle so large that it covers the canister compartment. Trapped heat can overheat the canister.

Our [Korean BBQ grill pan guide](/journal/best-korean-bbq-grill-pan-for-home) covers the same stoves for tabletop grilling. **Best for:** donabe owners and anyone who wants the restaurant feel.

## Step 2: Choose the Pot

### Divided (yin-yang) pot

→ [Shop divided hot pots on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=divided+hot+pot+yin+yang+induction&tag=borderlesskitchen-20)

A wide stainless steel pot split down the middle, so a spicy Sichuan-style broth and a mild one can share the table. Look for induction compatibility if you chose Option B. This is the pot for mixed groups where not everyone wants the heat.

### Shabu-shabu pot

→ [Shop shabu-shabu pots on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=shabu+shabu+pot&tag=borderlesskitchen-20)

Wide and fairly shallow, so the thin slices of meat can be swished through the broth ("shabu-shabu" is the swishing sound). Some have a chimney in the centre, a design that comes from charcoal-heated pots. For gas or induction it's mostly about looks, though it does stop the broth sloshing in a crowded pot.

### Donabe (clay pot)

→ [Shop donabe clay pots on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=donabe+japanese+clay+pot&tag=borderlesskitchen-20)

The Japanese clay pot for nabe and oden. It holds heat beautifully and goes from stove to table. It needs a gas flame unless it's labelled induction-compatible, and most need seasoning before first use. How to choose one and season it is in our [donabe guide](/journal/best-donabe-japanese-clay-pot-how-to-choose).

## Step 3: The Accessories That Matter

### Mesh strainer ladles

→ [Shop hot pot strainer ladles on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=hot+pot+mesh+strainer+ladle+set&tag=borderlesskitchen-20)

Small individual mesh scoops so everyone can fish out tofu, fish balls and noodles without chasing them with chopsticks. They're cheap, and a set of six makes a hot pot night work.

### Broth bases and dipping sauces

→ [Shop hot pot soup bases on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=hot+pot+soup+base+haidilao+little+sheep&tag=borderlesskitchen-20) · [Shop ponzu on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=ponzu+sauce+mizkan&tag=borderlesskitchen-20) · [Shop goma (sesame) sauce on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=goma+shabu+sesame+sauce&tag=borderlesskitchen-20)

Packaged soup bases from hot pot chains like Haidilao and Little Sheep make a good broth in minutes. For Japanese shabu-shabu, the broth is just kombu and water, and the flavour comes from two dipping sauces: citrusy ponzu and creamy sesame (goma). For a scratch broth, our guide to [the types of Japanese nabe](/journal/japanese-nabe-hotpot-types) covers the classics.

### Long chopsticks or tongs for raw meat

Keep a separate pair of long cooking chopsticks or tongs for raw meat and seafood, so nobody eats with the utensils that touched it raw. Thin slices cook in seconds, but the habit matters at a shared pot.

## Which Setup Should You Buy?

- **One purchase, no fuss:** an electric divided hot pot.
- **Most versatile:** an induction burner plus a divided stainless pot. The burner is useful year-round.
- **Most traditional:** a butane stove plus a donabe or shabu-shabu pot.
- **Already own a donabe?** Get the butane stove. Most donabe won't work on induction.

## What to Skip

- **Non-stick electric hot pots with very thin walls.** They lose temperature every time cold food goes in, and the coating scratches under metal ladles.
- **Huge pots on small burners.** A pot much wider than the burner simmers unevenly, and on a butane stove an oversized pot is a safety risk.
- **Charcoal hot pots indoors.** Burning charcoal releases carbon monoxide, even with windows open. Leave charcoal for outdoor use only.
