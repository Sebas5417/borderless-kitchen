Korean street food is cheap, quick and built around a handful of dishes you'll see over and over: rice cakes in red sauce, fish cake skewers in broth, blood sausage, fried snacks, and, in winter, sweet filled pancakes and fish-shaped pastries. Some of it is sold from daytime carts and market stalls. Some of it comes with a plastic stool, a bottle of soju and a tent.

That tent is the pojangmacha.

---

## What Is a Pojangmacha?

*Pojangmacha* (포장마차) translates roughly as "covered wagon": *pojang* is the cloth cover, *macha* a (horse-drawn) carriage. In practice it means a street stall wrapped in a tarp or tent, often orange or blue, with a few tables and stools inside. Many open in the evening and run late. Koreans often shorten the word to *pocha*.

A pojangmacha is part snack stall, part bar. People come for soju and *anju*, the food eaten with drinks: grilled skewers, stir-fried squid, odeng soup, fried chicken gizzards, whatever the owner cooks. How anju works is in our [guide to Korean drinking culture](/journal/korean-drinking-culture-anju-food-guide), and how to pour and drink the soju is in our [soju guide](/journal/korean-soju-guide-how-to-drink-soju).

The classic tented street stall has become less common. Cities including Seoul have cleared, licensed or regulated street vendors over the years, and many stalls have closed or moved indoors. The name lives on: plenty of indoor bars call themselves *pocha* and copy the look, plastic stools and all. What you find depends on the neighborhood and the year.

## The Core Street Foods

### Tteokbokki

The center of Korean street food. Chewy cylinder rice cakes simmered with fish cake in a sweet, spicy gochujang sauce, kept bubbling in a wide, shallow pan so it's ready to scoop. Most stalls also sell it alongside sundae and twigim, a trio common enough to have its own nickname, *tteok-twi-sun* (떡튀순). The dish's story, from the royal court's soy-sauce version to the gochujang one, is in our [tteokbokki history and culture guide](/journal/tteokbokki-history-culture-guide).

### Eomuk (odeng) skewers

Sheets of fish cake folded onto long skewers and kept warm in a pot of broth, often made with radish and kelp. *Eomuk* is the Korean word; *odeng*, from Japanese, is what many people still say. You pick skewers from the pot and eat standing up. At many stalls the broth is free while you eat: you ladle it into a paper cup yourself, or the vendor does. Practice varies, so watch what others do.

### Sundae

Korean blood sausage: pig intestine stuffed with sweet potato glass noodles (*dangmyeon*) and pork blood, sometimes with glutinous rice, barley or vegetables. It's steamed, sliced and often served with pieces of liver and lung. The dip depends on the region, from salt mixed with pepper to a seasoned soybean paste. Many people dunk it in the tteokbokki sauce instead.

### Twigim

Battered and deep-fried snacks, the Korean cousin of tempura: sweet potato, squid, vegetables, boiled eggs, and *gimmari*, seaweed rolls stuffed with glass noodles. Twigim is often ordered on the side and cut up into the tteokbokki sauce.

### Gimbap

Rice and fillings rolled in seaweed and sliced, sold all day from street stalls and small shops as fast food. The rice is seasoned with sesame oil, not vinegar, which is one of the ways it differs from sushi; more in our [kimbap guide](/journal/korean-kimbap-guide).

### Dakkochi and tornado potato

*Dakkochi* are chicken skewers, often alternated with pieces of scallion, grilled and brushed with a sweet soy or spicy glaze. The *tornado potato* (*hoeori gamja*) is a whole potato spiral-cut onto a long stick, fried and dusted with seasoning powder; it's a newer arrival, popular at festivals and tourist streets.

## Winter Snacks: Hotteok, Bungeoppang and Gyeran-ppang

Some street foods follow the season. When the weather turns cold, these carts appear.

- **Hotteok** (호떡): a yeasted dough pancake filled with brown sugar, cinnamon and often chopped nuts or seeds, pressed flat on an oiled griddle until the sugar melts into syrup. Bite carefully; the filling is very hot.
- **Bungeoppang** (붕어빵): a fish-shaped pastry baked in an iron mold, filled with sweet red bean paste. Custard fillings are common now too. It's similar to Japanese taiyaki; *bungeo* is the word for crucian carp.
- **Gyeran-ppang** (계란빵): "egg bread," a small, slightly sweet oblong bun baked with a whole egg on top.

Hotteok and bungeoppang are both thought of as winter food, and bungeoppang carts in particular tend to disappear in the warm months. Some sellers keep hotteok going year-round, especially in tourist areas.

## Where to Eat It: Myeongdong and Gwangjang Market

In Seoul, two places come up again and again.

**Myeongdong**, the shopping district, fills with food stalls in the evening along its main streets. The stalls lean toward visitors, with lobster tails, grilled cheese and newer novelty snacks next to the classics, and prices tend to be higher than elsewhere.

**Gwangjang Market**, one of the city's oldest markets, is better known for sit-down stalls in its covered alleys. Two foods are famous there: *bindaetteok*, thick mung bean pancakes fried in plenty of oil until crisp at the edges, and *mayak gimbap*, small, thin rolls of rice and a few vegetables served with a mustard and soy dipping sauce. (*Mayak* means "narcotic," a joke about how hard it is to stop eating them.) Our [Gwangjang Market food guide](/journal/gwangjang-market-seoul-food-guide) walks through the stalls, and bindaetteok's relatives are in our [guide to Korean jeon](/journal/korean-jeon-guide-all-types).

## How to Order and Eat

- **Point.** Most stalls show the food in front of you. Pointing and holding up fingers for the number works fine.
- **Odeng is counted by skewer.** At many stalls you take skewers yourself and count the sticks when you pay.
- **Pay cash or card.** Many stalls take cards now, but not all, especially small carts. Carrying small bills is still a good idea.
- **Eat where you stand.** Street snacks are usually eaten at the stall or on a stool, not carried far. Hand back the skewers and cups.
- **At a pojangmacha,** you sit, order a few dishes to share and drinks for the table. It's a place to stay a while.

## Make It at Home

Tteokbokki is the easiest street food to bring home, and it's the one most people miss. Start with our [classic tteokbokki recipe](/recipes/korean-tteokbokki-classic-recipe): rice cakes, fish cakes, gochujang and a little sugar, done in about twenty minutes. Add fish cake skewers to a pot of anchovy broth on the side for odeng soup.

For a picnic version of the street stall, roll [gimbap](/recipes/gimbap-korean-rice-rolls), or cut thin rolls with fewer fillings and make a mustard-soy dip for a mayak gimbap imitation. For something closer to the pojangmacha table, fry a [pajeon](/recipes/korean-pajeon-scallion-pancake-recipe) and open a bottle of soju.
