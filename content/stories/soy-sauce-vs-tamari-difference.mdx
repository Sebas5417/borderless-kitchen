**Short answer:** regular soy sauce is brewed from soybeans and wheat. Tamari is made mostly or entirely from soybeans, with little or no wheat. That makes tamari darker, thicker and rounder in flavor, and many tamari bottles are gluten-free. Not all of them are, so check the label.

---

## Side by Side

| | Regular soy sauce (koikuchi) | Tamari |
|---|---|---|
| Made from | Soybeans and wheat, roughly equal | Mostly or all soybeans |
| Gluten | Contains wheat | Often gluten-free; check for a certified label |
| Color and body | Reddish-brown, thin | Darker, slightly thicker |
| Flavor | Sharp, bright, a little sweet from the wheat | Rich, round, deeper umami |
| Best for | Everyday cooking, marinades, stir-fries | Dipping, glazes, gluten-free cooking |

## Regular Soy Sauce

The standard Japanese style, *koikuchi*, is what most bottles on a supermarket shelf are. The wheat gives it a brighter, slightly sweet aroma. It is the all-purpose choice for marinades, stir-fries and everyday seasoning. The other Japanese types are in our [Japanese soy sauce guide](/journal/japanese-shoyu-types-guide).

## Tamari

Tamari began as the liquid that collects during miso making, which is why it is so soybean-heavy. Without much wheat, it tastes deeper and less sharp, and it clings better as a glaze or dip.

**Is tamari gluten-free?** Usually, not always. Some tamari contains a small amount of wheat. If gluten matters, buy a bottle labelled **gluten-free**, ideally certified.

## Can You Swap Them?

Yes, one for one, in almost any recipe. Tamari makes a dish a little darker and richer. Regular soy sauce in place of tamari is fine unless someone is avoiding gluten.

**Other gluten-free options:** a gluten-free soy sauce brewed with rice instead of wheat tastes closest to regular soy sauce. Coconut aminos is soy-free, sweeter and much less salty, so it isn't a direct swap.
