**Short answer:** panko is made from crustless white bread broken into large, airy flakes. Regular breadcrumbs are ground fine from whole loaves, crust included. Panko gives a lighter, crunchier coating that soaks up less oil. Regular breadcrumbs give a denser, more even crust and work better as a binder.

---

## Side by Side

| | Panko | Regular breadcrumbs |
|---|---|---|
| Made from | Crustless white bread | Whole loaves, crust included |
| Shape | Large, jagged flakes | Fine, sandy crumbs |
| Coating | Light, crunchy, stays crisp | Dense, even, can turn soggy faster |
| Oil | Absorbs less | Absorbs more |
| Best for | Frying: katsu, karaage-style cutlets, croquettes, fried shrimp | Binding meatballs and meatloaf, gratin toppings, Italian-style cutlets |

---

## Panko

The big, irregular flakes trap air and leave gaps for steam to escape, so the coating fries up crisp and stays that way. It's the coating on [chicken katsu](/journal/chicken-katsu-recipe-japanese-fried-chicken), tonkatsu and [katsudon](/journal/japanese-katsudon-recipe). Panko also works baked: toss it in a little oil first and it browns in the oven.

Plain panko is the one to stock. Buy seasoned panko only if you want the seasoning in everything.

## Regular Breadcrumbs

Fine crumbs pack tightly. That makes a thinner, more even crust and makes them better at holding things together: meatballs, meatloaf, stuffed vegetables. Italian-style breadcrumbs come pre-seasoned with herbs and cheese.

---

## Can You Swap Them?

- **Panko for breadcrumbs:** yes, nearly always. Meatballs come out a little lighter. Pulse panko in a food processor if you need it finer.
- **Breadcrumbs for panko:** it works, but the coating will be denser and less crisp. For katsu, it's worth buying panko.

## What to Buy

A bag of plain Japanese panko. Store it airtight and it keeps for months. Serve katsu with homemade [tonkatsu sauce](/journal/japanese-tonkatsu-sauce-recipe).
