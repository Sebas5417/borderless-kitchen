They share a root — *gochu* is Korean for chili pepper — and they show up in the same recipes, which is why people mix them up. But they are different products that do different jobs.

**Short answer:** gochugaru is dried red pepper flakes. Gochujang is a fermented paste *made with* gochugaru plus glutinous rice, fermented soybean powder and salt. Gochugaru brings heat and colour. Gochujang brings heat, sweetness and savoury depth at once.

---

## Side by Side

| | Gochugaru | Gochujang |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Sun-dried, seedless red pepper, flaked or powdered | Fermented chili paste |
| Texture | Dry flakes | Thick, sticky paste |
| Flavour | Fruity, smoky, moderate heat | Sweet, savoury, spicy, slightly funky |
| Main job | Heat and red colour | Body, sweetness, umami and heat |
| Classic use | Kimchi, jjigae, seasoning | Bibimbap sauce, tteokbokki, marinades |
| Keeps | Freezer, up to a year | Fridge after opening, many months |

## When to Use Gochugaru

Use it where you want clean heat and colour without sweetness or thickness. It is the non-negotiable ingredient in [kimchi](/journal/how-to-make-kimchi-complete-beginners-guide) — the coarse flakes turn the brine red and season the cabbage as it ferments. It also goes into soups and stews, seasoned vegetables and dipping sauces.

There is no good substitute. Pizza-shaker crushed red pepper is hotter, harsher, full of seeds and the wrong texture. Our [gochugaru guide](/journal/gochugaru-korean-red-pepper-flakes-guide) explains coarse versus fine and how to store it.

## When to Use Gochujang

Use it where you want a sauce to have body. Gochujang thickens, sweetens and deepens anything it touches: the red sauce on bibimbap, the glaze on tteokbokki, a marinade for pork. Heat levels vary by brand, so check the rating on the tub.

It can also be made at home — our [homemade gochujang guide](/journal/gochujang-sauce-recipe-how-to-make-it-at-home) shows how, and why the fermentation matters.

## Can You Substitute One for the Other?

**Not well.**

- **Gochujang in place of gochugaru:** you add sweetness and thickness you did not want, and kimchi made this way ferments differently and tastes cloying.
- **Gochugaru in place of gochujang:** you get the heat but lose the body and sweetness. In a pinch, mix gochugaru with a little miso or doenjang, a little sugar and water to approximate the paste — it works for a marinade, not for a sauce that needs to cling.

Most Korean home cooks keep both, and so should you. Together they cost less than one takeout meal.
