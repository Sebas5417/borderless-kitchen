A bento (弁当, often *obentō*) is a single-portion meal packed in a box to be eaten later: at school, at work, on a train, at a picnic. In Japan it's ordinary food, not a special occasion. Parents pack them for children, office workers pack them for themselves, and convenience stores and department store food halls sell them ready-made.

What makes a bento different from a sandwich in a bag is the packing. Each element has its own place, the box is filled snugly so nothing slides around, and the food is chosen because it tastes good at room temperature.

---

## A Short History

Packed food for travel is old in Japan. By the Kamakura period, people carried *hoshi-ii* (糒), cooked rice that had been dried so it would keep, and ate it as is or softened in water.

Two later forms shaped the bento most people picture today:

- **Makunouchi bento.** The name means "between the acts." It's associated with Edo-period theatre, where audiences ate during intermissions. A makunouchi bento has small rice portions, often pressed into shapes, alongside a spread of side dishes such as grilled fish, egg, simmered vegetables and pickles. The name is still used for this kind of assorted box.
- **Ekiben.** Railway station bento, sold on platforms and in stations once trains spread across Japan in the late 1800s. Many regions have their own, built around a local specialty, and buying the ekiben is part of the trip for a lot of travelers.

In recent decades, **kyaraben** (character bento) became popular: food arranged to look like animals, cartoon characters or faces, with nori cut-outs and shaped rice. It's mostly a parent-to-child thing, and it's optional. An everyday home bento is rice, a protein, some vegetables and a pickle, packed neatly.

## The 4:3:2:1 Guideline

A ratio you'll often see for packing a balanced bento is roughly **4 parts rice, 3 parts protein, 2 parts vegetables, 1 part pickle or treat**, measured by space in the box rather than by weight.

Treat it as a starting point, not a rule. In practice it means:

- A little under **half the box** (about 40%) is rice or another starch.
- The protein takes up most of the rest.
- Vegetables get a real share, not a garnish.
- A small corner is for something sharp or sweet: a pickle, a few pieces of fruit.

Some cooks also think in colours: something red, yellow, green, black or brown and white. It's a quick check that the box has variety, and it tends to make the box look good without extra effort.

## What Goes Inside

### Rice

Plain short-grain rice is the base. Make it with good rice (our [short-grain rice guide](/journal/best-short-grain-rice-for-japanese-and-korean-cooking) covers what to buy) and, if you cook it often, a [rice cooker](/journal/best-japanese-rice-cooker-for-home-cooks) helps. Sprinkle the top with [furikake](/journal/furikake-what-it-is-and-how-to-use-it-beyond-rice), or press a single umeboshi (pickled plum) into the centre.

[Onigiri](/recipes/japanese-onigiri-recipe) are the portable alternative: rice balls, usually with a filling and often wrapped in nori. Fillings and shapes are in our [onigiri guide](/journal/onigiri-types-fillings-guide-japanese-rice-balls).

### Protein

- **Tamagoyaki.** Rolled egg is close to a bento standard. The sweeter Kanto style is common in lunch boxes. Make it with the [tamagoyaki recipe](/recipes/japanese-tamagoyaki-rolled-egg-bento), which covers both the sweet and savoury versions.
- **Karaage.** Japanese fried chicken stays good at room temperature, which is why it shows up in so many bento. Use the [karaage recipe](/recipes/japanese-karaage-fried-chicken-recipe).
- **Teriyaki.** Chicken or salmon in a soy, mirin and sugar glaze. Try the [teriyaki chicken](/recipes/japanese-teriyaki-chicken), and keep the sauce thick so it doesn't pool.
- **Grilled salted fish**, usually salmon, is another classic.

### Vegetables

Blanched greens dressed with sesame (*goma-ae*), green beans, broccoli, carrot simmered in dashi, a few cherry tomatoes. A vinegared cucumber salad (*sunomono*) is refreshing, but drain it well and pack it in its own cup. Simmered dishes with [konnyaku](/journal/what-is-konnyaku-japanese-konjac-guide) and root vegetables also travel well.

### Pickles and a treat

A little *tsukemono*: pickled daikon, cucumber, ginger or umeboshi. Or some fruit, such as orange segments or grapes.

## Food Safety

A bento often sits for hours before it's eaten, so it's worth taking seriously. The standard advice:

- **Cool cooked food completely before closing the lid.** Packing warm food traps steam, which makes things soggy and keeps the food warm longer. Spread it out on a plate to cool faster, and get it into the fridge or a cold bag soon after (cooling time counts toward the two-hour limit).
- **Keep it cold.** Refrigerate the packed bento, and use an insulated bag with at least two ice packs (or one ice pack and a frozen drink) if it won't be refrigerated before lunch. Perishable food shouldn't sit at room temperature for more than two hours, or one hour above 90°F (32°C).
- **Pack moist items separately.** Dressed vegetables, sauces and cut fruit go in silicone cups or small lidded containers so liquid doesn't spread into the rice.
- **Cook eggs through** for a packed lunch, and reheat leftovers thoroughly before cooling and packing them.
- **Use clean hands or utensils** when shaping rice, or wrap onigiri in plastic wrap to shape them.

## A Make-Ahead Plan

Bento mornings are fast because most of the work happens earlier.

- **On the weekend:** make a batch of karaage or teriyaki and refrigerate it. Blanch and portion vegetables. Make a jar of quick pickles.
- **The night before:** cook the rice on a timer if your cooker has one, or cook it and refrigerate. Make the tamagoyaki: it keeps a day in the fridge. Put the empty box, cups and dividers out.
- **In the morning:** reheat the protein, then let everything cool on a plate. Pack the rice first, then the protein, then fill the gaps with vegetables and pickles so nothing moves. Close the lid once everything is cool.

## Choosing a Bento Box

**Modern plastic boxes** are the practical choice for most people. Look for:

- **Two tiers.** Rice in one, everything else in the other. Smaller tiers suit children.
- **A leak-proof lid** with a silicone seal, if you'll carry it in a bag.
- **Microwave-safe**, if you'll reheat at work. Check the manufacturer's instructions: often the box is microwave-safe but the lid isn't.
- **Dividers and cups.** Removable dividers and silicone cups keep wet and dry foods apart.

**Traditional boxes** are lovely to use but need more care:

- **Lacquerware** (or lacquer-style) boxes are for display and special occasions. They usually aren't microwave- or dishwasher-safe.
- **Magewappa** are boxes of thin bent wood, traditionally Akita cedar (*Akita sugi*), most closely associated with Odate in Akita Prefecture. The wood absorbs some moisture from the rice, so it doesn't go soggy. Hand-wash and dry them well, and don't microwave them.

**Useful extras:** an onigiri mould for even rice balls, a small tamagoyaki pan, a few silicone cups, and furikake for the rice.
