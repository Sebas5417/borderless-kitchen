Japanese miso and Korean doenjang look alike in the tub and are both fermented soybeans with salt. But they are made by different methods, and you can taste it.

**Short answer:** miso is soybeans fermented with *koji* — a cultivated mould grown on rice or barley — which makes it rounder and often sweeter. Doenjang is made from *meju*, blocks of cooked soybean left to ferment with wild microbes, which makes it earthier, saltier and funkier. Miso is the smoother of the two; doenjang is the bolder.

---

## Side by Side

| | Miso (Japan) | Doenjang (Korea) |
|---|---|---|
| Fermentation starter | Koji (*Aspergillus oryzae*) on rice or barley | Meju blocks, wild fermentation |
| Texture | Smooth paste | Coarser, often chunky |
| Flavour | Mild to rich, sweeter (white) to deep (red) | Earthy, pungent, salty, funky |
| Heat tolerance | Add at the end; boiling dulls the aroma | Built to simmer; stews boil for a long time |
| Signature dish | Miso soup | Doenjang jjigae |

## When to Use Miso

Use miso where you want rounded savouriness that will not dominate: soups finished off the heat, glazes for fish, dressings, even butter for pasta and corn. White (shiro) miso is the most versatile first purchase; red (aka) miso is longer-fermented and stronger. Our [miso entry](/culture/miso) covers the types, and [fermentation science](/journal/fermentation-science-how-miso-and-aged-cheese-work-the-same-way) explains why it pairs so well with aged cheese.

## When to Use Doenjang

Use doenjang where a dish simmers and you want depth that survives the heat — above all doenjang jjigae, the stew many Korean households eat weekly. It also makes the classic dipping paste for barbecue when mixed with gochujang. See [how to use doenjang](/journal/korean-doenjang-uses-guide).

## Can You Substitute?

**Partly.**

- **Miso for doenjang:** works in a pinch, especially red miso. The stew will taste milder and sweeter. Add a little more than the recipe says.
- **Doenjang for miso:** use less — it is saltier and stronger — and do not use it in delicate miso soup, where its funk takes over.

If you cook both cuisines, keep both. They last for many months in the fridge.

## Why This Matters to Us

Fermented soybean paste is the quiet foundation of both kitchens. In *Tokyo Meets Tuscany*, miso sits beside Parmigiano; in *Seoul Meets Mexico City*, doenjang sits beside mole. Same principle — time turning beans into depth — two traditions.
