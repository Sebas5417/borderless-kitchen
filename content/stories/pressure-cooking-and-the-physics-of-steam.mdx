At sea level, water boils at 212°F (100°C). No matter how high you turn the flame, liquid water in an open pot never exceeds this temperature — additional heat just converts water to steam faster, not hotter.

A sealed pressure cooker traps that steam. As steam builds up, pressure inside the pot increases. Higher pressure raises the boiling point of water: at 15 PSI above atmospheric pressure (the typical pressure of a stovetop cooker), water boils at approximately 250°F (121°C). Food cooks in water that is 38°F hotter than any open pot can achieve.

That 38-degree difference is enormous. Every 18°F (10°C) increase in temperature roughly doubles the rate of chemical reactions — the same principle behind the Arrhenius equation in chemistry. At 250°F versus 212°F, cooking reactions proceed approximately four times as fast. A braise that takes 3 hours in a Dutch oven takes 45 minutes in a pressure cooker. Beans that require 90 minutes of simmering are done in 25.

## What Pressure Cooking Does Well

**Collagen-rich cuts:** Tough cuts of meat (beef short ribs, pork shoulder, lamb shanks, oxtail, chicken thighs) contain collagen — a structural protein that converts to gelatin at temperatures above 160°F (71°C) but requires sustained heat over time to complete the transformation. The higher temperature and steam environment of a pressure cooker accelerates this conversion dramatically. A short rib that needs 3–4 hours of low-and-slow braising becomes fork-tender in 45–55 minutes.

**Dried legumes:** Beans and lentils require hydration (water absorption) and protein denaturation. Both happen faster at higher temperatures. Unsoaked chickpeas cook in 35–40 minutes; unsoaked black beans in 25–30 minutes. This eliminates the overnight soak requirement entirely, though soaked beans cook even faster and with more even texture.

**Stocks and broths:** A chicken stock that extracts full gelatin and flavor in 4–6 hours of simmering on the stovetop is done in 1–1.5 hours under pressure. The higher temperature also extracts more collagen from bones, producing a richer, more viscous stock.

**Root vegetables and grains:** Dense vegetables (beets, carrots, potatoes) and grains (wheat berries, farro, steel-cut oats) that normally require long cooking times are ready in a fraction of the time. Steel-cut oats: 10–12 minutes versus 30 minutes. Beets: 15–20 minutes versus 45–60 minutes.

## What Pressure Cooking Does Poorly

**Delicate proteins:** Fish, shellfish, and eggs overcook in seconds under the conditions of a pressure cooker — the environment is too hot and the timing margin too narrow. These are better cooked with methods that offer more temperature control.

**Green vegetables:** The high heat and steam rapidly break down chlorophyll (the pigment that keeps vegetables green) and cell structure. Broccoli, peas, asparagus, and spinach turn army-green and mushy within minutes. Add green vegetables after pressure cooking, using residual heat.

**Crispy or browned textures:** Pressure cooking is a moist-heat method — no browning (Maillard reaction) occurs inside the sealed pot. Always sear meat in the open pot before sealing (most modern pressure cookers and Instant Pots have a sauté function for this purpose). The crust developed before sealing survives the pressure cooking phase.

**Reducing sauces:** A sealed pot prevents evaporation. Braising liquid inside a pressure cooker does not reduce. If a reduced, concentrated sauce is the goal, remove the lid after cooking and simmer uncovered to reduce, or transfer the liquid to a separate pan.

## How to Use a Pressure Cooker

**Step 1: Brown the meat.** Use the sauté function or a separate pan. Season, then brown in oil over high heat until a deep crust forms. Do not skip this step — the Maillard reaction adds complexity that pressure cooking itself cannot develop.

**Step 2: Build the base.** Add aromatics (onion, garlic, carrot, celery) and cook briefly. Add tomato paste if using, cook 1 minute. Deglaze with wine or stock, scraping up the fond.

**Step 3: Add liquid and protein.** Return the browned meat. Add stock or braising liquid — typically 1–2 cups minimum (enough to generate steam and maintain pressure). The liquid does not need to fully submerge the meat; pressure steam surrounds everything.

**Step 4: Seal and pressurize.** Lock the lid. Heat over medium-high until the cooker reaches full pressure (the valve rises or weight begins to rock). Reduce heat to maintain pressure — a gentle rock or hiss, not an aggressive blast.

**Step 5: Time from full pressure.** Timing begins when full pressure is reached, not when the lid was sealed.

**Step 6: Release pressure.** Two methods:
- **Natural release:** Turn off heat and allow pressure to drop on its own (10–20 minutes). Best for meat — the continued gentle cooking as pressure drops keeps the proteins relaxed and moist.
- **Quick release:** Open the valve manually to release steam immediately. Best for vegetables and dishes that would overcook during a natural release. Steam releases forcefully — keep hands and face clear.

## The Instant Pot Question

Electric pressure cookers (Instant Pot and similar) operate at slightly lower pressure (10–12 PSI) than stovetop models (15 PSI). The cooking temperature is correspondingly lower — approximately 239°F–244°F (115°C–118°C) versus 250°F (121°C). Times for electric cookers run 10–20% longer than stovetop times for the same results.

The tradeoff: electric models are significantly more convenient, with automatic pressure regulation, timers, and keep-warm functions. For most home applications, the slight time difference is irrelevant compared to the convenience gain.

The physics is the same in both. Understand the mechanism, and both devices become predictable.
