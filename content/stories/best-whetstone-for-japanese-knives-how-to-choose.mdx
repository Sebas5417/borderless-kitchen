**Short answer:** buy a **1000/6000 combination water stone**. The 1000 side sharpens and the 6000 side polishes. That covers almost all home sharpening. The **King 1000/6000** is the classic inexpensive choice. Add a **flattening stone** later, because a whetstone wears hollow with use.

---

## What the Grit Numbers Mean

Lower numbers are coarser and remove more steel; higher numbers are finer and polish the edge.

| Grit | What it's for | Need it? |
|---|---|---|
| 200–400 | Repairing chips, reshaping a damaged edge | Only if a knife is chipped |
| 800–1000 | Sharpening a dull knife | **Yes: the workhorse** |
| 3000–6000 | Refining and polishing the edge | **Yes: for a keen, smooth edge** |
| 8000+ | Mirror polish | No, for most home cooks |

---

## Soaking vs Splash-and-Go

- **Soaking stones** need 10 to 15 minutes in water before use. Many inexpensive stones, including the King 1000 side, are this type.
- **Splash-and-go stones** need only a splash of water on the surface. They are usually harder, wear more slowly and cost more. Shapton's Kuromaku (Pro) series is a well-known example.

Either works. Check your stone's instructions: some fine stones shouldn't be soaked for long.

## Keep It Flat

A whetstone wears a dip in the middle as you use it, and a dished stone puts a curve on your edge. Rub the stone flat on a **flattening stone** (or a coarse diamond plate) every few sessions.

## The First-Session Routine

1. **Wet the stone** (soak it, or splash it, depending on type). Set it on a damp towel or its base so it doesn't slide.
2. **Find the angle.** Most Japanese double-bevel knives are sharpened at roughly 10 to 15 degrees per side. On a typical chef's knife, 15 degrees means lifting the spine roughly 1 cm off the stone; a clip-on angle guide helps while you learn.
3. **1000 side:** push the edge across the stone in sections, heel to tip, keeping the angle steady. Work one side until you can feel a slight burr along the opposite edge, then do the other side.
4. **6000 side:** repeat with lighter pressure and fewer strokes to refine and remove the burr.
5. **Rinse and dry** the knife and stone. Let the stone dry completely before storing it.

Single-bevel knives like a yanagiba or deba are sharpened differently: almost all the work is on the front side. Learn on a double-bevel knife first.

**Skip pull-through sharpeners** for Japanese knives. They grind at a fixed, usually wider angle and can chip hard steel.
