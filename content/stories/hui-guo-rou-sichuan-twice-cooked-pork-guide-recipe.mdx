In Sichuan culinary education and culinary competitions, hui guo rou is one of the dishes used to evaluate a cook's fundamental skill. Not because it is the most complex dish — it is actually quite simple — but because it requires getting several things exactly right simultaneously: the initial boil to the right degree of doneness (overcooked means it crumbles when sliced; undercooked means it's tough), the precision slice (even, very thin, with the fat and skin intact), the heat control in the wok (high enough for caramelization but not so high that the doubanjiang burns before the pork cooks), and the balance of the two pastes (doubanjiang spicy and fermented versus sweet bean paste's rounded sweetness).

The *return pot* name describes the technique: the pork is cooked in one pot (boiled), then returns to a second pot (the wok) for the final stir-fry. Some versions return the pork to the same pot; the concept is the same.

---

## The First Cook: Boiling

**The pork belly:** A piece of pork belly, skin-on — approximately 400–500g, in one piece. The skin and fat layers are essential; lean pork belly won't produce the characteristic rendered fat and caramelized-exterior texture.

**The boil:** Place pork belly in cold water; bring to a boil; add ginger slices, Shaoxing wine, and scallion. Simmer 20–25 minutes. The pork is done when a chopstick inserted meets resistance but not firmness — it should be just cooked through, still slightly firm (not fully tender).

**The cool:** Remove from water; let cool to room temperature (or cold water shock to stop cooking). The cooling is necessary for clean slicing.

**The slice:** Using a sharp knife, slice the cooled pork belly as thin as possible — ideally 3–4mm. The slice should show the distinct layers (skin, fat, meat) in each piece.

---

## The Pastes: The Two-Paste Balance

**Doubanjiang (豆瓣酱):** Fermented broad bean paste with chili — the foundation of Sichuan cooking. Provides: deep red chili oil color; fermented, salty, umami depth; moderate heat. Look for *Pi Xian doubanjiang* (郫县豆瓣) — the standard for Sichuan cooking; aged versions have more complexity.

**Sweet bean paste (甜面酱 tiánmiànjiàng):** A wheat-flour-based sweet bean paste — thicker, sweeter, and less spicy than doubanjiang. Provides: sweetness that rounds the doubanjiang's sharpness; a slightly sticky lacquering quality on the pork.

**The ratio:** Approximately 2 parts doubanjiang to 1 part sweet bean paste. Too much sweet paste makes the dish cloying; too little makes it too aggressively spicy. The balance is the technique.

---

## The Garlic Shoots (Suanmiao)

**Suanmiao (蒜苗):** Garlic shoots or garlic greens — the green stalks of immature garlic before the bulb forms. They look like large scallions but taste strongly of garlic. Cut into diagonal pieces.

**The timing:** Added late in the stir-fry — they cook in 60–90 seconds; they should be wilted but still bright green and slightly crunchy. Overcooked suanmiao is yellow and mushy.

**Substitute:** Leeks (the closest substitute available in Western markets), or scallions with an extra garlic clove added.

---

## The Complete Recipe

**Serves:** 2–3 (over rice) | **Time:** 1 hour

### First Cook
- 400g pork belly, skin-on, in one piece
- 4 slices fresh ginger
- 2 tablespoons Shaoxing wine
- 2 scallion stalks

### Stir-Fry
- Cooked pork belly (from above), sliced thin
- 2 tablespoons doubanjiang (Pi Xian preferred)
- 1 tablespoon sweet bean paste (tiánmiànjiàng)
- 4 garlic cloves, sliced
- 3 slices fresh ginger, julienned
- 200g garlic shoots (suanmiao) or leeks, cut diagonally
- 1 tablespoon Shaoxing wine
- 1 teaspoon soy sauce (optional, to adjust salt)
- 2 tablespoons neutral oil

### Method

**1. First cook:** Place pork belly in cold water; add ginger, Shaoxing wine, and scallion; bring to a boil; reduce to a simmer; cook 20–25 minutes until just cooked through. Remove; cool to room temperature. Slice thin (3–4mm) against the grain; set aside.

**2. Stir-fry:** Heat wok over high heat until smoking; add oil. Add doubanjiang; stir-fry 30–60 seconds until the paste darkens slightly and the oil turns red — this is the *xiāng* (fragrance release). Add sweet bean paste; stir 20 seconds. Add garlic and ginger; stir 20 seconds.

**3. Add pork:** Add sliced pork belly; stir-fry over high heat 2–3 minutes, tossing continuously, until the pork slices have absorbed the paste coating and developed slight caramelization. The pork fat will render slightly; the edges may curl.

**4. Add Shaoxing wine:** Splash Shaoxing wine around the wok edge; stir quickly.

**5. Add garlic shoots:** Add garlic shoots; stir-fry 60–90 seconds until wilted but still bright green. Taste; add soy sauce if more salt is needed.

**Serve:** Immediately over steamed white rice.
