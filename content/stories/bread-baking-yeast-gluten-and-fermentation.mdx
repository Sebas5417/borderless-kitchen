Bread is, at its core, a fermentation project. Four ingredients — flour, water, salt, yeast — combine and then transform over time into something entirely different. Understanding the transformation is understanding the craft.

---

## The Four Ingredients and What They Do

**Flour** provides structure. Wheat flour contains two proteins — glutenin and gliadin — that, when hydrated and agitated, link together into gluten: a stretchy, elastic network that traps the gas produced by yeast and gives bread its structure. The protein content of the flour determines the potential strength of this network. Bread flour (12–14% protein) builds strong, chewy gluten networks. All-purpose flour (10–12%) produces softer, more tender bread. Cake flour (7–9%) has so little protein it produces very little gluten — unsuitable for yeasted breads.

**Water** hydrates the flour proteins, enabling gluten development, and activates the yeast. The ratio of water to flour — expressed as a percentage of the flour's weight, called hydration — dramatically affects the dough's handling and the final bread's texture. A 65% hydration dough (650g water per 1000g flour) is manageable and produces a moderately open crumb. An 80% hydration dough is slack and sticky but produces a more open, airy crumb when handled correctly. Higher hydration = more complex handling, more open crumb.

**Salt** serves multiple functions. It strengthens the gluten network by tightening protein bonds. It slows fermentation, giving the baker more control over timing. It suppresses competing bacteria that would otherwise sour the dough. And it is the primary seasoning — bread without salt tastes flat and hollow. Standard range: 1.8–2.2% of flour weight.

**Yeast** — commercial instant yeast, active dry yeast, or wild yeast in sourdough starter — consumes the sugars in the flour and produces carbon dioxide (which leavens the bread) and alcohol (which contributes flavor). Yeast activity is governed almost entirely by temperature: cold slows it; warmth accelerates it. At refrigerator temperatures (38–40°F), yeast activity is minimal — this is how slow cold fermentation works. At room temperature (68–75°F), yeast is active. Above 95°F, it begins to die. Above 140°F, it is killed.

## Gluten Development

Gluten does not exist in dry flour. It forms when flour is hydrated and agitated. The agitation — kneading, folding, stretching — aligns the gluten proteins into increasingly organized, connected sheets.

**Underworked dough:** The gluten network is weak and disorganized. The dough tears easily, doesn't hold its shape well, and produces dense bread because the network can't trap gas effectively.

**Properly developed dough:** The windowpane test — stretch a small piece of dough thin enough to see light through without it tearing. This indicates the gluten network is strong enough to trap gas and hold structure.

**Methods for gluten development:**

*Traditional kneading:* Push the dough away with the heel of the hand, fold, rotate, repeat. 8–12 minutes by hand. Effective but labor-intensive.

*Stretch and fold:* Used in high-hydration doughs (which are too sticky to knead traditionally). With wet hands, stretch one side of the dough up and fold it over the mass. Rotate 90 degrees, repeat four times. This is one "set." Perform 4 sets at 30-minute intervals during the first 2 hours of fermentation. The resting time between sets allows the gluten to relax so the next set can develop it further.

*Time (autolyse):* Mixing flour and water and letting them rest for 20–60 minutes before adding yeast and salt allows the flour to fully hydrate and gluten to begin forming on its own, reducing the kneading needed afterward.

## Fermentation: Bulk Rise and Final Proof

Fermentation happens in two stages, and both matter.

**Bulk fermentation (first rise):** Occurs after mixing, before shaping. The yeast is active, producing CO2 that causes the dough to expand. Simultaneously, organic acids and alcohols are developing — these are the flavor compounds that make bread taste complex rather than simply yeasty.

Duration depends on temperature and yeast quantity:
- 70°F with 0.5% instant yeast: 3–4 hours
- 75°F with 0.5% instant yeast: 2–3 hours
- Refrigerator (38–40°F), same yeast: 8–16 hours (cold retard)

The longer, cooler the fermentation, the more complex the flavor. This is why overnight refrigerator-proofed bread tastes better than two-hour room-temperature bread.

**Knowing when bulk fermentation is complete:** The dough should be noticeably larger (roughly 50–75% volume increase for commercial yeast; look for bubbles on the surface and sides for sourdough). Press a floured finger into the surface — it should spring back slowly. If it springs back immediately, it's under-proofed. If it doesn't spring back, it may be over-proofed.

**Shaping:** After bulk fermentation, the dough is divided (if making multiple loaves) and shaped. Shaping builds surface tension — the outer skin of the dough tightens, which helps the loaf hold its form and produces good oven spring. A poorly shaped loaf spreads flat; a well-tensioned one rises upward.

**Final proof (second rise):** After shaping, the dough proofs one more time. This can happen at room temperature (45–90 minutes) or in the refrigerator overnight (better flavor, easier to handle for scoring).

**Over-proofing:** If either rise goes too long, the yeast exhausts its food supply and the gluten network weakens from extended acid exposure. Over-proofed dough deflates when scored, produces dense bread, and may taste sour or yeasty rather than complex. The fix is not more proofing time — it's baking at the right moment.

## The Bake: Oven Spring and Crust

**Oven spring:** In the first 10–15 minutes of baking, the yeast has one last burst of activity before the heat kills it (above 140°F), producing a final surge of CO2 that causes the loaf to expand dramatically. This is oven spring, and it is responsible for much of the final loaf's height and open crumb.

Steam is critical in this phase. Steam keeps the crust surface moist and extensible so the loaf can expand freely. Without steam, the crust sets immediately and the loaf cannot spring. Professional deck ovens inject steam; home bakers use a covered Dutch oven (the lid traps the bread's own steam) or place a pan of water in the oven.

**The Dutch oven method:** Preheat the Dutch oven in the oven at 475–500°F (245–260°C). Carefully lower the shaped loaf in, cover, and bake for 20 minutes. The steam from the dough is trapped inside. Remove the lid and bake another 20–25 minutes for color and crust development.

**Scoring:** The cuts made on the surface of the loaf before baking are not decorative — they control where the loaf expands. An unscored loaf will burst unpredictably at weak points in the crust. A properly scored loaf opens along the score in a controlled, predictable way (the "ear"), producing better volume and a more even crumb.

**Doneness:** The loaf is done when the internal temperature reaches 200–210°F (93–99°C). The crust should be deep brown — paler means underbaked, which produces a gummy interior. Tap the bottom: a hollow sound indicates the interior is fully baked. Cool on a wire rack for at least 30 minutes before cutting — the crumb continues to set as steam redistributes.

## The Simplest Starting Point

For first-time bread bakers, a no-knead overnight loaf eliminates most of the variables:

1. Mix 450g bread flour, 360g water (80%), 9g salt, 1g instant yeast in a bowl. Stir until combined — rough is fine.
2. Cover and leave at room temperature for 12–18 hours.
3. Turn out onto a floured surface, fold a few times, shape into a round, and place seam-side up in a floured proofing basket or bowl lined with a floured towel.
4. Refrigerate for 1–8 hours, or let rest at room temperature for 45–60 minutes.
5. Bake in a preheated Dutch oven at 475°F: covered 20 minutes, uncovered 25 minutes.

This recipe teaches everything important about bread: what fermented dough looks and smells like, how shaping works, what oven spring looks and sounds like, and what a finished loaf feels like when it's done. Everything more complex builds on these fundamentals.

---

Bread baking is unusual among cooking techniques because it cannot be rushed. The time is not a constraint — it is the ingredient. Fermentation takes as long as it takes. The baker's job is to understand what's happening at each stage and intervene at the right moment, not before.
