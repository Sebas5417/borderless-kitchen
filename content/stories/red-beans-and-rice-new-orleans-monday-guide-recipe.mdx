Louis Armstrong signed his letters "Red Beans and Ricely Yours." He traveled with a pressure cooker and made red beans and rice on the road in hotel rooms. When asked about his favorite food, he always said red beans and rice. There is a bronze statue of him in Armstrong Park in New Orleans. He would have approved of the beans.

The dish is practically the symbol of New Orleans home cooking — not the fancy Creole restaurant cooking of Commander's Palace, but the everyday cooking of working-class households that has been feeding the city for centuries. The Monday tradition dates to the 1800s: Monday was washday (laundry, done by hand, took all day), so the cook put the beans on in the morning with salt pork and let them cook unattended all day. By evening, dinner was ready.

## The Beans

**Camellia brand red kidney beans** are specifically the standard in New Orleans. If you can find them, use them — there is a meaningful difference in flavor and texture from generic kidney beans. Outside Louisiana, dried small red beans or standard red kidney beans work.

Always start with dried beans, soaked overnight. Canned beans are not the same dish — the long cooking time is what breaks down the beans and creates the creamy, thick gravy that makes real red beans and rice distinctive.

## The Holy Trinity

Onion, celery, and green bell pepper — cooked in the pork fat before the beans.

## The Pork

**Andouille sausage** is the standard for flavor. Sliced into rounds, browned first to render fat and add color.

**Pickled pork or salt pork** is the traditional addition — a piece of pork cured in spiced brine (vinegar, pickling spice, garlic), slow-cooked with the beans until it falls apart. Pickled pork is hard to find outside Louisiana; ham hocks or smoked pork neck bones make good substitutes.

## The Technique

Low and slow. The beans go in after the trinity with enough water to cover by several inches. They cook for 2–3 hours, stirring occasionally, until the beans are completely tender. Then approximately 20–25% of the beans are smashed against the side of the pot — this is what creates the thick, creamy "gravy." The beans are not entirely smooth (not refried beans); they are creamy but still have whole beans and texture.

**Recipe: New Orleans Red Beans and Rice (Serves 6–8)**

- 450g dried red kidney beans (Camellia brand preferred), soaked overnight
- 400g andouille sausage, cut into rounds
- 250g pickled pork, ham hocks, or smoked pork neck bones
- 1 large onion, diced
- 3 stalks celery, diced
- 1 green bell pepper, diced
- 5 cloves garlic, minced
- 2 bay leaves
- 1 teaspoon dried thyme
- 1 teaspoon dried oregano
- 1/4 teaspoon cayenne pepper
- Salt, black pepper
- 2 liters water or chicken stock
- Cooked long-grain white rice to serve
- Green onions, hot sauce, and white bread for serving

**Method:**

1. Soak beans overnight in cold water. Drain.

2. In a large heavy pot, brown andouille over medium-high heat until caramelized, about 5 minutes. Remove; leave fat in pot.

3. Add pickled pork/ham hocks to the same fat. Brown lightly on all sides.

4. Add onion, celery, and bell pepper. Cook in the fat until softened, 8 minutes.

5. Add garlic, bay leaves, thyme, oregano, and cayenne. Stir 1 minute.

6. Add drained beans. Add enough water or stock to cover beans by 5cm.

7. Bring to a boil; reduce heat to a gentle simmer. Cook uncovered (or partially covered) for 2–3 hours, stirring every 30 minutes, until beans are very tender and the liquid has thickened.

8. When beans are fully tender, use a large spoon to smash approximately 1/4 of them against the side of the pot. Stir to incorporate — the smashed beans thicken the liquid into a creamy gravy. If too thick, add water; if too thin, continue simmering.

9. Return andouille to the pot. Season generously with salt, black pepper, and cayenne. Remove bay leaves.

10. Serve over white rice with sliced green onions, hot sauce, and white bread.

*Red beans and rice improve significantly the next day as the flavors meld. Reheat with a splash of water to loosen. They freeze very well.*
