**Short answer:** buy a **Benriner Japanese mandoline**. It is light, very sharp, adjusts its thickness with a dial, and comes with julienne blades. It is the slicer many restaurant cooks keep at their station. Always use the hand guard or a **cut-resistant glove**: mandolines cause some of the worst kitchen cuts, usually to the fingertips.

---

## Why a Japanese Mandoline

Heavy French-style mandolines are stainless steel, with stands and many settings. Japanese mandolines are simple plastic frames with a single very sharp, angled blade. They're cheaper, lighter, easy to wash, and many cooks find they slice as cleanly as models costing several times more.

**Benriner** is the best-known Japanese brand. The standard model is narrow and suits cucumbers, radishes, carrots and onions. The wider **Super Benriner** takes bigger vegetables like cabbage and large potatoes. Both come with interchangeable julienne blades.

## What You'll Use It For

- **Japanese:** paper-thin cabbage beside [tonkatsu](/journal/japanese-tonkatsu-sauce-recipe), cucumber for [sunomono](/journal/sunomono-recipe-japanese-cucumber-salad), shaved daikon.
- **Korean:** julienned radish for *musaengchae* and the radish that goes into kimchi filling.
- **Mexican:** shaved radish and cabbage to top tacos, tostadas and pozole.
- **Italian:** shaved fennel salads, and potatoes for a gratin.

---

## The Safety Rule

**Never slice the last inch by hand.** The blade is sharp enough that a fingertip goes through before you feel it.

- Use the **hand guard** that comes with it, or
- Wear a **cut-resistant glove** on the hand holding the food. Many cooks prefer this because the guard can be awkward with small vegetables.
- Stop when the piece gets small. Save the end for stock or the cutting board.

## Care

Rinse it right after use, brushing away from the blade, and let it air-dry. Don't leave it soaking in a sink, where nobody can see the blade.
