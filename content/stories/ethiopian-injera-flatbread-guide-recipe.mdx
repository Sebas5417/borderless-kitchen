Injera has been the foundation of Ethiopian cuisine for at least a thousand years — possibly much longer. The teff plant (Eragrostis tef) is indigenous to the Ethiopian Highlands, where it has been cultivated since at least 3000 BCE. Teff is a micro-grain (the world's smallest grain by volume) with exceptional nutritional value: high in iron, calcium, and complex carbohydrates.

The injera system is one of the most elegant solutions in food culture. By using the bread as plate and utensil simultaneously: there are no dishes to wash (the injera absorbs the stew juices and is eaten); there is no serving of food (everything is placed on the shared injera, and everyone eats from the common plate); and the flavor system works — the slightly sour injera balances spiced, rich stews like doro wat and misir wat.

## The Teff Flour and Fermentation

**Teff flour** is available at Ethiopian grocery stores, natural food stores, and online. The authentic injera is made entirely from teff flour. Some commercial and home recipes use a blend of teff and other flours (wheat, barley, sorghum) — these reduce the characteristic tang and the slight earthy flavor of pure teff, but make fermentation more reliable.

**Fermentation:** The injera batter must ferment for 2–3 days (or up to 5 days for more sour flavor). During fermentation, wild yeasts and bacteria in the teff flour produce carbon dioxide (for lift and bubbles) and lactic acid (for sourness). The starter is simply teff flour mixed with water — no added yeast or salt.

Fermentation is the step that makes injera what it is. Unfermented teff batter produces a thin, non-sour flatbread — not injera.

## The Cooking

Injera is cooked in a mitad — a large, circular clay griddle, heated over fire. The batter is poured in a spiral from the outside in, covering the surface. It cooks covered for 2–3 minutes; the top surface becomes set and the characteristic "eyes" (holes from CO2 escaping) form. Injera is cooked only on one side — the bottom that contacts the griddle; the top remains soft and spongy.

At home: a large non-stick pan works. The injera will be smaller (limited by pan size) but the technique is the same.

**Recipe: Injera (Makes 6–8 large injera)**

*Begin 2–3 days before serving.*

**Batter:**
- 300g teff flour
- 600ml warm water (approximately — adjust during fermentation)
- Small pinch of active dry yeast (optional — speeds fermentation, not traditional)
- 1/2 teaspoon salt (added only just before cooking)

**Method — Fermentation (2–3 days):**

1. In a large bowl, whisk teff flour with 450ml warm water until a smooth batter forms. Cover loosely with a cloth (not airtight — the fermentation needs to breathe).

2. Leave at room temperature (ideally 21–25°C). Stir once per day.

3. After 12–24 hours: you should see small bubbles forming. The batter will smell slightly sour and yeasty.

4. After 2–3 days: the batter smells distinctly sour and fermented. If you prefer milder sourness, proceed after 2 days. For stronger sourness, ferment up to 5 days.

5. The batter may separate (liquid on top, flour below) — stir to recombine. Adjust consistency with water to a thin, pourable consistency (like loose pancake batter).

**Cooking:**

1. Stir salt into the fermented batter.

2. Heat a large non-stick pan or cast iron griddle over medium heat. No oil is needed (or a very light wipe).

3. Pour enough batter to coat the surface in a thin layer, starting from the outside and spiraling in. Work quickly — it should set within seconds.

4. Cover the pan with a lid. Cook 2–3 minutes until the top is set (no longer liquid), small holes have formed across the surface, and the edges begin to lift.

5. Remove with a spatula. The bottom will be slightly golden; the top should be soft and spongy.

6. Cool on a clean towel, top-side up, before stacking.

**To serve:** Lay the largest injera on a round platter as the base. Place stews and salads on top. Roll remaining injera and serve alongside for scooping.
