The viscosity of the broth is one of Taiwan's street food mysteries for first-time visitors. The soup appears to be soup — it is served in a bowl, it contains liquid — but when the spoon moves through it, the broth resists and flows slowly, like a thin gravy or a light starch gel. This is not an accident or a soup-making failure; it is the entire point. Sweet potato starch (or a combination of sweet potato starch and tapioca starch) dissolved in water and added to the simmering broth creates a gentle gelatinous texture that coats the noodles and the oysters and makes the broth cling instead of running off. The effect is warming, comforting, and filling in a way that a clear broth is not.

The *mī-suàn* noodles (麵線, *mǐ xiàn* in Mandarin) are thin wheat noodles, reddish-brown (colored during manufacturing by the alkaline process), and they absorb liquid quickly — in the starchy broth, they soften into a slightly silky, yielding texture that is different from a standard cooked noodle. The noodles are pre-cooked briefly before being added to the individual serving bowl; they finish cooking in the hot broth.

The two main versions sold at night market stalls are the oyster version (*ô-á* = oyster) and the intestine version (*da cháng* = braised pork large intestine, 大腸) — or a combination of both (*dà cháng ô-á*, 大腸蚵仔). The intestine version uses pork intestines braised until tender with soy sauce, five-spice, and aromatics; the intestine pieces float in the starchy broth alongside the noodles.

## The Starchy Broth: Construction

**The base:** Chicken broth, light pork broth, or kombu dashi provides the savory foundation. The broth should be light and clear before the starch is added — the starch will make it opaque and thick.

**Seasoning:** Soy sauce (color and salt); a small amount of sugar; oyster sauce for depth; white pepper; garlic (lightly fried).

**The starch slurry:** Sweet potato starch dissolved in cold water (approximately 3–4 tablespoons starch per 600ml broth) — this is the thickener that creates the characteristic viscosity. Add the slurry to the simmering broth slowly, stirring constantly, until the desired thickness is reached. The broth should coat the back of a spoon but be pourable.

**The oysters:** Fresh small oysters (shucked) added to the broth just before serving — they cook in 30–60 seconds in the hot starchy broth. Overcooked oysters become rubbery.

## The Garnishes (All Traditional)

- **Fresh cilantro:** A generous handful; the herb's brightness cuts through the starchy richness
- **Black vinegar** (Chinkiang or Taiwanese rice vinegar) — drizzled over at the table; adds acid that lifts the thick broth
- **Minced garlic:** Raw garlic, very finely minced; distributed over the surface
- **Chili sauce:** A squeeze of chili paste or a drizzle of chili oil

The black vinegar and garlic applied at the table are not optional additions — they are part of the dish's flavor balance; the thick broth without the acid garnish is one-dimensional.

## The Complete Recipe

**Serves:** 4 | **Time:** 45 minutes

### Broth
- 1 liter chicken broth or light pork broth
- 3 garlic cloves, minced
- 2 tablespoons neutral oil
- 3 tablespoons soy sauce
- 1 tablespoon oyster sauce
- 1 teaspoon sugar
- ½ teaspoon white pepper
- 4 tablespoons sweet potato starch dissolved in 120ml cold water (the slurry)

### Noodles and Protein
- 200g *mī-suàn* (thin wheat vermicelli, red-brown variety) — available at Taiwanese/Chinese grocery stores; or thin wheat noodles as substitute
- 200g fresh small oysters, shucked and rinsed gently
- (Optional: 200g braised pork intestines, sliced — braised separately with soy sauce, five-spice, ginger until tender)

### At-the-Table Garnishes
- Fresh cilantro, roughly chopped
- 4 tablespoons black vinegar (Chinkiang or similar)
- 3 tablespoons minced raw garlic
- Chili paste or chili oil

### Method

**1. Cook noodles:** Boil mī-suàn in a large pot of water for 1 minute (they cook very quickly); drain; rinse briefly with cold water; set aside in individual serving bowls.

**2. Build broth:** Heat oil in a pot over medium; fry minced garlic until fragrant (1 minute). Add chicken broth; bring to a boil. Add soy sauce, oyster sauce, sugar, and white pepper; taste and adjust.

**3. Thicken:** Reduce heat to a steady simmer. Stir the starch slurry well (it settles); add to the simmering broth slowly, stirring constantly. The broth will thicken within 30–60 seconds. Add more slurry for a thicker consistency; add more broth for thinner.

**4. Add oysters:** Slip oysters into the thickened broth; cook 30–45 seconds until just set (they will turn from translucent to opaque). Remove from heat immediately.

**5. Serve:** Ladle the hot thickened broth and oysters generously over the noodles in each bowl. Top with cilantro.

**Bring to the table immediately** with black vinegar, garlic, and chili sauce in separate small dishes for each diner to add to taste.
