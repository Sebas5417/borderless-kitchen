Seollal (설날), the Korean Lunar New Year, is one of Korea's two biggest holidays, along with Chuseok in the autumn. It falls on the first day of the lunar calendar, usually in late January or February; in 2027 it lands on **February 6**. The public holiday runs three days, the day before, the day itself and the day after, and most of the country travels home to family.

The morning has its rituals. Many families hold *charye*, a memorial rite for ancestors with a table of prepared food. Then the younger generation performs *sebae*, a deep bow to their elders, who answer with good wishes for the year and, for children, an envelope of money (*sebaetdon*). And everyone eats tteokguk.

## Tteokguk: The Soup That Makes You a Year Older

*Tteokguk* (떡국) is a clear soup of sliced rice cakes, and it's the one dish nearly every Seollal table shares. By tradition, you don't turn a year older on your birthday but by eating your New Year bowl of tteokguk. (South Korea moved to international age counting for legal purposes in 2023, but the saying stays.)

The rice cakes are *garaetteok*, long white cylinders, cut on the diagonal into thin ovals. The white is said to stand for a clean start, and the oval slices are often compared to old coins, for prosperity.

**How it's made:**
1. **The broth.** Simmer beef, often brisket, in water until tender and the broth is rich. Season with soup soy sauce (*guk-ganjang*) and salt, and shred the beef. A quicker version uses a dried anchovy and kelp stock instead; how the two broths compare is in [dashi vs Korean anchovy broth](/journal/dashi-vs-korean-anchovy-broth).
2. **The rice cakes.** Soak the sliced rice cakes in cold water while the broth cooks, especially if they were frozen, then simmer them in the broth until soft and floating, just a few minutes. Overcooked tteok goes mushy.
3. **The garnish.** Pour beaten egg into the simmering soup in a thin stream, or fry it into a thin sheet and cut it into strips (*jidan*). Finish with the shredded beef, sliced scallion, and strips of toasted seaweed (*gim*).

In parts of the country, especially the north, families add **mandu** (dumplings) to make *tteok-manduguk*.

**A safety note:** rice cakes are dense and chewy. Cut them thin, give small children small pieces, and eat slowly.

## The Rest of the Table

A Seollal spread is a feast, and like any holiday feast it leans on dishes that can be made ahead or served at room temperature.

### Jeon (pan-fried dishes)

Jeon are ingredients coated in flour and egg and pan-fried: thin slices of fish (*saengseon-jeon*), zucchini rounds (*hobak-jeon*), patties of seasoned ground meat and tofu (*donggeurang-ttaeng*), and more. Families often fry them together the day before, in big batches, and the smell of jeon frying is part of the holiday. More types, with technique, are in our [guide to Korean jeon](/journal/korean-jeon-guide-all-types), and [pajeon](/recipes/korean-pajeon-scallion-pancake-recipe) is the easiest place to start.

### Galbi-jjim (braised short ribs)

Beef short ribs braised slowly in a sauce of soy sauce, grated pear, garlic and sesame oil, with chestnuts, jujubes, carrot and radish, until glossy and tender. It's the special-occasion main course, and it reheats well, so it can be made a day ahead. More in our guide to [Korean jjim](/journal/korean-jjim-steamed-braised-dishes); for a grilled alternative, see [galbi](/recipes/korean-galbi-grilled-short-ribs).

### Japchae (glass noodles)

Sweet potato glass noodles stir-fried with beef, spinach, carrot, onion and mushrooms, dressed in soy sauce and sesame oil. It's a celebration dish at every Korean holiday, and it's good at room temperature. Make the [japchae recipe](/recipes/korean-japchae-recipe), and read [its history from the royal court](/journal/japchae-history-culture-royal-court-guide).

### Namul (seasoned vegetables)

A trio of namul is classic: something green, something white, something brown, such as spinach, bean sprouts and fernbrake (*gosari*). Each is blanched and seasoned with sesame oil, garlic and salt or soy sauce. Make [sigeumchi namul](/recipes/korean-sigeumchi-namul-spinach) and [kongnamul](/recipes/korean-kongnamul-bean-sprout-namul).

### Something sweet

*Sikhye*, a cold, sweet rice drink, and *sujeonggwa*, a cinnamon and ginger punch served with pine nuts, are the classic drinks after the meal. *Yakgwa*, honey-soaked fried cookies, and other traditional sweets are in our [hangwa guide](/journal/korean-traditional-sweets-hangwa-guide).

## A Make-Ahead Plan

- **Two days before:** make the beef broth for the tteokguk and chill it. The fat sets on top and lifts off easily. Shop for rice cakes, and soak dried fernbrake overnight if using (it's boiled before seasoning).
- **The day before:** braise the galbi-jjim. Make the namul. Fry the jeon (they reheat in a dry pan).
- **The morning of:** make the japchae. Reheat the galbi-jjim. Bring the broth to a simmer, add the rice cakes, garnish and serve the tteokguk first.
