Korean food is spicy but not only spicy. It's fermented but not intimidatingly so. It's built on a pantry of about 12 ingredients that, once you have them, lets you cook most of what you'll encounter in Korean cooking.

This guide is for people who want to start cooking Korean food at home but feel overwhelmed by the unfamiliarity of the ingredients and techniques. The goal is to make the starting point clear.

## The Essential Korean Pantry

These are the ingredients that appear in Korean cooking constantly. Acquire these before you start.

**The chili products:**
- **Gochugaru (고춧가루):** Korean red chili flakes, coarsely ground. Used in kimchi, stews, banchan. Medium heat, sweet and slightly smoky. This is the most foundational Korean ingredient. Do not substitute cayenne.
- **Gochujang (고추장):** Fermented chili paste. Thick, savory-sweet-spicy. Used in tteokbokki, dipping sauces, marinades, stews. Comes in a red tub with a green lid at Korean grocery stores.

**The fermented soybean products:**
- **Doenjang (된장):** Fermented soybean paste, similar to but different from miso. Earthier and more pungent. Used in doenjang jjigae, ssamjang, and marinades.
- **Soy sauce (간장):** Korean or Japanese brand, both work. Kikkoman is fine.

**The finishing fats and acids:**
- **Sesame oil (참기름):** Toasted sesame oil for finishing. Used as a drizzle, not a cooking oil. Do not cook with it.
- **Rice vinegar:** For dipping sauces, pickles, some marinades.

**The aromatics:**
- **Garlic:** Used in almost every Korean dish, often in substantial quantities.
- **Ginger:** Less central than in Japanese cooking, but important in marinades and braised dishes.
- **Scallions:** Both garnish and ingredient throughout Korean cooking.

**The starches:**
- **Short-grain white rice:** The constant companion. Korean rice is the same short-grain variety as Japanese rice.
- **Sesame seeds:** Toasted, for garnishing and finishing.

**Where to buy:** Korean grocery stores (H Mart, Assi, Lotte, local Korean markets) have everything. If you don't have a Korean grocery nearby, all of these are available on Amazon.

## The Five Dishes to Learn First

These cover the range of Korean cooking — a soup, a stew, a vegetable dish, a rice dish, and a street food — and teach you the foundational techniques.

**1. Doenjang jjigae (된장찌개):** The reference stew. Doenjang paste in anchovy broth with zucchini, tofu, and mushrooms. Once you can make this, you understand how Korean stews are built. 20-minute weeknight dish.

**2. Kimchi fried rice:** Uses old kimchi (essential pantry item), leftover rice, and whatever you have. The bloomed gochujang and kimchi caramelization technique transfers to dozens of other dishes.

**3. Japchae:** Glass noodle stir-fry that teaches the Korean technique of cooking every ingredient separately before combining. More complex but teaches the organized prep approach Korean cooking requires.

**4. Bibimbap:** The rice bowl. Teaches the namul technique (seasoning vegetables with sesame oil and garlic), which is the foundation of Korean vegetable cooking.

**5. Tteokbokki:** The street food. Fast, satisfying, teaches you the gochujang sauce reduction technique that appears across Korean cooking.

## Eating at a Korean Restaurant — First Timer's Guide

**The automatic banchan:** When you sit down, small dishes arrive without ordering. These are included. Eat them. They're designed to accompany your meal, not as separate dishes. Refills are usually free — ask.

**The standard order for a group:**
- 1-2 jjigae (stew) per person, or one shared larger pot
- 1 rice dish
- Or, if going to a BBQ restaurant: order by meat type

**What to order first:**
- Sundubu jjigae (spicy soft tofu stew) — accessible, flavorful, not too unfamiliar
- Bibimbap — the most visual dish; a complete meal
- Doenjang jjigae — milder, deeply savory
- Japchae — glass noodles; non-spicy, universally appealing
- Samgyeopsal (grilled pork belly) — if you go to a BBQ restaurant

**What to avoid ordering until you're familiar:**
- Naengmyeon (cold buckwheat noodles) — the cold temperature and specific flavor are an acquired taste
- Foods described as "raw" that you're not sure about
- Dishes with jeot-gal (fermented seafood) if you're sensitive to strong fermented flavors

## How Korean Meals Are Structured

Korean meals are not Western in structure. There's no strict "courses."

A typical home Korean meal: rice (individual bowls), one main protein dish (jjigae or a grilled/braised dish), and 3-5 banchan (small side dishes) set communally. Everyone eats from the communal banchan. The meal ends when the rice is finished.

At restaurants: food is ordered all at once and arrives together (not in sequence). Banchan comes before everything.

The meal is complete when you've had rice. A bowl of excellent sundubu jjigae eaten without rice is not a complete Korean meal — it's a preview. This is a cultural norm, not a rule, but it explains why Korean menus always include rice dishes.

---

The best way to start Korean cooking is to make doenjang jjigae this week with a bowl of rice and a side of kimchi. That 20-minute combination is representative of what Korean home cooking actually is: deeply flavorful, quick to prepare once the pantry is established, and satisfying in a way that keeps you coming back.
