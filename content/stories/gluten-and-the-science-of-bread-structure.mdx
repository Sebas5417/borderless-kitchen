Wheat flour contains two proteins — glutenin and gliadin — that are present in their native, separate forms in dry flour. When water is added, these proteins hydrate and begin to interact. As the dough is mixed or kneaded, mechanical energy causes glutenin and gliadin to bond with each other, forming the viscoelastic network called gluten.

Gluten has two essential properties that make bread possible:
- **Elasticity** (from glutenin): the network wants to spring back to its original shape when stretched. This gives dough its resistance and helps it hold structure.
- **Extensibility** (from gliadin): the network can be stretched without tearing. This allows the dough to expand as CO₂ bubbles produced by yeast push against it.

The ratio of elasticity to extensibility determines whether a dough is suitable for a given bread. A dough that is too elastic resists expansion and produces dense bread. A dough that is too extensible has no structure and collapses.

## Flour Types and Protein Content

The protein content of flour determines how much gluten can develop. Higher protein = stronger, more elastic network.

| Flour Type | Protein Content | Best Use |
|-----------|----------------|----------|
| Cake flour | 6–8% | Tender cakes, biscuits — minimal gluten desired |
| Pastry flour | 8–9% | Pie crusts, delicate pastries |
| All-purpose flour | 10–12% | Versatile: most baked goods |
| Bread flour | 12–14% | Yeast breads — strong gluten network needed |
| Whole wheat flour | 13–14% | Higher protein, but bran cuts gluten strands |
| High-gluten flour | 14–15% | Bagels, pizza — chewy texture required |
| Durum/semolina | 12–13% | Pasta — very strong, low extensibility |

**Whole wheat note:** whole wheat flour has high protein but also contains bran particles. Bran has sharp edges that physically cut the gluten strands as they form, producing a weaker network than its protein content would predict. This is why 100% whole wheat bread is denser — the bran interrupts the gluten structure.

## Hydration and Its Effect on Gluten

Hydration percentage (the ratio of water to flour by weight) is the most important variable in bread making after flour protein content.

**Low hydration (55–65%):** stiff dough. Gluten develops quickly with mechanical energy. Less extensible but easier to shape. Used for bagels, traditional French bread (lower hydration for longer shelf life), and pasta.

**Medium hydration (65–75%):** the workable range for most home bread. Moderate stickiness. Good balance of strength and extensibility.

**High hydration (75–85%+):** wet, slack dough. The high water content allows the gluten strands to move more freely — this produces more extensibility and ultimately more open crumb (the large irregular holes in artisan sourdough). These doughs cannot be traditionally kneaded and are developed instead through stretch-and-fold techniques. Difficult to shape but produces spectacular crumb when handled correctly.

The relationship: water acts as a lubricant in the gluten network. More water = more mobility of protein strands = more extensibility and potentially more open crumb. But too much water produces a dough that lacks the structure to hold its shape.

## Gluten Development: Kneading vs. Time

Gluten develops through two mechanisms:

**Mechanical development (kneading):** physically aligning and stretching the gluten strands. This is the traditional method. As gluten forms, the dough transitions from shaggy and rough to smooth and elastic. The windowpane test: stretch a small piece of dough thin enough to see light through it without tearing. If it tears before reaching translucency, gluten is not fully developed.

**Time (autolyse and long fermentation):** when flour and water are mixed and allowed to rest (even briefly), gluten forms without any mechanical intervention. The proteins hydrate and bond with each other through the action of water alone. Extended fermentation (overnight cold proofing) develops gluten significantly — this is why no-knead bread works. The 12–18 hour rest replaces kneading.

**Stretch and fold:** a technique used with high-hydration doughs. Instead of kneading, the dough is stretched upward and folded over itself every 30–45 minutes during the first 2–3 hours of fermentation. Each fold reorganizes and strengthens the gluten strands without the vigorous energy of kneading. Four rounds of stretch and fold over 2 hours produces equivalent gluten development to a 10-minute knead.

## What Inhibits Gluten

Several ingredients and conditions weaken or inhibit gluten formation:

**Fat:** fat coats the protein strands and prevents them from bonding with each other. This is why enriched doughs (brioche, challah, croissants) made with butter are tender rather than chewy — the fat literally interrupts gluten formation. Adding fat too early in mixing prevents adequate gluten development; most enriched bread recipes add fat after the initial gluten network is established.

**Sugar:** sugar competes with proteins for water, reducing the amount of hydration available for gluten formation. High-sugar doughs are tender and cake-like rather than chewy.

**Acid:** moderate acid (from sourdough fermentation) has a complex relationship with gluten. At appropriate levels, it strengthens certain gluten bonds (this is why sourdough has excellent structure despite long fermentation). At excessive levels, acid degrades the gluten network.

**Salt:** salt strengthens gluten by tightening the network and making it more stable. This is why bread baked without salt is often flat — the gluten is weaker. Salt also slows fermentation, giving the dough more time to develop structure.

**Over-fermentation:** if yeast is allowed to produce too much CO₂ over too long a period, the acids produced eventually degrade the gluten network. This is why over-fermented bread collapses — the gluten can no longer hold the gas.

## Fermentation and Gas Trapping

Yeast produces CO₂ through fermentation — the conversion of sugars to carbon dioxide and ethanol. The gluten network traps these CO₂ bubbles and stretches to accommodate them. As the bubble expands, the gluten stretches (extensibility); when expansion stops, the gluten holds the shape (elasticity).

The size and distribution of holes in the crumb is determined by:
- **Hydration:** higher hydration = larger, more irregular holes (more extensibility allows larger expansion before the network tears)
- **Gluten strength:** stronger gluten traps more gas before holes coalesce or collapse
- **Fermentation speed:** fast fermentation (more yeast, higher temperature) produces rapid CO₂ production but less complex flavor; slow fermentation produces better flavor and more even crumb

## The Crust: Starch Gelatinization and Maillard

The crust forms through two processes distinct from the crumb:

**Steam in the oven:** steam introduced in the first 15–20 minutes of baking keeps the surface of the dough pliable, allowing the loaf to expand fully before the crust sets. Without steam, the surface dries too quickly, crust forms prematurely, and the bread cannot expand. Scoring (cutting the surface before baking) controls where expansion happens — the cuts open rather than random cracking along the weakest points.

**Maillard and caramelization:** after steam is removed, the oven temperature dries the surface and triggers Maillard reactions between the amino acids and sugars in the dough. The hundreds of flavor compounds produced are what distinguish bread crust flavor from crumb flavor. A properly baked crust should be deeply colored — pale crust is under-baked and will not have the same flavor complexity.

---

**The principle**: bread is gluten plus fermentation. Everything else — hydration, flour type, salt, fat, sugar, time — modifies those two variables. Understanding what each modification does to the gluten network and to fermentation is understanding bread.
