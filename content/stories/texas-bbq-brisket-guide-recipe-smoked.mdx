Aaron Franklin's Austin restaurant Franklin Barbecue has a line that forms at 7 AM for an 11 AM opening. By 1–2 PM, they're often sold out of brisket. People travel to Austin specifically to eat there. This is the degree to which Central Texas brisket has become a pilgrimage food.

The secret, as Franklin himself has explained in his book, is not particularly secret: quality beef, proper seasoning, good wood, controlled temperature, and time. The craft is in doing each of these things correctly and consistently.

## The Brisket

Beef brisket comes from the chest muscle of the cow — one of the most worked muscles, full of connective tissue (collagen) that is tough unless cooked slowly at low temperature, at which point it converts to gelatin and produces the unctuous, melting texture that makes good brisket exceptional.

A whole packer brisket includes two muscles:
- **The flat:** The larger, leaner, flatter muscle. More uniform thickness, less fat. Used for slicing.
- **The point:** The thicker, fattier end (deckel/point). More marbling, richer flavor. Often used for burnt ends.

**Grade:** Select grade brisket is too lean for long smoking and dries out. Choice is the minimum; Prime is what the best Texas BBQ uses. The fat content (intramuscular fat) is what keeps the meat moist over 12+ hours of cooking.

**Size:** A full packer brisket is typically 6–8 kg. This is the size for proper results; smaller pieces don't have the mass to maintain moisture through the full cook.

## The Seasoning

Central Texas seasoning is simple to an extreme: salt and black pepper. That's it. No sauce, no marinade, no rub with multiple spices. The Central Texas philosophy is that the beef and smoke should carry the flavor.

The ratio varies slightly by cook but is typically 50/50 or 60/40 coarse ground black pepper to kosher salt by volume. Applied generously, coating all surfaces.

## The Wood

Post oak is the Central Texas wood — available regionally and preferred for its clean, moderate smoke and long burn time. Oak produces a smoke that is present without being overwhelming, which is what allows the beef flavor to come through.

Mesquite burns hot and produces intense smoke — good for shorter cooks but overwhelming for 12-hour brisket.

## The Temperature and Time

Low and slow: the target pit temperature is 225–250°F (107–121°C). At this temperature:
- Collagen converts to gelatin (requires sustained heat above 160°F/71°C internal for several hours)
- The crust (bark) develops from the Maillard reaction and caramelization of the rub
- The fat renders slowly, basting the meat from the inside

The brisket is done not at a target temperature but at a target texture: a probe (thermometer or skewer) inserted into the thickest part should slide in with no resistance, "like room-temperature butter." This typically happens around 200–205°F (93–96°C) internal temperature.

The "stall": at around 150–165°F (65–74°C), the brisket's internal temperature plateaus for several hours — sometimes 4+ hours. This is evaporative cooling (moisture evaporating from the surface cools the meat as fast as the smoker heats it). Some cooks wrap the brisket in butcher paper (the "Texas crutch") at this point to push through the stall; others wait it out unwrapped for more bark development.

## The Rest

After pulling from the smoker, the brisket rests — wrapped in butcher paper, inside a cooler (not plugged in) — for at least 1 hour, ideally 2–4 hours. During this time, the carry-over cooking continues briefly, and the juices redistribute. Slicing immediately after pulling produces dry brisket even if the cook was perfect.

**Recipe: Central Texas Smoked Brisket (1 whole packer)**

**Equipment needed:** Offset smoker (preferred) or kettle grill with indirect setup or Kamado grill

- 1 whole packer brisket (5–7 kg), USDA Choice or Prime
- Kosher salt
- Coarse ground black pepper (equal parts with salt by volume)
- Post oak or hardwood oak for smoke

**Method:**

1. **Trimming:** The day before, trim excess hard fat (white, waxy fat) to leave approximately 6mm fat cap on the flat. Remove large chunks of hard fat from the point-flat seam. Don't over-trim — some fat is essential.

2. **Seasoning:** Mix salt and pepper. Season all surfaces generously — coat completely, especially the flat which has less fat. Refrigerate uncovered overnight (improves bark development).

3. **Fire:** Set up smoker for indirect heat at 225–250°F (107–121°C). Add 2–3 chunks of post oak. Maintain temperature throughout the cook.

4. **Placing:** Fat side up on the smoker (fat baste the meat from above as it renders). Place the thicker point end toward the heat source.

5. **Smoking:** Cook 12–16 hours total, maintaining temperature. Add wood as needed (you want thin blue smoke, not heavy white smoke). Check temperature every 2–3 hours.

6. **The stall:** At 150–165°F, the temperature will plateau. Either wait it out (for more bark), or wrap tightly in butcher paper (for faster completion).

7. **Done:** When a probe slides through the thickest part with no resistance, typically 200–205°F internal.

8. **Rest:** Wrap in butcher paper; place in an empty cooler. Rest minimum 1 hour, up to 4 hours.

9. **Slicing:** Separate the point from the flat. Slice the flat across the grain, 6mm thick. Slice the point against the grain for burnt ends or serve chunked.

**Serve on butcher paper with:** White bread, pickles, sliced white onion. Sauce is optional in Texas and traditionally offered on the side.
