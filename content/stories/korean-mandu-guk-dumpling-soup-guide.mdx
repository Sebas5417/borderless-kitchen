Korean New Year (*Seollal*, 설날) is associated in popular imagination with *tteokguk* — the rice cake soup eaten to symbolically gain a year of age and start the new year fresh. But in the northern provinces of Korea (historically including what is now North Korea), and in many Seoul families, the New Year soup is *mandu-guk* — dumplings in clear broth.

The two dishes are also commonly combined into *tteok-mandu-guk* (rice cake and dumpling soup together), particularly in contemporary Korean cooking where regional traditions have merged through urban migration.

---

## What Mandu-Guk Is

Mandu-guk (만두국) is simple in concept: *mandu* (handmade or purchased Korean dumplings) cooked in a clear beef broth (*sagol guk*), finished with egg, green onion, and sesame oil.

The components:
- **Broth:** Beef bone or beef brisket broth — light, golden, clean
- **Mandu:** Korean dumplings, typically boiled (not fried) for this preparation
- **Egg:** Whisked and drizzled in thin threads (not scrambled) — this is *hwangbaek jidan* technique (pouring thin egg threads into hot soup)
- **Garnish:** Green onion, sesame oil, sometimes dried laver (gim/nori) shredded

The flavor is delicate — the broth is the feature, with the dumplings providing body and richness. Unlike the assertive broth of many Korean soups, mandu-guk's broth is intentionally restrained so the dumpling filling flavors develop gradually with each bite.

## The New Year Connection

In Korean culture, the bowl of tteokguk or mandu-guk eaten on Seollal morning represents crossing the threshold of the new year. The symbolism:

**Tteokguk (rice cake soup):** The white, cylindrical tteok (*garaetteok*) is sliced into oval coins — representing coins, prosperity, and the cycle of the year. Eating tteokguk is literally how you "gain a year" in the traditional Korean age system.

**Mandu-guk:** The dumpling's round shape represents fullness and completeness. In northern Korean tradition (which influenced Seoul food culture significantly through migration after the Korean War), mandu-guk holds the role tteokguk does in the south.

**Regional division:**
- Southern Korea (Cholla, Gyeongsang provinces): tteokguk is the New Year tradition
- Northern Korea and Seoul tradition: mandu-guk
- Contemporary Korea: often tteok-mandu-guk (both together)

## The Broth

Mandu-guk broth should be clear and clean, distinctly savory without heaviness.

**Traditional beef broth method:**

*Ingredients (makes 2 liters broth):*
- 500g beef brisket or bone-in beef short rib (*saengbiban*)
- 1 medium onion, halved
- 1 head garlic, halved crosswise
- 2-3 green onion stalks
- 2 bay leaves
- 10 whole peppercorns
- 2 liters cold water

*Method:*
1. Blanch beef in boiling water 3-5 minutes; discard water — this removes impurities and produces a clearer stock
2. Rinse beef under cold water
3. Combine blanched beef with 2L fresh cold water + aromatics
4. Bring to boil, skim any foam carefully
5. Reduce to gentle simmer; cook 1.5-2 hours
6. Strain; season with soy sauce (1-2 tbsp) and salt to taste

The broth should be a pale, clear amber. If it's cloudy, strain through a fine mesh or cheesecloth. Clear broth is the visual ideal for mandu-guk.

**Shortcut:** Anchor brand or similar Korean instant beef broth powder works reasonably well for weeknight mandu-guk. Not identical to homemade but effective. Dissolve in water per package directions, then proceed.

## Mandu for This Soup

Mandu cooked in soup should be:

**Boiled style (mul mandu, 물만두):** Not pan-fried (*gun mandu*). The soft, tender wrapper is essential for soup — pan-fried crust would become soggy and unpleasant.

**Size:** Medium-small — 4-5cm width. Larger mandu can be used but take longer to cook through and can be unwieldy in soup.

**Wrapper:** Traditional Korean-style dumpling wrapper — slightly thicker than Chinese potsticker wrapper, made from wheat flour. Thinner wonton wrappers work but produce a more delicate result; thicker gyoza wrappers also work.

**Filling for mandu-guk:**

Traditional filling (serves 4, approximately 20 mandu):
- 150g ground pork (or beef/pork mix 50/50)
- 150g firm tofu — pressed dry, crumbled
- 100g napa cabbage — salted, squeezed very dry, minced
- 50g glass noodles (*dangmyeon*) — soaked, cooked, minced into 1cm pieces
- 3 stalks green onion, finely minced
- 2 cloves garlic, minced
- 1 tsp soy sauce
- 1 tsp sesame oil
- 1/2 tsp salt
- 1/4 tsp white pepper

Combine all; mix well. The filling should be cohesive; squeeze to test — it should hold together without being too wet. If too wet, add a tablespoon of plain breadcrumbs.

**Critical step:** All vegetables must have moisture thoroughly squeezed out before mixing into filling. Wet filling makes watery, soggy mandu. Squeeze the napa cabbage after salting until almost no liquid comes out; press the tofu between paper towels.

**Pleating:** Standard half-moon fold — place filling in center, fold wrapper over, pinch edges together pressing air out before sealing. For a more decorative pleat, make 5-7 small folds along the sealed edge (*jjabu*, the pleated fold). This holds the filling better and looks better in soup.

## Complete Mandu-Guk Recipe

**Serves 4**

*Ingredients:*
- 2L clear beef broth (recipe above, or prepared)
- 20-24 fresh mandu (see filling above)
- 2 eggs
- 3 stalks green onion, sliced into rounds
- 2 tsp sesame oil
- 1-2 tbsp soy sauce (for seasoning broth)
- Salt to taste
- Optional: dried seaweed (gim), crumbled, for garnish

*Method:*

1. **Season and heat broth:** Bring broth to a gentle simmer. Season with soy sauce and salt — the broth should be pleasantly savory but not too salty (mandu filling adds additional salt).

2. **Cook mandu:** Add mandu to simmering broth in batches — do not overcrowd. Fresh mandu take 5-7 minutes to cook through; they float when done, but give them another minute after floating to ensure the wrapper is fully tender and filling is cooked. Frozen mandu take 7-10 minutes from frozen.

3. **Egg threads:** Beat 2 eggs until smooth. With the broth at a gentle simmer (not boiling), drizzle the egg in a thin stream while stirring slowly. The egg will set in thin threads (*silgyeran*, 실달걀). Do not pour all at once — thin, distributed threads are the goal.

4. **Finish:** Add green onion. Taste for seasoning. Drizzle sesame oil over each bowl when serving.

5. **Serve:** Ladle broth and mandu into bowls (5-6 mandu per serving for a main dish). Add crumbled gim if using. Serve immediately — mandu in broth continue cooking and become overly soft if left too long.

## Tteok-Mandu-Guk (Rice Cake and Dumpling Soup)

For the combined version — add *garaetteok* (cylindrical rice cakes) sliced into 5mm rounds alongside the mandu:

- 200g garaetteok, sliced (fresh; if dried, soak in water 30 min first)
- Add to broth 3-4 minutes before mandu (rice cakes need slightly more time if very thick)
- Proceed as above

This is now the dominant version at Seollal tables and in restaurants — the combination satisfies both the "rice cake = New Year" tradition and the "dumpling = fullness and completeness" tradition simultaneously.

---

## When to Make Mandu-Guk Beyond New Year

Mandu-guk is not strictly a Seollal food — it's eaten throughout the year as an everyday soup, a restaurant menu item, and a comfort food during cold months. The ritual significance is concentrated at Seollal, but the dish itself is too good to limit to one day a year.

It pairs well alongside other Korean dishes as part of a bansang, or stands alone as a complete, satisfying one-bowl meal.
