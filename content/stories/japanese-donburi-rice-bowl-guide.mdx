Donburi (丼) is the Japanese word for a deep rice bowl, and for the meal served in it: hot rice with a topping, eaten as a single dish. It is everyday food in Japan. Chains sell it fast and cheap, home cooks make it from whatever is in the fridge, and short names like gyudon and katsudon come from adding "-don" to the topping.

Most donburi share the same few parts, so once you understand them you can make most bowls on this page. This guide covers the main types, those shared building blocks, how to serve the bowl, and how to build one from leftovers. The full recipes live on their own pages, linked below.

## The Main Types of Donburi

### Gyudon (beef bowl)

Thinly sliced beef and onion simmered in a sweet soy and dashi broth, ladled over rice with some of the liquid. It is the best-known fast-food donburi in Japan. The beef must be cut very thin so it cooks in a minute or two. Our [gyudon recipe](/recipes/japanese-gyudon-beef-rice-bowl) has the quantities, and the [longer gyudon guide](/journal/gyudon-recipe-japanese-beef-rice-bowl) covers slicing beef at home.

### Oyakodon (chicken and egg)

The name means "parent and child" bowl: chicken and egg cooked together. Bite-size pieces of chicken thigh and sliced onion simmer in the sauce, then beaten egg goes in at the end and is taken off the heat while still soft. It is the clearest example of the simmered-egg method described below.

### Katsudon (pork cutlet and egg)

A fried pork cutlet, sliced, simmered briefly with onion in the same kind of sauce, then bound with egg. The cutlet is [tonkatsu](/recipes/japanese-tonkatsu-pork-cutlet), and a fresh one or a leftover one both work. Our [katsudon recipe](/journal/japanese-katsudon-recipe) walks through the egg timing.

### Tendon (tempura bowl)

Tempura, usually shrimp and a few vegetables, set over rice and drizzled with a thick, sweet soy sauce. The batter soaks up some of the sauce, which is the point. The difference between tempura batter and other Japanese frying styles is in our [tempura vs karaage guide](/journal/japanese-tempura-vs-karaage).

### Kaisendon (seafood bowl)

Raw seafood, such as tuna, salmon, salmon roe, scallop or sweet shrimp, arranged over rice. The rice may be plain or seasoned with vinegar like sushi rice. Nothing is simmered here. Buy fish sold for raw eating from a source you trust, and keep it cold until you serve it.

### Unadon (eel bowl)

Grilled freshwater eel glazed with a sweet soy tare, served over rice with extra sauce. Most home cooks buy eel already grilled (often vacuum-packed or frozen), then warm it under the broiler and brush on more sauce.

### Butadon (pork bowl)

Sliced pork, grilled or pan-fried, glazed with a sweet soy sauce and laid over rice. The version from Obihiro in Hokkaido is the best known. A soy, mirin and sake glaze like our [teriyaki sauce](/recipes/japanese-teriyaki-sauce-recipe) gets you close.

### Tamagodon (egg bowl)

Oyakodon without the chicken: onion and egg simmered in sauce. It is cheap, fast, and a good way to practice the egg technique before you try it with a cutlet.

### Chukadon (Chinese-style bowl)

Chukadon is Japanese-Chinese food: stir-fried vegetables with pork or seafood in a glossy sauce thickened with starch, poured over rice. The method is different from the other bowls, but it is served and eaten the same way.

## The Building Blocks

### The sauce: a ratio, not a recipe

The simmered donburi (gyudon, oyakodon, katsudon, tamagodon) use a sauce sometimes called don tsuyu (or warishita, a name borrowed from sukiyaki): dashi, soy sauce and mirin, sometimes with sake and sugar. Cooks tend to remember it as a ratio. One common starting point is roughly 4 parts dashi to 1 part soy to 1 part mirin. Add a little sugar if you want it sweeter, or use less dashi for a stronger sauce. Recipes vary, so treat the ratio as a place to start and adjust to taste.

Thinking in ratios means you can make one bowl or four without looking anything up. Mix the sauce, taste it, and change one part at a time.

The bowls with a glaze instead of a broth (unadon, butadon, tendon) use a thicker tare. It has less or no dashi and more soy, mirin and sugar, and it is reduced until it coats a spoon.

### Dashi

Dashi gives the simmering sauce its savory depth. Kombu and katsuobushi dashi takes about 15 minutes, and our [dashi guide](/journal/japanese-dashi-complete-guide) explains how to make it. Granules such as Hondashi are fine for a weeknight bowl. They are salty, so taste before adding all the soy.

### The simmered egg

This is the technique that defines oyakodon, katsudon and tamagodon. Cook the topping in a small, shallow pan in a thin layer of sauce. Beat the eggs lightly, so streaks of white and yolk stay separate. Pour about two-thirds of the egg over the topping, cover for 20 to 30 seconds, until the edges start to set, then add the rest and take the pan off the heat. The egg should be just set at the edges and soft in the middle. Slide everything onto the rice in one piece.

A dedicated oyakodon pan is small, shallow and has an upright handle, which makes the slide easier. A small frying pan works too.

→ [Shop oyakodon pans on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=oyakodon+pan&tag=borderlesskitchen-20)

**A note on egg safety.** The traditional half-set egg in oyakodon and katsudon is not fully cooked, and undercooked eggs can carry a risk of salmonella. Serious illness is more likely in young children, older adults, pregnant people and anyone with a weakened immune system. If that applies to you or the people you are feeding, cook the egg until it is firm, or use pasteurized eggs.

### The rice

Japanese short-grain rice is best: it clings together and soaks up the sauce. Long-grain rice works, but the grains stay separate and the sauce runs straight through to the bottom. Our [short-grain rice guide](/journal/best-short-grain-rice-for-japanese-and-korean-cooking) explains what to buy, and our [rice cooker guide](/journal/best-japanese-rice-cooker-for-home-cooks) covers the machines that make it reliably. Serve the rice hot, and don't pack it down in the bowl.

## Serving: The Bowl and the Lid

A donburi bowl is deeper and wider than an ordinary rice bowl, so there is room for rice, topping and sauce without spilling. Many come with a lid, and the lid does real work:

- **It keeps the bowl hot.** Rice and topping stay warm between the stove and the table.
- **It finishes the egg.** In oyakodon and katsudon, trapped steam keeps cooking the egg gently for the minute before you lift the lid.
- **It holds in the aroma.** Lifting the lid at the table is part of the meal.

If you don't have lidded bowls, a small plate or saucer on top does the same job.

## How to Build a Donburi From Leftovers

Donburi is one of the best ways to use leftovers, because the sauce and rice do most of the work. The method:

1. **Start with hot rice.** Fresh, or leftover rice reheated with a splash of water and covered so it steams.
2. **Pick a protein.** Leftover roast chicken, a cold tonkatsu or chicken katsu, grilled fish, sliced steak, tofu or even fried tempura.
3. **Simmer it in a little sauce.** Mix dashi, soy and mirin roughly in the ratio above, soften a sliced onion in it, then add the protein just long enough to heat through. Fried foods only need a minute or the coating goes soggy.
4. **Add egg if you like.** Use the simmered-egg method, or set a soft-boiled egg on top.
5. **Finish with something sharp or fresh.** Sliced green onion, pickled ginger, shichimi togarashi, nori or a few leaves of mitsuba.

Some bowls need no cooking at all. A pack of natto and a few condiments over hot rice is a complete breakfast. See our [natto rice bowl](/recipes/japanese-natto-rice-bowl).
