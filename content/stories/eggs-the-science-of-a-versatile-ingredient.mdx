An egg white is 90% water and 10% protein — primarily albumin and other globular proteins in their native, coiled state. An egg yolk is about 50% water, 30% fat, 17% protein, and contains lecithin (a powerful emulsifier) and pigments that give it its color. These two components behave differently under heat and serve different purposes in cooking.

The core principle of egg cookery: protein denaturation and coagulation. When egg proteins are heated, they unfold (denature) and then bond with each other (coagulate), forming a solid network. The temperature at which this happens — and how firmly the network sets — determines the texture of every egg preparation.

---

## Temperature and Egg Protein Coagulation

Egg white proteins begin to denature at around 140°F (60°C) and set firmly by 180°F (82°C). Egg yolk proteins behave differently — they begin to thicken at 149°F (65°C) and set fully around 158°F (70°C). The overlap between these ranges is why a soft-poached egg (whites barely set, yolk still liquid) requires precise temperature control.

| Preparation | Target Temperature | Result |
|------------|-------------------|--------|
| Soft-scrambled | 160–165°F (71–74°C) | Small, creamy curds; not fully set |
| Scrambled (custardy) | 165–170°F (74–77°C) | Larger, soft curds; fully set but moist |
| Poached (soft yolk) | 140–145°F (60–63°C) water | White set, yolk liquid |
| Fried (over easy) | Surface ≥350°F, flip briefly | White set, yolk just warmed |
| Hard-boiled | 180°F+ (82°C) internal | Both fully set |
| Custard (crème brûlée) | 170–175°F (77–79°C) | Barely set, smooth, no curd |

**The green ring problem:** Hard-boiled eggs cooked too long or at too high a temperature develop a gray-green ring around the yolk. This is iron sulfide — the iron in the yolk reacts with the hydrogen sulfide produced when egg white proteins overheat. Prevent it by cooking to exactly 212°F and immediately cooling in ice water.

## Scrambled Eggs: The Technique That Reveals Everything

Scrambled eggs are the most informative cooking exercise for understanding egg protein behavior. The variables are simple; the results vary dramatically with small changes in technique.

**The fat and technique matter:** Cooking scrambled eggs in butter over medium-low heat with constant gentle stirring produces small, creamy curds. Cooking over high heat produces large, rubbery curds.

**Why low and slow produces better scrambled eggs:**
- Lower temperatures mean proteins coagulate gradually, forming smaller, more tender curds
- Constant stirring breaks up forming curds before they overset
- The eggs are removed from heat while still slightly underdone — residual heat completes the cooking (carryover)

**The cream or water question:** Adding a small amount of water or cream to beaten eggs dilutes the protein concentration, producing more tender curds. Cream adds fat, which physically interferes with protein bonding — the fat coats the protein strands and prevents them from bonding as tightly, producing a creamier texture.

**The salt timing question:** Salting beaten eggs 15+ minutes before cooking dissolves some of the protein structure, producing silkier, more cohesive curds. Salt added during cooking or after produces the same scrambled eggs — the timing effect only applies when salt has time to work on the raw protein.

## Poached Eggs: Water Temperature and Freshness

A perfectly poached egg has a fully set white and a liquid yolk. The challenge: egg white proteins set at a range of temperatures, and older eggs have more watery whites that spread in the poaching water.

**Temperature:** Keep the water between 160°F–180°F (71°C–82°C) — a gentle simmer, not a rolling boil. Boiling water tears the white apart before it can set.

**Freshness:** Fresh eggs have tighter whites that hold together in the water. Older eggs spread extensively. Test freshness: fresh eggs sink and sit flat in water; older eggs stand upright or float.

**The vinegar trick:** Adding 1–2 tablespoons of white vinegar to the poaching water speeds the coagulation of the white. Acid lowers the pH of the water, which accelerates protein bonding. The result: the white sets faster before it can spread. The egg should not taste of vinegar if the amount is small and the water volume is large.

**The swirl method:** Creating a gentle vortex in the water before adding the egg causes the white to wrap around the yolk as it spins. The egg poaches in a neater oval shape. Works for one egg at a time.

## Fried Eggs: Contact Heat and the Lacy Edge

A fried egg cooked in a very hot pan with enough oil develops crispy, lacy edges where the thin white makes contact with the oil. The white near the yolk remains softer; the yolk warms but stays liquid (for over-easy). This range of textures within a single egg — crispy edge, set white, liquid yolk — is the goal.

**The temperature split:** The pan must be hot enough (350°F+ / 177°C+) to immediately set the white and create lacy edges, but the ambient heat of the pan should not be so intense that it overcooks the yolk before the white is done. A moderate amount of oil (enough to baste the white) helps regulate this — the oil transfers heat to the top of the white without the egg needing to be flipped.

**Basted eggs:** A variation where oil is spooned over the top of the egg repeatedly as it cooks — the hot oil sets the white from above while the pan heat sets it from below. The result: no flip required, fully set white, intact liquid yolk.

## Egg Whites as Foam: The Physics of Meringue

Egg whites, when whipped, incorporate air and form a foam. The protein molecules unfold and align at the air-water interface, trapping air bubbles and stabilizing them. The more the foam is whipped, the smaller and more numerous the bubbles, and the stiffer the foam.

**Stages of whipping:**
- Soft peaks: foam holds a shape but tips over when the whisk is lifted
- Medium peaks: holds shape with slight droop at tip
- Stiff peaks: holds a sharp, upright peak — the foam is dense and dry

**What stabilizes egg white foam:**
- **Cream of tartar (1/8 teaspoon per egg white):** An acid that stabilizes the foam by lowering pH, which makes the protein network more stable and resistant to overwhipping
- **Sugar (added gradually):** Creates a syrup that reinforces the foam structure; dissolved in the water, it makes the foam more stable and glossy
- **Room temperature:** Cold egg whites take longer to foam; room temperature whites whip faster and reach greater volume

**What destroys egg white foam:**
- Any fat (including yolk) — even a trace amount of yolk prevents foam formation. The fat coats the proteins before they can form the air-trapping network
- Overwhipping — the network eventually collapses and the foam becomes lumpy and weeping (liquid separating from the foam)

## The Egg as Emulsifier

Egg yolk lecithin is the most important emulsifier in cooking. It allows oil and water to coexist in a stable mixture — the principle behind mayonnaise, hollandaise, aioli, and many custard sauces.

One egg yolk contains enough lecithin to emulsify approximately 1 cup (240ml) of oil in mayonnaise — a remarkable ratio that illustrates how potent a small amount of the right emulsifier can be.

## Eggs as Binders in Baking

Whole eggs in baking contribute:
- **Structure:** coagulated egg protein strengthens the crumb, especially in cakes with less gluten
- **Fat and richness:** from the yolk
- **Emulsification:** lecithin helps incorporate fat and liquid uniformly
- **Leavening:** beaten eggs trap air; when heated, that air expands

The ratio of whole eggs to yolks in a recipe changes the texture: more whole eggs = more structure and lift; more yolks = richer, more tender texture with less structural support.

---

The egg is not a single ingredient — it is a system. Understanding which part of that system you are engaging (protein coagulation, foam, emulsification, or enrichment) determines how to treat it and what result to expect.
