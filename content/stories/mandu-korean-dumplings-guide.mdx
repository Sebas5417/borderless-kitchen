*Mandu* (만두) — Korean dumplings — are one of the oldest prepared foods in Korean cuisine. The word *mandu* derives from the Chinese *mantou* (馒头, steamed buns), though the Korean version diverged significantly over centuries into distinct forms that have little resemblance to Chinese mantou.

Mandu appears at Korean New Year (*Seollal*), where *tteok-guk* (rice cake soup) is served with mandu added — the combination called *tteok mandu-guk* is the traditional New Year meal. It's also street food, restaurant food, convenience store food (frozen mandu from brands like CJ Bibigo are among the best-selling Korean frozen foods globally), and a home cooking project.

## History and Origins

The earliest recorded mention of mandu in Korea is from the Goryeo dynasty (918-1392 AD). The style was introduced from Central Asian and Chinese cooking traditions that reached Korea via the Mongol Empire, then adapted significantly over subsequent dynasties.

During the Joseon dynasty, *mandu* became more firmly established in Korean court cuisine — large *daemandu* (large dumplings) were elaborate preparations for royal banquets, while smaller everyday mandu developed in common household cooking.

Korean mandu has been influenced by regional geography: northern Korean mandu (particularly *Pyongyang mandu* from what is now North Korea) tend to be larger and milder, made with kimchi and tofu; southern Korean mandu are often smaller and more varied in filling.

## Types of Mandu by Cooking Method

The same mandu can be prepared differently to produce radically different textures and flavor profiles:

### Jjin Mandu (찐만두) — Steamed

Steamed in a bamboo or metal steamer for 10-12 minutes. The wrapper becomes tender, slightly translucent, and soft. The filling stays moist. This is the most traditional preparation and shows off the quality of the wrapper and filling directly.

**Best for:** Appreciating the filling and wrapper. Clearly the cleanest expression of mandu flavor.

### Gun Mandu (군만두) — Pan-Fried (Potsticker Style)

Pan-fried in a thin layer of oil, then steam-cooked with added water. Produces a distinctive textural contrast: crispy, browned bottom with a soft, steamed top. The pan-fried base forms a golden, slightly crackling crust.

**Method:** Heat 2 tablespoons neutral oil in a flat pan. Add mandu. Fry 2-3 minutes until golden on the bottom. Add water (2-3 tablespoons), cover immediately. Steam 6-8 minutes until water evaporates. Uncover and let bottom re-crisp 1-2 minutes.

**Best for:** Most people's favorite preparation — the texture contrast is compelling.

### Mul Mandu (물만두) — Boiled

Boiled in water or broth until the wrapper becomes soft and the filling is fully cooked (6-8 minutes). Served directly in the cooking liquid or transferred to soup.

**In tteok mandu-guk:** Boil mandu directly in the beef or anchovy broth used for the New Year soup. The mandu's filling seasons the broth; the broth seasons the mandu.

### Twigim Mandu (튀김만두) — Deep-Fried

Fried at 180°C until golden brown and crispy all over. Completely different character from the other preparations — the wrapper becomes cracker-crisp, the filling develops a concentrated, intensely savory quality.

Common as street food (*pojangmacha* tents) and in Korean-Chinese restaurants (*jungguk-jip*).

## Types of Mandu by Shape

**Gogi mandu (고기만두):** Meat mandu — the most common. Half-moon or crescent shaped, pleated along the edge.

**Kimchi mandu (김치만두):** Kimchi filling. Often slightly red-tinted.

**Haemul mandu (해물만두):** Seafood filling.

**Wang mandu (왕만두):** "King mandu" — very large, sometimes the size of a fist. Common as street food.

**Pyeonsu (편수):** Square-shaped mandu, traditionally served in summer in a cold broth. Less common today.

## Standard Mandu Filling

### Ingredients (makes approximately 30 mandu)

**Meat and protein:**
- 200g ground pork (or 150g pork + 50g ground beef)
- 100g firm tofu, pressed dry and crumbled
- 100g mung bean sprouts, blanched 1 minute and squeezed completely dry
- 100g kimchi, squeezed dry and finely chopped (optional — for kimchi mandu)
- 100g cabbage or Korean chives (*buchu*), finely chopped and salted, water squeezed out

**Seasoning:**
- 2 tablespoons soy sauce
- 1 tablespoon sesame oil
- 1 tablespoon minced garlic
- 1 teaspoon ginger, grated
- 1 tablespoon green onion, finely chopped
- 1 egg
- Salt and white pepper

### Filling Technique

The most important step in making good mandu filling is removing moisture.

Every wet ingredient — tofu, kimchi, cabbage, bean sprouts — must be squeezed completely dry before mixing. Excess moisture in the filling:
1. Makes the wrapper soggy and prone to tearing
2. Produces steam inside during cooking that collapses the structure
3. Dilutes the flavors

Mix all filling ingredients until uniform. Test by frying a small patty in a pan and tasting for seasoning.

## Wrapper Options

**Store-bought mandu wrappers (만두피):** Available at Korean grocery stores, usually in round or square shapes. Thin, consistent, the practical choice for home cooking.

**Homemade wrappers:**
- 300g all-purpose flour
- 150ml warm water (adjust as needed)
- Pinch salt

Mix flour and salt. Add water gradually, kneading until a smooth, firm dough forms (firmer than pasta dough — roughly the texture of modeling clay). Rest covered 30 minutes. Roll thin (1-2mm) and cut into circles.

Homemade wrappers have better chew and a slightly more rustic quality; store-bought are more uniform and convenient.

## Pleating and Sealing

Mandu sealing is essential — a poorly sealed mandu opens during cooking, spilling the filling into the water or oil.

**Basic half-moon:** Place filling in the center. Fold wrapper over into a half-moon. Pinch the edges firmly together, pressing out any air pockets. Pleat along the sealed edge by making small folds.

**Crescent with twisted top:** Seal the half-moon, then bring the two pointed ends together and press to form a curved shape. This is the most common Korean mandu shape.

**Testing the seal:** Press along the entire edge and make sure there are no gaps or weak points. Water or oil will find any opening.

## Dipping Sauce

Standard mandu dipping sauce:

- 2 tablespoons soy sauce
- 1 tablespoon rice vinegar
- 1 teaspoon sesame oil
- 1 teaspoon gochugaru (optional)
- Finely sliced green onion

This ratio balances the salty-savory quality of the filling. Vinegar cuts through the fat.

## Mandu vs. Gyoza

| | Mandu | Gyoza |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | Korea (from Chinese influence) | Japan (from Chinese influence) |
| Wrapper | Thicker, slightly chewier | Thinner, more delicate |
| Filling | Pork + tofu + kimchi/cabbage | Pork + garlic + cabbage + ginger (no kimchi) |
| Primary cooking | Steamed or pan-fried | Pan-fried (yaki gyoza) primarily |
| Flavor | Bolder, kimchi and sesame character | Subtler, garlic-forward |
| Size | Medium to large | Small to medium |
| Shape | Half-moon, crescent, various | Crescent, tightly pleated |

Both derive from Chinese *jiaozi* — Korean and Japanese cooks adapted the format to local ingredients, preferences, and cooking methods.

## Where to Find the Best Mandu

**Seoul:** Gwangjang Market has several famous mandu stalls — hot steamed *wangmandu* (king mandu) eaten standing at the market. Myeongdong has street mandu vendors. Mangwon Market is known for handmade mandu.

**Korean restaurants:** Most traditional Korean restaurants serve mandu as an appetizer or side dish. *Mandu-jip* (mandu restaurants) specialize in them.

**Frozen:** CJ Bibigo mandu are genuinely good and available internationally. Bibigo has made Korean-style dumplings accessible in Western markets without significant quality compromise.

---

Mandu is one of the most rewarding Korean dishes to make from scratch — the process of making filling, wrapping, and cooking a batch is hands-on and satisfying, and homemade mandu is noticeably better than frozen versions in texture and filling quality. The technique improves quickly with practice, and the first batch, even imperfect, is usually delicious.
