Every cooking technique is a decision about temperature, and every temperature decision is a decision about which flavor compounds to extract and which to destroy. The problem is that most of us learned cooking temperatures as rules — keep dashi below a boil, don't let garlic brown — without understanding why those rules exist. When you know what's happening at the molecular level, the rules become tools you can adapt rather than constraints you're following blindly.

What follows is a framework built around four temperature zones, each with its own extraction chemistry, its own risks, and direct applications across both Japanese and Italian cooking traditions.

---

## The framework: four temperature zones

Flavor compounds differ in two critical ways: **solubility** (how readily they dissolve into a liquid medium at a given temperature) and **stability** (the temperature at which they begin to degrade, volatilize, or transform into something else).

The result is a sequence of extraction windows — temperature ranges within which specific compounds are available in maximum concentration. Cook cooler and you don't reach the window; cook hotter and you pass through it and into degradation or transformation.

---

## Zone 1: Below 70°C — delicate aromatics and cold-extract compounds

Below 70°C, the chemistry is dominated by dissolution rather than transformation. Compounds that are soluble in cold or warm water are available at this range without the structural disruption that heat introduces.

**Kombu glutamate extraction.** The free glutamic acid in dried kombu is water-soluble at any temperature — it begins dissolving the moment kombu contacts water. The advantage of keeping extraction below 60°C (as in cold-brew kombu dashi or a slow-heated dashi start) is that the heat-labile compounds in kombu — the volatile aromatics that give it an oceanic character and the alginic acid polysaccharides that add sliminess — are not activated. The glutamate yield at cold temperatures approaches that of hot extraction, but the flavor profile is cleaner and more transparent.

**Cold brew catechins.** Green tea steeped in cold water (4–10°C) for 6–12 hours extracts a higher ratio of L-theanine and certain catechins relative to the astringent tannins that hot water pulls aggressively. Cold-brew green tea is noticeably sweeter and less bitter than hot-steeped tea from the same leaves — the temperature dictates the compound ratio, not just the extraction speed.

**Delicate aromatic compounds.** Fresh herbs — basil, tarragon, chervil — contain highly volatile aromatic compounds (terpenes, linalool, estragole) that are lost rapidly above 60°C. This is why finishing dishes with fresh herb additions is done off heat or at the very end of cooking — you're extracting aromatics into the dish at a temperature that preserves them. Heating basil in a sauce above 70°C for more than a few minutes reduces it to a green-colored bitter residue.

## Zone 2: 70–100°C — the main extraction window

The range from 70°C to boiling is where most cooking happens, and it is the broadest extraction zone — most proteins, starches, and moderate-stability aromatics are available here.

**Miso and heat.** Miso is added off-heat or at the very end of Japanese cooking for a compound reason. First, miso's enzymatic activity (protease and amylase enzymes that contribute to its complexity) is permanently destroyed above approximately 72°C — adding miso to a boiling broth kills those enzymes. Second, the volatile aromatic compounds formed during miso fermentation (esters, alcohols, short-chain organic acids) are highly volatile above 80°C and will rapidly escape the pot. Third, miso's delicate sweetness (from residual sugars in white miso in particular) becomes bitter when subjected to prolonged heat. The result: miso should dissolve into a hot but non-boiling liquid immediately before serving. 65–70°C is the ideal temperature.

**Why dashi never boils.** The kombu rule (remove before boiling) and the katsuobushi rule (steep off heat, never boil) both operate in this zone. Kombu heated above 80°C releases alginic acid, an algal polysaccharide that creates a slimy texture and bitter off-notes. Katsuobushi boiled in water releases bitter amino acids and fat-soluble compounds from the fish that don't belong in a clean dashi. Both the glutamate from kombu and the IMP from katsuobushi are fully extracted below boiling temperature — there is no extraction benefit to boiling dashi, only degradation.

**Herb teas and tisanes.** Delicate herbal infusions (chamomile, lemon verbena, fresh mint) extract best between 75–85°C. Boiling water drives off the aromatic compounds before you can steep them. Most herb tea instructions call for "just-boiled" water — this means letting boiling water rest for 30–60 seconds before adding the herbs, which drops the temperature into the optimal range.

**Protein extraction in stocks.** The collagen-to-gelatin conversion that gives long-cooked stocks their body occurs in this zone, but takes time — collagen begins converting around 70°C and the process is slow. A well-made stock held at a sub-boiling simmer (85–90°C) for 4–6 hours extracts more gelatin with less cloudiness than a full-boil stock for the same duration. Full boiling agitates proteins that have coagulated, breaking them into fine particles that cloud the stock. Sub-boiling keeps the liquid clear while allowing the same collagen conversion.

## Zone 3: Above 140°C — Maillard reactions, caramelization, and dry-heat transformation

Liquid water caps cooking temperature at 100°C — water absorbs heat energy as it evaporates and maintains the surface of wet food at or below its boiling point. The flavor compounds in this zone require dry-heat conditions: roasting, pan-searing, grilling, or baking.

**The Maillard reaction.** At approximately 140°C, a complex series of reactions begins between amino acids (from proteins) and reducing sugars. These reactions produce hundreds of new compounds — including melanoidins (the brown pigments responsible for crust color), pyrazines (nutty, roasted flavors), furans (caramel-adjacent), and thiophenes (sulfurous, savory). The specific compounds produced depend on which amino acids and sugars are present, which is why a seared chicken thigh, a grilled onion, and a toasted bread crust all produce Maillard products but taste entirely different.

Maillard reactions accelerate significantly with temperature — they proceed slowly near 140°C and rapidly above 165°C. A pan that is not hot enough will steam the meat's surface moisture away before reaching Maillard temperatures, producing gray boiled-looking meat rather than a brown crust.

**Caramelization.** Different sugars caramelize at different temperatures: fructose at approximately 110°C, glucose and sucrose at approximately 160°C, maltose at approximately 180°C. The caramelization reactions produce furanones, diacetyl (buttery flavors), and hydroxymethylfurfural — compounds that don't exist in raw sugar. A caramelized onion is chemically different from a raw onion that has been sweetened — the sugars have transformed, not just concentrated.

**Garlic: the Italian case study.** Garlic confited in olive oil at low temperature (60–70°C) for 45–60 minutes produces a completely different flavor profile than garlic browned in a hot pan. Low-temperature garlic: the allicin (the pungent, aggressive compound formed when garlic cells are crushed) converts slowly to milder ajoene and other thioethers; the result is sweet, mild, and complex. High-temperature garlic: Maillard reactions between garlic's sugars and amino acids produce nutty, savory, roasted flavors — but the allicin is also destroyed rapidly, and the volatile sulfur compounds that give garlic its characteristic sharpness largely escape. The choice of cooking temperature for garlic is a choice between two entirely different ingredients.

## Zone 4: Above 180°C — char, bitter compounds, and controlled carcinogen formation

Above 180°C, caramelization products begin to degrade into bitter compounds. Above 200°C, pyrolysis begins — the irreversible thermal decomposition of organic compounds into acrid, bitter, and in some cases toxic compounds (heterocyclic amines, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons in grilled and charred meats).

Char is not uniformly bad. The controlled charring of ingredients — the deliberate application of very high heat to specific parts of a dish — produces bitter, smoky compounds that add complexity when present in small amounts and at the surface only. A properly charred yakitori skewer has Maillard products through most of its surface, with a small amount of char at the highest-heat points that contributes smokiness and complexity. Full carbonization — blackened throughout — produces primarily bitter and acrid compounds with no culinary benefit.

**Coffee as a calibration.** Roasted coffee passes through all four zones in sequence. Green coffee beans contain raw aromatic precursors; at 150–165°C (light roast), Maillard products dominate and produce bright, acidic, fruity flavors; at 170–185°C (medium roast), caramelization products balance the Maillard compounds; above 200°C (dark roast), the caramelization products degrade into bitter compounds that dominate the cup. The roast level is entirely a temperature and time decision.

## The unified framework applied

**Italian: low-temp garlic confit vs. high-temp browning.** As described above, these are not the same process or the same outcome. Use garlic confit where you want depth without sharpness — smeared on bruschetta, incorporated into mashed potatoes, as a base for slow-cooked tomato sauce. Use browned garlic where the nutty, Maillard-produced flavor is the point — in aglio e olio (where the flavor of browned garlic in olive oil is the sauce), in quick sautés where garlic is a background note.

**Japanese: the dashi temperature constraint.** Japanese cuisine's most important flavor base — dashi — operates entirely in zones 1 and 2, and specifically at the upper limit of zone 1 and the lower range of zone 2. The precision is not obsessive — it's the only temperature range in which you can extract maximum glutamate and IMP without also extracting the degradation compounds that boiling introduces. The rule "dashi never boils" is zone 1-2 chemistry in practice.

**Why miso is added off-heat.** Operating at 65–70°C preserves enzymatic activity, volatile aromatics, and the delicate sweetness of the miso's residual sugars — all zone 2 and below. Adding miso at a boil destroys all three.

---

The framework is a tool for reading any cooking instruction: when a recipe specifies low and slow, it's targeting zone 1 or 2 extraction while avoiding the zone 3 transformation reactions. When it calls for high heat, it's specifically pursuing those Maillard products. The temperature is never arbitrary — it's always an argument about which compounds you want in the dish.
