Chocolate is arguably the most chemically complex ingredient in everyday cooking. A single bar contains over 600 flavor compounds, more than red wine. It also behaves in ways that confuse home cooks: it seizes with a drop of water, blooms white if stored wrong, and refuses to set properly unless handled at very specific temperatures. None of this is mysterious once you understand what's happening.

---

## What Chocolate Is

Chocolate is made from cacao beans, which are fermented, dried, roasted, and ground into a paste called chocolate liquor (no alcohol — the name refers to liquid). This paste contains two things in roughly equal measure: cocoa solids and cocoa butter.

The ratio and treatment of these two components, plus added sugar and milk solids (in milk chocolate), determines the type of chocolate:

| Type | Cocoa Solids | Cocoa Butter | Notes |
|------|-------------|-------------|-------|
| Dark (70%+) | High | High | Intense, bitter-forward, complex |
| Semisweet | Medium | High | Balance of sweet and bitter |
| Milk | Lower | High | Sweeter, creamier, less complex |
| White | None | High | Technically not chocolate — no cocoa solids |

**For cooking:** use chocolate with a cocoa percentage you'd enjoy eating. Higher percentage = more intensity, less sweetness, and more complicated behavior in recipes.

## Why Chocolate Blooms

Bloom is the white or grey film that appears on chocolate left in fluctuating temperatures. There are two types:

**Fat bloom:** Occurs when cocoa butter crystals melt and resolidify on the surface. Caused by temperature fluctuation (warm → cool repeatedly) or improper storage. The chocolate is safe to eat — it's just cosmetically damaged.

**Sugar bloom:** Occurs when moisture condenses on the surface, dissolves surface sugar, then evaporates, leaving sugar crystals behind. Caused by moving chocolate from cold to warm humid environments without allowing it to equalize temperature first.

Prevention: store chocolate at a stable 65–70°F (18–21°C), away from humidity, and let refrigerated chocolate come to room temperature slowly before opening.

## Why Chocolate Seizes

Chocolate is almost entirely fat (cocoa butter). When you melt it, the fat melts and the cocoa particles are suspended smoothly within it. Add a small amount of water and those cocoa particles immediately clump together — the water creates a sticky paste between them that the fat can't re-integrate. The result is a thick, grainy, unworkable mass. This is seizing.

**The counterintuitive fix:** add more liquid. A tablespoon of water seizes chocolate. Adding enough liquid (at least 1 tablespoon per ounce of chocolate) fully incorporates the water into a ganache or sauce. The problem isn't water — it's a small amount of water.

**What will seize chocolate:** steam from a double boiler (keep the bowl above the water, not touching), a wet spoon, or a wet bowl. Even a few drops will seize a large batch.

## Tempering: Why Chocolate Snaps

Properly tempered chocolate is glossy, snaps cleanly, and melts at body temperature with a smooth, even sensation. Improperly melted and re-cooled chocolate is dull, soft, and may show streaks.

The reason: cocoa butter can crystallize into six different forms. Only one form — called Form V — produces the ideal texture and gloss. Tempering is the process of guiding the chocolate to crystallize into Form V and only Form V.

**The seed method (simplest at home):**
1. Melt two-thirds of your chocolate to 115°F (46°C) for dark, 110°F (43°C) for milk/white — fully liquid, all crystals destroyed.
2. Remove from heat. Add the remaining one-third finely chopped chocolate (the "seed") and stir constantly.
3. The seed contains Form V crystals. As you stir, these crystals propagate through the melted chocolate, encouraging the same crystal structure to form.
4. Continue stirring until the temperature drops to 88–90°F (31–32°C) for dark, 84–86°F (29–30°C) for milk/white.
5. Test: dip a small piece of parchment into the chocolate. At 70°F (21°C) ambient temperature, it should set within 3–5 minutes. If it sets shiny and snaps cleanly, you're tempered. If it's streaky or soft, repeat the process.

**Why bother with tempering?** For dipping, molding, or coating — anywhere the chocolate is the finished product. For ganache, brownies, or any recipe where chocolate is combined with other ingredients, tempering is irrelevant.

## Ganache: The Foundation of Chocolate Confections

Ganache is an emulsion of chocolate and cream. It's the filling in truffles, the glaze on cakes, the base of chocolate sauce, and, when whipped, a frosting. The ratio of chocolate to cream determines the consistency:

| Ratio (chocolate:cream by weight) | Result | Use |
|----------------------------------|--------|-----|
| 2:1 | Firm truffle filling | Rolled truffles, chocolate centers |
| 1:1 | Soft, pourable when warm | Tart filling, glaze, soft truffles |
| 1:2 | Thin sauce | Chocolate sauce, fondue |

**Method:**
1. Chop chocolate finely — fine pieces melt evenly and completely.
2. Heat cream to just below a boil (180°F / 82°C). Remove from heat.
3. Pour hot cream over chocolate. Wait 1 minute without stirring — the heat penetrates the chocolate.
4. Stir from the center outward in small circles, gradually expanding. This builds the emulsion. Do not whisk or beat — that introduces air bubbles.
5. Add a pinch of salt and any flavorings (vanilla, spirits, espresso) once smooth.
6. For a glossy glaze finish, add 1 tablespoon of butter per cup of ganache after it's smooth.

**Ganache split:** if ganache looks broken (greasy, separated), it means the emulsion failed. Add a tablespoon of warm cream and stir from the center — it usually re-emulsifies. Or blend with an immersion blender.

## Melting Chocolate Without Tempering

For baking and cooking where you don't need snap or gloss:

**Double boiler:** Set a heatproof bowl over a saucepan with 1–2 inches of barely simmering water. The bowl must not touch the water. Stir frequently. Remove from heat before fully melted — residual heat finishes the job.

**Microwave:** Most effective for home use. Heat in 30-second bursts at 50% power, stirring between each. Chocolate holds its shape even when melted — it won't look melted until you stir it. Overheating in a microwave burns the outside while the center is still solid.

**Chopping tip:** the smaller you chop the chocolate, the faster and more evenly it melts.

## Chocolate Flavor: What You're Tasting

When you taste good chocolate, the flavor evolves across three phases:

1. **Initial impression (first 5 seconds):** Fat perception, sweetness, and the first aromatic volatiles releasing.
2. **Development (5–30 seconds):** The cocoa solids' flavor compounds develop — fruity, floral, nutty, or bitter notes depending on origin and roast. Chocolate from different regions has genuinely different flavor profiles: West African is typically earthy and nutty; South American tends toward fruit and citrus notes; Southeast Asian can have earthy, herbal qualities.
3. **Finish (30 seconds+):** Tannins, acidity, and lingering aromatics. High-quality chocolate has a long, evolving finish; poor-quality chocolate stops short.

**What ruins chocolate flavor in cooking:**
- Overheating (above 120°F / 49°C for extended periods) scorches the cocoa solids and produces a harsh, acrid note
- Competing flavors that overwhelm (too much vanilla, too much sugar) mask the chocolate's complexity
- Under-salting — salt is as important in chocolate as in savory cooking; it suppresses bitterness and amplifies chocolate flavor

---

Chocolate rewards careful handling and close attention. The techniques that make it misbehave — incorrect temperatures, moisture, overheating — are the same ones that make it extraordinary when done right. Once you've tasted properly tempered chocolate or a ganache made from excellent chocolate and cream, nothing else compares.
