## VIDEO 1: "The 15-Minute Stock That Changed How I Cook"
**Format:** Talking head + hands cooking B-roll
**Duration:** 90 seconds

**SCRIPT:**

[0:00 - Hook]
"There's a stock that takes 15 minutes, has two ingredients, and tastes like it cooked for hours. Japanese cooks have been making it for centuries. Here's why it works."

[0:06 - Setup]
"It's called dashi. You need two things: kombu — dried kelp — and katsuobushi — dried bonito flakes."

[0:12 - The science]
"Here's the reason it tastes so deep: kombu is packed with glutamate — the compound responsible for umami, depth, the flavor that makes you keep eating. Katsuobushi is packed with inosinate — a different umami compound. When these two meet, they don't add. They multiply. Six to eight times more perceived depth than either ingredient alone."

[0:30 - How to make it]
"Add kombu to cold water. Let it sit for 20 minutes. Heat until just before boiling — you'll see small bubbles forming. Pull the kombu out. Add the bonito flakes. Let them steep for three minutes. Strain."

[0:48 - The result]
"What you have is the foundation of Japanese cooking. Pale. Clear. Impossibly deep."

[0:54 - The Italian connection]
"And here's the thing Italian cooks figured out separately: Parmigiano rind in broth does the exact same thing. Glutamate leaching into liquid. Two traditions. Same chemistry."

## VIDEO 3: "The Most Underrated Umami Ingredient in Your Pantry Right Now"
**Format:** Kitchen reveal / talking head
**Duration:** 60 seconds

**SCRIPT:**

[0:00 - Hook]
"I'm going to guess you have this in your pantry right now — and you've been using it way below its potential."

[0:06 - Reveal]
"Tomato paste. When most people add tomato paste, they dump it straight into liquid. That's not wrong. But this is better."

[0:14 - The technique]
"Push everything in your pan to the sides. Put the tomato paste in the center — direct contact with the hot pan. Let it cook. Two, three minutes. Until it goes from bright red to brick. Until it smells almost caramelized."

[0:28 - The science]
"What's happening: you're concentrating glutamate — the umami compound already in the tomatoes — and you're triggering Maillard reactions that add another layer of flavor. The paste goes from 'tomato' to 'depth.'"

[0:40 - The payoff]
"Then add your liquid. The caramelized paste dissolves into the sauce and takes everything else with it."
