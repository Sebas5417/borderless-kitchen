## CAPTION 1 — Ramen alla Carbonara (food photo needed)

Why does Ramen alla Carbonara work?

Not because "fusion." Because both dishes are built on the same logic.

Carbonara: fat (guanciale) + fermented protein (eggs + Pecorino) + starch to bind it.

Ramen: fat (tare) + fermented umami (miso or soy) + noodles to carry it.

The carbonara sauce goes on the noodles. The dashi broth goes around them. The guanciale floats on top.

The result tastes like both dishes got better at the same time.

## CAPTION 2 — Dashi (process shot needed: kombu in water)

The most important cooking lesson I ever learned took 15 minutes and cost $4.

Cold kombu in water. Heat until almost boiling. Add katsuobushi. Steep. Strain.

That's ichiban dashi — the foundation of Japanese cooking.

And the reason it tastes so deep: kombu has 1,608mg of glutamate per 100g. Katsuobushi is packed with inosinate. When these two umami compounds combine, they don't add — they multiply. Six to eight times more perceived umami than either ingredient alone.

This is why a simple miso soup made with real dashi tastes like it cooked for hours.

Save this. Make it this week.

## CAPTION 3 — Miso Cacio e Pepe (food photo needed)

I added white miso to Cacio e Pepe.

Not a lot. Just enough to replace some of the Pecorino. Dissolved in dashi instead of pasta water.

The sauce was more stable. The flavor was more intense. The cheese tasted more like itself.

This is the umami synergy principle: Pecorino is high in glutamate. Dashi katsuobushi is high in inosinate. Combined, they produce 6-8× more perceived umami than either alone.

The result? A Cacio e Pepe that tastes like a better Cacio e Pepe. No gimmick.

## CAPTION 4 — The Pairing Matrix (text graphic / quote card)

White miso and Parmigiano-Reggiano are the same ingredient.

Not the same flavor. The same structural function.

Both are fermented. Both are among the highest natural sources of glutamate on earth. Both emulsify beautifully into fat. Both provide salt + depth + the background note that makes everything else taste more like itself.

This is why you can use them interchangeably — or both at once, for twice the depth.

Italian and Japanese cooking aren't separate traditions. They're the same logic, expressed through different ingredients.

## CAPTION 6 — Umami science (no image needed — carousel or quote card)

The five tastes:
— Sweet (sucrose, fructose)
— Salty (sodium ions)
— Sour (hydrogen ions / acids)
— Bitter (alkaloids)
— Umami (glutamate, inosinate, guanylate)

Umami was identified in 1908 by Japanese chemist Kikunae Ikeda. It wasn't recognized by Western food science until 2002.

The reason it took 94 years: we already knew umami foods. We just called them "rich," "savory," "complex," "satisfying." We had no word for the specific receptor that causes that experience.

Now we do. And knowing the mechanism makes you a better cook.
