Nori — the dried seaweed used to wrap sushi — is not usually considered a pasta ingredient. It should be.

When you toast nori sheets in a dry pan until crisp, then add butter and let the heat melt the nori into the fat, two things happen. The nori's structure breaks down and its flavor compounds — glutamate, guanylate, the mineral-oceanic aromatics — dissolve into the butter. And the butter browns around the nori, adding its own nuttiness to the deepening green-black color of the fat.

The resulting nori compound butter is intensely savory in a direction that doesn't taste Japanese, doesn't taste Italian, and doesn't taste like seaweed in the way that concerns people. It tastes oceanic and umami-rich in the way that brown butter pasta tastes nutty and rich — like a brown butter that lived near the sea.

Dress spaghetti with this butter, add pasta water to emulsify, and finish with Parmigiano. That's the whole dish.

---

## What you'll need

- 160g (5.5 oz) spaghetti or linguine
- 2 tablespoons salt (for pasta water)
- 3 full sheets nori (standard sushi nori size)
- 4 tablespoons (60g) unsalted butter
- 1 clove garlic, very thinly sliced
- 20g (¾ oz) Parmigiano-Reggiano, freshly grated
- 1 teaspoon toasted sesame seeds
- Flaky salt and black pepper to serve
- ½ teaspoon white miso (optional — adds additional umami depth)

---

## Method

### Step 1: Make the nori butter

Tear the nori sheets into rough pieces. Heat a wide skillet over medium heat until hot. Add the nori pieces to the dry (ungreased) pan. Toast for 30-60 seconds until the nori changes from dark green to bright green and becomes fragrant and slightly crisp. Remove from heat briefly.

Add the butter to the pan (with the nori still in it). The butter will foam. Let it foam and then continue cooking over medium heat, swirling occasionally, until the butter turns golden-brown and smells nutty — about 2-3 minutes. The nori will begin to dissolve into the butter as it cooks; this is correct.

Add the sliced garlic. Cook for 30 seconds until just softened. Remove from heat.

If using white miso: dissolve it in 1 tablespoon of water and add to the butter sauce off-heat. Stir to incorporate.

The nori butter can be made ahead and refrigerated for up to 3 days. Reheat gently before using.

### Step 2: Cook the pasta

Cook spaghetti in well-salted boiling water to al dente — 1-2 minutes less than the package suggests.

Before draining, reserve a mug (about 250ml / 1 cup) of the starchy pasta water.

### Step 3: Combine

Return the nori butter pan to medium heat. Add the drained pasta directly to the pan. Pour in 3-4 tablespoons of pasta water. Toss aggressively — the pasta water starch will help the butter emulsify into a glossy sauce that clings to the noodles.

If the sauce looks greasy or broken: add a small splash more pasta water and toss. The starch in the water re-emulsifies the fat.

Off heat, add Parmigiano. Toss until melted. The pasta should look glossy and evenly coated.

### Step 4: Serve

Plate immediately. The nori butter pasta doesn't hold well — serve as soon as it's ready.

Finish with: toasted sesame seeds, a small extra sprinkle of Parmigiano, flaky salt, and black pepper.

Optional garnish: torn fresh nori on top (not cooked — the raw nori's texture contrasts with the cooked nori in the sauce). Or a soft-boiled egg halved and placed on top.

## Why nori works in pasta

Nori contains both glutamate and guanylate — two different umami compounds. When dissolved into butter, both compounds become fat-soluble and coat every strand of pasta as the sauce emulsifies.

The Parmigiano added at the finish contributes a third umami compound (more glutamate, plus casein proteins from the aged milk). The combination of nori's guanylate + nori's glutamate + Parmigiano's glutamate creates the same triple-umami stacking that makes the Miso Mushroom Tagliatelle and the Dashi Risotto taste disproportionately deep.

Additionally: brown butter's nuttiness (from the Maillard reaction in the milk solids) and nori's oceanic quality are complementary, not competing. The nutty-oceanic combination has parallels in classical Italian cooking — anchovies in butter, browned butter over roe — where fat and sea flavor work together rather than against each other.

## The two-ingredient version

If you want the simplest possible version: nori + butter + pasta water + salt. No Parmigiano, no garlic. Dress the cooked pasta with nori butter and pasta water only. Finish with flaky salt and sesame seeds.

The dish is still excellent. The Parmigiano adds additional umami and richness, but the nori butter on its own is a complete sauce.

This is the test version to confirm whether nori butter is in your repertoire before adding the other elements. If you like what it does to pasta, the miso variation (add 1 teaspoon white miso to the butter) takes it further.

## Variations

**With katsuobushi:** Add a small handful of katsuobushi (dried bonito flakes) on top at serving. The katsuobushi's inosinate adds a fourth umami source to the nori's glutamate and guanylate + Parmigiano's glutamate. This is the maximum-umami version.

**With citrus:** Squeeze ½ a yuzu (or ¼ lemon) over the finished pasta before serving. The acid lifts the richness of the brown butter and makes the dish feel lighter.

**With fresh nori:** If you can get fresh nori (rare in Western markets), wilt it briefly in the butter instead of using dried. The flavor is different — greener, less concentrated — but the result is beautiful.

**Without Parmigiano (dairy-free):** Nori butter + pasta water + nutritional yeast (2 tablespoons). The nutritional yeast's glutamate approximates the Parmigiano's umami contribution while keeping the dish dairy-free (except the butter — substitute with good vegan butter for a fully plant-based version).
