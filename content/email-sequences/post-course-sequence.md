## Email 7 — Day 16
**Subject:** The recipe I make when I have nothing in the house

---

This recipe requires four things: pasta, miso, butter, and Parmigiano.

**Miso Butter Pasta:**

Cook 200g spaghetti. While it cooks, melt 2 tablespoons butter in a pan over medium heat until it starts to foam. Add 1 tablespoon white miso and stir — it will hiss and bubble. Add ¼ cup pasta water and stir until smooth.

Toss in the pasta. Add Parmigiano. More pasta water if needed. Done.

What's happening: the miso brings glutamate, the Parmigiano brings more glutamate, and they synergize. The butter emulsifies everything into a glossy sauce. The total cook time is 12 minutes including the pasta.

This is the simplest expression of what the entire Borderless Kitchen is about: two fermented umami sources doing more together than they would apart.

Try it this week and tell me what you think.

## Email 8 — Day 23
**Subject:** The thing nobody tells you about learning to cook Japanese food

---

The barrier to Japanese cooking isn't technique. It's pantry.

Most Western cooks don't have kombu, katsuobushi, miso, mirin, and sake in the house. When a recipe calls for four of those, you either skip the recipe or make an expensive special trip.

Here's how I built the pantry incrementally:

**Month 1: Just miso and mirin.** You can make miso soup, a simple glaze, and miso salad dressing with these alone. Total cost: $12.

**Month 2: Add soy sauce (Japanese, not Chinese).** Now you have teriyaki. Total cost: $6.

**Month 3: Kombu.** One $8 bag lasts 3-4 months. Add it to beans, broth, or cold water. Instant umami upgrade to anything liquid.

**Month 4: Katsuobushi.** Combine with the kombu and you can now make ichiban dashi. This is when the whole flavor system clicks.

You don't need everything at once. Each ingredient is useful standalone. Build gradually.

If you want a shortcut: the [Borderless Kitchen pantry guide](https://borderlesskitchenseries.com/journal) has Amazon links to exactly what I use.

## Email 10 — Day 44 (ongoing newsletter cadence begins)
**Subject:** The fish that isn't fish

---

This week: katsuobushi.

Katsuobushi is dried bonito — a tuna-like fish that's been fermented, dried, and smoked into a hard block, then shaved into paper-thin flakes. It's one of the most ancient preserved foods in Japanese cuisine.

When you add it to hot liquid, something strange happens. The flakes wave in the steam like they're alive. Then they sink. Then you strain them out, and what's left is pale liquid that smells like smoke and ocean.

That liquid is dashi.

The reason katsuobushi matters is not the smoke or the ocean. It's inosinate — the second umami compound. Combined with glutamate from kombu, inosinate creates synergy. The perceived umami isn't doubled. It's multiplied by 6-8x.

This is the same reason Parmigiano + prosciutto in the same dish tastes disproportionately deep. Parmigiano = glutamate. Prosciutto (cured meat) = inosinate. Two traditions, same chemistry.

One thing to try this week: make a quick ichiban dashi and use it as the base for a cacio e pepe. Instead of pasta water, use dashi. Watch what happens.

Full recipe here: [Dashi Cacio e Pepe](https://borderlesskitchenseries.com/journal)
